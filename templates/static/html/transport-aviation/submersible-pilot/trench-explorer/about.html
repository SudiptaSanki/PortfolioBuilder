The pilot

I am the third person in a two-person rule.

Every crewed dive has a pilot, a co-pilot and a client scientist. My job is to make the other two forget the vehicle is a pressure vessel and get their samples. The way I do that is boring on purpose: checklists, weighed ballast, and a written way back to the surface.

From ROV pilot to crewed vehicles

I started in 2003 flying work-class ROVs off the Mongstad supply base, mostly inspection on pipelines and one very long month on a stuck valve at 340 m. Remote vehicles taught me to plan a dive as a sequence of failures, because when the vehicle stops answering there is no one inside to argue with.

In 2005 I moved to the crewed side as a co-pilot on a two-person observation submersible working the Norwegian fjords, and took command in 2008. Since then the work has been vent chemistry on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, trench-wall survey in the Kermadec, and a growing share of natural-history filming where the vehicle has to hold a rock-steady hover at 30 cm from a fragile chimney.

I hold a DNV class pilot certificate for manned submersibles to 6,500 m, IMCA D 014 supervisor, and an advanced first-aid-at-sea ticket renewed every two years. The vehicle is insured for crewed operation through Lloyd's, and every dive is logged to the minute whether or not anyone pays for the log.

Standing rules

Four things that end a dive

01

Loss of comms for ten minutes

The surface is the only other set of eyes on the trim and the battery. Ten minutes of silence and the dive is over, whatever is on the bottom. It has happened twice; both times the acoustic modem had fouled on a kelp strand and both times we came up with an empty basket.

02

Trim that will not trim

If two consecutive ballast adjustments do not produce the calculated response, there is something on the frame that should not be there. I stop the descent, hover, and look at the thrusters before going deeper.

03

A client who is cold

The sphere sits at 2 °C for six hours. A scientist who is shivering cannot sample and will not say so. Suit heating is checked on deck; if the observer's hands are cold at 1,000 m, we come up and fly the dive another day.

04

Anything I cannot explain in one sentence

Noise in the frame, a pressure reading that lags, a thruster drawing more current than its neighbours. Unexplained anomalies are not interesting; they are a reason to surface and write it down while the detail is fresh.

Flying with a client scientist

Observers get a two-hour briefing on the sphere, the trim, the life support and what to do if I stop talking. Nobody has ever needed the last part, and I intend to keep it that way.
