Log 03 — The Practice

Nineteen seasons, bottom up

Solveig Iversen came to the sphere the slow way: deck technician on research vessels out of Bergen, launching and recovering other people's dives; then submersible technician, maintaining the vehicle she would later fly; then pilot — first in the 1,000 m class, then 4,500 m, then the 6,500 m class she flies now.

That order matters, and it is why this practice is run the way it is. A pilot who has stood on a rolling deck at 3 a.m. waiting for a recovery does not treat the surface half of a dive as an afterthought. A pilot who has personally replaced a scrubber canister does not argue with one.

“I have never once been impressed by a depth. I have been impressed by a margin.”

The practice is deliberately small: one pilot, a season booked at a time, flown from the client's institute vessel or a chartered support ship. 214 dives as pilot-in-command, 1,186 hours submerged — every one of them in a classed, surveyed vehicle, every one of them logged with its profile and its margins, and the log is the portfolio: extracts are available to charter clients on request.

§ 01

How an expedition runs

Four stages, in order. The planner governs stage three; the abort criteria govern all of them.

STAGE 1

Charter & profiles

Science goals become dive profiles: each site gets a depth, a bottom time and a margin, computed from the printed class figures and agreed before anyone sails. Abort criteria are signed at this stage — not on the day.

STAGE 2

Mobilisation & checks

Vehicle and vessel meet; certificates are read, the pre-dive check series is run end to end, and the season's first dive is always a shallow systems dive, whatever the schedule says.

STAGE 3

Dive days

One dive a day, flown to the profile. Bottom time is spent on the science plan; the reserve is not a negotiating position. A scrubbed day is logged as a successful procedure and the programme re-sequences.

STAGE 4

Debrief & the log

Every dive is written up with its actual profile against its planned one — where the minutes went, what the margins were, what would change. The client keeps the full log; it is their data and their record.

§ 02

Certification — the licence to operate

This profession learned, at terrible cost, that a pressure hull is not a place for improvisation or optimism. The rule here is plain: the practice flies only vehicles that are classed, surveyed and inspected on schedule — and the pilot reads the certificates herself before a season starts. Before the first dive of any engagement, these are on the table:

  • ▸Current class certificate for the vehicle from a recognised classification society, in date for the whole season — not “renewal in progress”.
  • ▸Viewport inspection record — acrylic viewports have a service life and an inspection schedule; both are checked against the vehicle's dive history.
  • ▸Pressure-hull survey status, including any life-extension assessment, with the surveyor's findings unabridged.
  • ▸Life-support test records — scrubber and oxygen systems tested to the printed envelope the abort criteria refer to.
  • ▸The vehicle's own dive log — depth history, incidents, modifications. A modification without a paper trail ends the conversation.

If a vehicle cannot produce these, the practice will not pilot it and — said respectfully, as one professional to the people who fund this work — you should not ride in it either. Enthusiasm is not a certification regime.

§ 03

The honesty page

The ocean does not care about the schedule. Weather windows close, currents run, equipment reads out of envelope. A practice that promises a fixed number of dives in a fixed number of days is selling something the ocean has not agreed to. What is promised here is the discipline: profiles computed in the open, criteria applied without exception, and a log that shows its working.

A record depth with no science done at the bottom is a stunt, and stunts are not chartered here. The deepest dive in the log — 6,480 m — was a survey grid that happened to be deep. If your programme's real aim is a number for a press release, this is the wrong practice, and it is better to say so now.

The reserve is not a resource. Every profile on this site surfaces with at least a quarter of its life support unused. That is not waste; it is the difference between a plan and a hope. Clients are buying the margin as much as the bottom time.

§ 04

Engagement terms

Abort authority. The pilot's decision to scrub, hold or ascend is final, is not subject to client override, and is not a billable dispute. This clause is in the contract because it has to be.

Billing. Sea days are billed at the day rate including weather and scrub days — see the rate card for why that protects the client. Mobilisation is billed at cost against receipts.

Vehicles. Pilot-only engagements are accepted solely on classed vehicles whose certificates and records (see § 02) are produced before contract, not before launch.

Data. All science data, imagery and logs belong to the client. The practice retains its own dive log and may cite anonymised profile figures — depths, times, margins — in its record.

Seasons. One expedition at a time; the 2026 North Atlantic season is open, and planning engagements are waitlisted. Enquiries are answered in the order they arrive.
