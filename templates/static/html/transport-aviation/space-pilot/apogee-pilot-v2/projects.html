02 / Engagement files

Three files, told the way they happened.

Clients are described at programme level — operators do not publish their safety cases through a consultant’s portfolio, and neither do I. What is printed here is the shape of the work and the outcome, including the outcomes that were uncomfortable at the time.

File 01 · Participant preparation

First Season — the programme that said “not yet”

Complete
Client
Suborbital operator, first commercial season
Cohort
24 participants, 3 training cohorts
Profile
Profile A — Standard Arc, 104 km
Duration
11 weeks, screening to final flight

The brief

Prepare the operator’s entire first-season manifest — 24 paying participants — to a standard the commander would sign, and build the preparation programme the operator would run without her afterwards. The operator’s sales material had promised “astronaut training”; the first job was making the promise true and smaller at the same time.

The decisions

The standard was published to participants before training began: aeromedical screening first, three training days, egress against the clock, and a standing right for the programme to say “not yet”. Two participants were held. One was referred back to the aeromedical examiner over blood pressure the screening caught and the sales process had not asked about. One could not meet the egress standard and was moved to a later season with a training plan rather than a refund argument.

The outcome

22 of 24 flew in season, with zero medical or readiness events on flight days. The held participant with the training plan flew the following season. The operator kept the standard verbatim in its own programme — including the “not yet” clause, in the brochure, where it now does the selling.

“Astrid grounded two of our customers and I braced for the refund fight. There wasn’t one — she had explained the standard before we sold a single seat, in writing, in the brochure. The following season one of them flew, and sent her a photograph. That is what the preparation buys.”

File 02 · Crew-training syllabus review

The Ninety-Second Gap

Complete
Client
Operator flight-operations department
Scope
Full crew syllabus, 340 pages + simulator programme
Profile
Profile B — High Arc, 118 km
Duration
6 weeks, documents to re-issue

The brief

An outside read of a mature crew-training syllabus before its annual re-issue. The operator’s own instructors suspected the abort training had drifted; they wanted a commander who had flown the profile to find where, and to say it in writing.

The decisions

The review laid the syllabus against the profile’s own timeline — the same arithmetic as the planner on this site. The finding: on the high-arc profile, the abort decisions that matter most fall inside a roughly ninety-second window during boost and early coast, but the decision drills were scheduled in the glide block, weeks later in the course, taught as theory. The drills were moved into the boost-phase block, rebuilt as timed simulator exercises, and a commander-to-participant communication protocol was added for the window — who speaks, who is silent, and what a participant is told at each call.

The outcome

The revised syllabus was adopted at re-issue with the timeline analysis attached as its rationale — so the next reviewer can check the reasoning, not just the result. The operator’s instructors now run the timed drills themselves; the review’s last recommendation was that it not be needed again.

File 03 · Campaign readiness

The Scrub — a non-event, by design

Complete
Client
Operator campaign team + 6 participants
Scope
Flight-week readiness, weather & participant handling
Profile
Profile A — Standard Arc, 104 km
Duration
One campaign week, two launch windows

The brief

Run flight-week readiness for a six-participant campaign: weather discipline, go/no-go support to the flight director, and the participants — who had travelled from three countries and had exactly one week.

The decisions

Upper winds scrubbed the first window at T−2 hours. The scrub had been rehearsed in training as a normal outcome — participants had a planned scrub day (simulator sessions, a commander’s briefing on the wind data itself, dinner in town) instead of a corridor of rumours. The call was explained with the actual sounding data, in the room, by the person who supported making it. Nobody’s week depended on pretending the weather was a surprise.

The outcome

The campaign flew on the second window, four days later. Refund requests: zero. The operator’s post-campaign survey rated the scrub day among the best-run days of the week — which is the entire argument for rehearsing the flights that don’t happen.
