03 / The commander

The left seat is a teaching job.

I came to the left seat from the ground. My first career was flight-test engineering — chasing research aircraft with a telemetry van and a notebook, learning how programmes actually fail: slowly, in documents, long before anything fails in the air. When commercial suborbital vehicles began flying participants, I retrained as a commander and flew 27 suborbital flights on research and training profiles.

What the flying taught me is that the arc is the easy part. The vehicle goes up and comes down on physics; everything else — what the participants understand, what the crew has rehearsed, what the organisation does when the weather closes — is preparation. So the practice I run now is the preparation: crew training, participant programmes, and outside review for operators who want their safety case read by someone with no season to sell.

I work from Las Cruces, New Mexico, and I still fly check rides. I do not sell seats, I do not take commissions from operators I review, and my name goes on a programme only when the standard on the Profile page is the standard that ran.

03.1 / How programmes run

Four stages, in this order.

STAGE 1
Screening first

Medical and readiness screening before any training is sold or scheduled. It is cheaper — in every currency — to find a problem in a clinic than in a cabin.

STAGE 2
Profile literacy

Crews and participants learn the arc until they can recite it: phases, times, calls, and their own job in each. Nobody should meet the profile for the first time at altitude.

STAGE 3
Rehearsed aborts

The flights that go wrong are practised on the ground — simulator blocks, timed egress, scrub days — until the abnormal is merely unusual.

STAGE 4
Flight-day discipline

Weather read plainly, calls made on criteria not optimism, and a debrief that writes down what the day actually did. Then the paperwork, which is part of the flying.

03.2 / The honesty page

Printed, because brochures won’t.

H.1

Suborbital is not orbit

Reaching 100 km straight up takes a small fraction of the energy of reaching orbit — orbit means travelling at roughly 7.8 km/s sideways so that you keep missing the Earth, which takes on the order of ten times the energy of the arc. A suborbital vehicle does not have that energy and does not pretend to. The flight is a thrown ball’s path, flown well. That is a real thing. It is not the other thing.

H.2

Eleven minutes is the product

Count it on the planner: about eleven minutes wheels-up to touchdown, three or so of them weightless, under a minute above the Kármán line. I have told people with paid-up tickets to go home and see their cardiologist first. Nobody has ever thanked me on the day; two have written since. A ticket is not a qualification, and a short flight is not a lesser one — but it is a short flight, and it will be sold here as one.

H.3

The record, plainly

This industry has killed people. In 2014 a test vehicle broke up in flight and its co-pilot died; there have been other fatal accidents on the ground and in the air across the sector’s short history. Those events are part of the record every participant should hear before flying, from us — not discovered afterwards. The answer this practice keeps returning to is unglamorous: screening, training, rehearsed aborts, and commanders empowered to say no.

H.4

Participants are not “tourists” here

The word matters. A tourist is carried; a participant has a job — egress, communication, brace positions, knowing the calls. The people in the back of these vehicles are crew-assigned participants with training days behind them, and the programmes on this site are built to keep it that way.

H.5

What I won’t do

I won’t sell you a ticket, reserve you a seat, or take a commission from an operator I also review. I won’t overrule an aeromedical examiner, shorten the standard for a schedule, or sign a programme I haven’t watched run. If a review finds nothing wrong, the report says so at the same price as one that finds plenty.

03.3 / Engagement terms

The terms, before the call.

  • Fees follow Table F1 on the Profile page; a written proposal fixes the figure before work begins. Simulator costs pass through at cost.
  • Medical decisions belong to the operator’s aeromedical examiner. This practice coordinates screening; it does not make, soften or appeal medical calls.
  • The commander’s “not yet” on readiness is final within a programme and is not a billable dispute; the participant’s seat and fee position are protected in the programme terms.
  • Reviews are independent: no commissions, no ticket inventory, no success fees. Findings are delivered in writing to the client who commissioned them.
  • Availability: two programme slots per season; campaign weeks are booked a season ahead.

03.4 / Next step

Programmes begin with a conversation.

Tell me the operator, the profile and the season — or, if you are a prospective participant, the flight you are preparing for and what you have been told so far. I will reply with what I would check first.
