Selected programmes

Six campaigns, and the number each one bought.

A programme is only worth listing if it produced a figure that changed a design, a certificate or a permission. These six did. The ones that produced a nice video are not here.

X-3B boom carpet survey

Twelve sorties over eleven days at 11,000 to 16,000 m, with five ground stations on a 19 km lateral line. Established the Mach-altitude envelope for the type's overpressure limit and found a 2.4× focus at Mach 1.08 in a 4 K inversion that no model had predicted.

The client changed the low-altitude speed schedule as a result. The inversion pass is now a mandatory item on every campaign of this type.

2025 · 12 sorties
Mach 1.05–1.85
Peak 3.9 psf at 4 km lateral
Certificate: supplemented type data

Q-2 quiet supersonic shaping

Eighteen sorties on a long-body demonstrator with a low-boom forebody, flown as back-to-back A/B passes against the unshaped configuration at the same Mach and altitude. The shaped aircraft cut peak overpressure by 41% and stretched rise time from 8 ms to 96 ms at the primary station.

Two passes were lost to a fuel imbalance that moved the centre of gravity outside the test tolerance; both were re-flown the same day once the fuel schedule was revised.

2024 · 18 sorties
Mach 1.15–1.42
1.3 psf shaped / 2.2 unshaped
Certificate: research, no type

Lateral cut-off trials, two airframes

A paired trial to measure where the boom stops reaching the ground at 9 km lateral, flown at six altitudes from 8,000 to 19,000 m in 1,000 m steps. The airframes differ by 38 m in length and the shorter one cuts off 2,300 m lower, which was the point of the trial.

Weather cost four of the eleven planned days; the cut-off altitudes came out within 200 m of the prediction for the long airframe and 500 m off for the short one, which is the sort of disagreement that pays for a second campaign.

2023 · 9 sorties
8,000–19,000 m
Cut-off 13,200 / 10,900 m
Both airframes, same crew

Turn focus investigation

A short campaign for a regulator who wanted to know how much a standard 30° turn adds to the ground overpressure. Four sorties, sixteen turns, flown both directions to cancel the wind. The answer was 2.7× at Mach 1.3, falling to 1.4× at Mach 1.7, and it arrives on the inside of the turn several seconds before the aircraft's ground track passes.

2022 · 4 sorties
16 instrumented turns
Focus 1.4× to 2.7×
Client: civil aviation authority

Valley array, terrain effects

A terrain campaign in a 900 m deep valley, where the boom arrives twice: once direct and once reflected off the far wall. Six stations at 1.8 km spacing recorded a double signature on every pass, with the reflection 40 to 90 ms behind the direct arrival and 30% lower.

The result matters for anyone certificating an aircraft over inhabited terrain, and it is the only dataset I know of with paired direct and reflected measurements at this scale.

2020 · 7 sorties
12,100–13,400 m
6 stations, 84 recordings
Client: research consortium

Transonic acceleration limits

Fourteen passes through Mach 0.95 to 1.15 at three altitudes, measuring overpressure as a function of acceleration rate. Below 0.02 M/s the boom is indistinguishable from steady flight; above 0.05 M/s the focus climbs quickly and the pass stops being a certification data point and starts being an event.

2019 · 6 sorties
14 acceleration passes
0.01–0.07 M/s rates
Certificate: type data, engine limits

Winter campaign, weather losses

A ten-day window in February produced six usable days. The two dry days were used for ground-station calibration and microphone swaps; the two lost days were lost to a jet stream that put 60 kt of wind at 12 km and moved the carpet 4 km off the planned line.

The lesson was not about weather. It was that a campaign without two spare days is a campaign that reports half a dataset.

2017 · 6 of 10 days
3,000 m cloud base
60 kt at 12 km
Client: airframer, repeat work

What the next campaign needs

An airframe, an envelope, a ground-station budget and a regulator who has agreed what the certificate will say. In that order. The last one is usually the slowest.
