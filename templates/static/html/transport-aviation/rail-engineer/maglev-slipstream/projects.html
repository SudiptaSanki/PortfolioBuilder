Four routes, measured in millimetres

Each entry gives the length, the tolerance we held and the number that mattered. The stopped concept is here with the reason, because cost per kilometre is the constraint that ends most maglev projects.

01

Yamanashi section 4

Test line · 12.4 km · 2019–2021
In service

Twelve kilometres of new guideway through a valley with a 4 % grade, tied into the existing test line at both ends. The tie-in was the hard part: the new beams had to meet a line built to a different survey datum in 1996, and the joint could not have a step.

We re-observed the old control network with GNSS and found 11 mm of accumulated drift over 24 years. The alignment was re-fitted rather than the new line being bent to match, which cost six weeks and saved the ride quality at the joint.

02

Low-pressure tube trial

Aero test section · 1.2 km · 2023
Operating

A 1.2 km tube at 30 kPa around a single-track guideway, built to measure the pressure wave a car pushes ahead of itself and what it does to the gap control. The first run lost 40 % of the lift margin at the portal because the pressure transient loaded the car downwards.

The fix was a 14 m vent slot at each portal and a slower pressure ramp in the loop. The aerodynamics is now measured; the cost of the tube is still the reason no operator has built 40 km of it.

03

Urban shuttle study

City route · 18 km · 2024
Study delivered

An 18 km urban route at 200 km/h with eight stations, where the value is acceleration rather than top speed. At that speed the magnets and the gap control are easy; the guideway is not, because the alignment has to thread under a river and over two rail corridors with a 5 m vertical envelope.

We delivered the alignment, the station interface and a cost range. The city is still deciding, and the honest answer is that a conventional metro would carry more people for less money — but the maglev route has no tunnel ventilation to build.

04

900 km/h concept

Long-distance · 320 km · closed 2022
Stopped

A 320 km route at 900 km/h in a 10 kPa tube, priced against a conventional maglev at 500 km/h. The vehicle worked in the model, the tube did not: the pressure vessel, the airlocks at every station and the thermal expansion of a 320 km steel tube put the cost at 2.4× the 500 km/h option for a 22 % time saving.

We stopped the study at the concept stage and published the comparison. Two years later the same arithmetic is still the reason nobody has built one.
