Trusses that hold their shape in a thermal vacuum

We design the metal between the instruments. A bay has to survive 9.4 g at launch, then hold a 2 mm alignment through a 240 K eclipse cycle with nothing but conduction to move heat. Those two jobs pull in opposite directions, and the whole practice lives in the compromise.

Load cases and how we work

01

Launch

Quasi-static 9.4 g axial and 3.1 g lateral with a 1.25 qualification factor, plus a sine sweep at 5 oct/min to catch a joint that only opens under vibration. Every bolt is a preload calculation before it is a drawing.

02

Thermal

Eclipse entry drops a sunlit tube 240 K in eleven minutes. A truss bows because one face cools before the other, and a 20 K through-depth gradient on a 3 m bay is worth a few millimetres of pointing error at the end of a mast.

03

Microgravity

Without gravity there is no self-weight to damp a mode, and a crew push is an impulse with nowhere to go. We model the 0–50 Hz band, find the local modes the global model misses, and tune the joints with viscoelastic shims if they land in the control band.

Notes on this sheet

  1. Numbers in the model are closed-form estimates. Flight hardware is qualified by test, and a test that disagrees with the model is the one we believe.
  2. “Alignment budget” is the allowed movement of an instrument line of sight at the tip, not the truss tolerance at assembly.
  3. All four material systems have flown. CFRP is the default where mass is scarce; Invar exists for the case where nothing is allowed to move and mass is not.
