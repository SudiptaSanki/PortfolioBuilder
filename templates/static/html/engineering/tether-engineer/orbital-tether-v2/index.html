DYNAMICS LOG · VAN DER MEER TETHER DYNAMICS · BREMEN

Tethers fail interestingly. Design for it.

I am Dr Jeroen van der Meer, a space tether mechanics and cable dynamics specialist. I run dynamics analysis, deployment studies and independent design reviews for agencies, satellite programmes and research groups. The honest starting point, printed here rather than in a footnote: the flight record of tethers is a record of partial successes and instructive failures — jammed deployers, severed lines, one satellite lost. The dynamics are nonlinear, deployment is the dangerous hour, and a sizing number is the start of an analysis, never the end of one.

ENTRY 01

The tension sizer

RUNS ON TABLES 1 + 2, BELOW

Every study starts with the preliminary relation printed here, exactly as it appears in my reports. For a tip mass m held on a near-vertical tether of length L in an orbit with angular rate n = 2π/T, the gravity-gradient tension at the tip mass is approximately T ≈ (3/2) · m · n² · L. It assumes a tether of negligible mass — so the sizer checks that assumption against Table 2 as it goes, and flags it in print when the assumption stops holding. Choose an orbit, set the mass and length, pick a line material.

Read the diameter honestly: pure tension sizing gives a line thinner than a hair, and nobody flies that line. Real tethers are sized by survivability — micrometeoroid tolerance, handling, deployment snatch loads — which is why flown lines are far thicker than this arithmetic suggests. The sizer sizes the load. The study sizes the line.

FIG. 02 — The geometry the sizer prices. A gravity-gradient tether hangs along the local vertical because the nearer end is pulled harder than the farther end; the difference is the tension. The system librates — swings — about that vertical at angle θ, and managing the swing is most of the real engineering. Original diagram, drawn for this page.

ENTRY 02

Failure modes, listed like weather

THE REGISTER EVERY STUDY OPENS

A tether study that begins with performance is written backwards. Mine begin with this register: the ways tether systems have actually failed, or nearly have, and what an analysis owes each one. Severity is the practice’s own grading — mission-ending, mission-shortening, or manageable, if modelled.

THE FAILURE REGISTER — REVIEWED AT THE START OF EVERY ENGAGEMENT, AGAINST THE CLIENT’S ACTUAL DESIGN, NOT A GENERIC ONE.
Failure modeWhat happensSeverityWhat the study does about it
Deployment libration runaway Deploying line feeds energy into the swing. Past a rate-dependent threshold the libration grows instead of settling, and the system can end up rotating or wrapped. MISSION-ENDING Deployment-rate profile simulated across the full length range, with the unstable bands named and excluded before flight.
Skip-rope resonance The line’s transverse modes — the skipping-rope modes — couple to libration or to deployer forcing at specific deployed lengths. Models without bending stiffness can miss it entirely. MISSION-ENDING High-fidelity bead model with bending terms; resonance lengths printed in the report, not averaged away.
Micrometeoroid / debris severance A single-strand line is one particle away from two free-flying objects. The flown record includes a tether severed days after a successful deployment. MISSION-ENDING Survivability sizing (multi-strand or tape geometries), flux exposure by orbit regime, expected-life arithmetic shown.
Deployer jam or snag The line stops paying out partway — a protruding bolt did exactly this to a 20 km experiment, which deployed 256 m. The mission becomes a retrieval problem. MISSION-SHORTENING Deployer design review, payout-torque margins, and a written partial-deployment plan before anyone flies.
Electrodynamic arcing A bare conductive tether collects current along its length; an insulation flaw can arc and sever the line — the accepted explanation for the loss of a 19.7 km experiment. MISSION-ENDING Current-collection modelling, insulation and termination review, arc-risk statement in the deliverable.
Slack-line snap loads Orbital perturbations can momentarily slacken a line; when it re-tensions, the snatch load is a multiple of the steady tension the sizer prints. MISSION-SHORTENING Minimum-tension analysis across the orbit; snatch factors applied to fittings and terminations, stated numerically.

WHAT MOVES THE PRICE, IN ORDER

  1. Dynamics scope — a steady-state sizing is days; a full deployment campaign with resonance mapping is weeks. This is most of the price.
  2. Deployment analysis — flying within 18 months adds the deployment study, because that is where the risk lives.
  3. Model fidelity — more beads, bending stiffness, deployer mechanics: each fidelity step is priced as simulation cases, and the report says which step changed the answer.
