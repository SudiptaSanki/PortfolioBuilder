ABOUT & METHOD · THE SPECIALIST, THE STUDY, THE RECORD

A small field, learned the hard way. Mostly by other people’s hardware.

I am Dr Jeroen van der Meer. I trained as an aerospace engineer, spent my doctoral years on flexible-body dynamics — the mathematics of things that bend, swing and vibrate when they were supposed to hold still — and have specialised in space tether mechanics since 2016, first inside agency study teams and since 2021 as an independent practice in Bremen.

Bremen is a deliberate address. The city’s drop tower gives researchers 4.7 seconds of free fall at a time, and deployment mechanisms can be tested there — payout behaviour, tip-mass separation, the first seconds of a line going out. Orbits cannot be tested there. That gap, between what can be rehearsed on the ground and what can only be simulated, is exactly where this practice works, and the method below is built around respecting it.

The practice is small on purpose: one specialist, a simulation toolchain that gets rebuilt per engagement rather than trusted from the last one, and a hard rule that every number in a deliverable carries its assumptions on its face. Clients are agencies, satellite programmes and research groups. The work is studies, not hardware — I sell analysis, and the analysis is the whole product.

METHOD

How a study runs

FOUR STAGES, IN ORDER
STAGE 01

Mission geometry

Orbit, masses, lengths, deployer characteristics — agreed in writing before any simulation. Most bad tether studies are born here, in a geometry nobody signed. The preliminary sizing (the front-page relation) is run at this stage and printed in the study as the baseline everything else must beat or explain.

STAGE 02

Dynamics modelling

A lumped-mass (bead) model built for this system, with bending stiffness where deployment makes it matter. Fidelity is increased in priced steps, and the report states which step changed the answer — if a coarser model would have missed the finding, that is written down, because the next programme deserves to know.

STAGE 03

Deployment analysis

The dangerous hour, simulated end to end: rate profiles swept, unstable bands mapped, resonance lengths printed as numbers to avoid. The deliverable includes a partial-deployment plan — what the operations team does if the line stops at 40%, written before flight, not improvised during it.

STAGE 04

Review & margins

Findings are stated against the failure register, one mode at a time, with the margin policy applied uniformly: preliminary factor of 4 on line breaking strength, snatch factors on fittings, survivability sized separately from strength. Recommendations are allowed to be “do not fly this” — three delivered studies have been exactly that.

TAUGHT MOSTLY BY FAILING

Tether engineering has a short flight history and an unusually instructive one. These are the missions the field actually learned from, at programme level. Any specialist who quotes you the successes without this table is selling something.

THE FLIGHT RECORD, AS THE FIELD TELLS IT — PROGRAMME LEVEL, FROM THE PUBLIC RECORD.
MissionYearWhat happenedWhat it taught
TSS-11992The deployer jammed — a protruding bolt — after roughly 256 m of a planned 20 km deployment. The satellite was retrieved.Deployer mechanics are flight-critical hardware, not packaging. Partial deployment needs a plan.
SEDS-11993A 20 km tether deployed successfully and the payload deorbited as planned.Deployment dynamics, modelled well, can work exactly as simulated. The field’s founding success.
SEDS-21994Deployed successfully — then the tether was severed days later, most likely by a debris or micrometeoroid strike.A single strand is a single point of failure. Survivability sizing exists because of this entry.
TSS-1R1996Deployed to about 19.7 km and generated real electrodynamic power — then the tether broke, most likely through an insulation flaw and arcing. The satellite was lost.Electrodynamic tethers work, and their failure modes are electrical as much as mechanical.
TiPS1996A 4 km non-conducting tether that survived in orbit for years, far beyond early expectations.Durability data is possible: some constructions endure. Long-life claims should cite this class of evidence.
YES22007Deployed roughly 30 km of tether — among the longest structures ever flown. The return capsule was released; its recovery was never confirmed.Deployment at record length is achievable; tracking and end-of-mission operations are part of the dynamics problem.
ProSEDS2003An electrodynamic propulsion demonstration cancelled before flight.Programmes die of risk posture as well as physics. A study that prices risk honestly is a deliverable, not an obstacle.

A spreadsheet is not a simulation. The sizer on this site’s front page implements one preliminary relation under one stated assumption, and flags the assumption when it fails. It cannot see libration, skip-rope modes, deployer friction, plasma contactors, thermal snap, or your operations team at 3 a.m. Studies exist because the gap between that arithmetic and a flying system is where missions are lost.

What this practice will not do: certify a system as flight-ready (that authority belongs to the programme and its review boards), quote a deployment success probability as a single number, or soften a “do not deploy” finding into a “proceed with caution”. Caution is not a rate profile. The finding is the product; clients who want a different product should hire a different practice.

ENGAGEMENT TERMS, IN BRIEF

  • Studies are advisory. Flight decisions, and responsibility for them, remain with the operator and its review authorities — stated in every deliverable.
  • Fixed scope once the mission geometry is agreed in writing; geometry changes re-open the estimate, openly, before work continues.
  • No success fees, in either direction. A study that recommends against deployment is billed at the same rate as one that clears it.
  • Client work is confidential; findings may be published in anonymised, aggregated form after 24 months unless the client objects in writing.
  • One engagement per competing programme in the same mission class at a time. Conflicts are declared before contract, not discovered during it.
