Method · the wire and the plasma

A tether is a single-point failure that lasts two years

There is no redundancy in a wire. Everything we do is aimed at the moment the line is deployed, because after that the only decisions left are whether to keep the current on and when to cut.

StudioLongline AS, Trondheim. Four engineers and a workshop that used to build fishing gear instrumentation, which is where the spool design came from.

FacilitiesA 22 m drop tower for deployment, a 4 m³ plasma chamber with a hollow-cathode test stand, and a hypervelocity range shared with the university.

StandardsISO 24113 for orbital debris, ECSS-E-ST-10-04C for the space environment, and the IADC 25-year guideline as the deorbit target.

RuleNo tether flies without two independent cut mechanisms and a deployment test at the flight tension profile.

An electrodynamic tether does not push against anything. It moves charge across the geomagnetic field and the field pushes back, which means the spacecraft's orbit is the propellant and the wire has to survive long enough to spend it.

  1. 01

    Mission model

    Orbit, mass, drag area, plasma density and the debris rule that applies. We run the deboost in the calculator on the front page, then a full numerical propagation with the field model and the actual duty cycle.

    2–3 weeks
  2. 02

    Line and spool design

    Conductor, insulation, tape versus round wire, and the winding pattern that lets 5 km come off a 90 mm spool without a snag. We have wound every line we have flown by hand.

    6–10 weeks
  3. 03

    Deployment test

    The full length, at 1 g, from the flight spool, with the flight tension profile and a camera on the exit guide. It takes a day and it has found a problem on four out of five designs.

    1 day + rebuild
  4. 04

    Plasma and contactor

    The contactor runs in the chamber at flight current for 500 h before it is accepted, and we measure its impedance curve rather than assuming one. A contactor that quenches at 2 A turns the mission into a passive drag experiment.

    5–8 weeks
  5. 05

    Cut and survive

    Two cutters, independent power and telemetry, and a hypervelocity test of the line against a 1 mm aluminium sphere at 12 km/s. The line will be cut by something; the question is whether it fails safe.

    4 weeks
  6. 06

    Operations

    Deploy, measure the current, compare with the model, and decide within one orbit whether the line stays. We ask for 30 days of commissioning support and a data-sharing agreement, because the flight data is the only thing that improves the model.

    30 days

The physics is generous. The engineering is not: a 5 km line, 0.5 mm², is 6.8 kg of aluminium that has to survive launch, deployment, thermal cycling and whatever the sky throws at it.

Why the current is lower than the arithmetic suggests

The Lorentz force is F = I L B, and with a 5 km line at 400 km altitude the geometry is friendly: 2.59 × 10⁻⁵ T and 7.67 km/s give about a kilovolt of EMF. The limit is not the field, it is the contactor. A hollow cathode can emit around an amp before erosion and power become the problem, and the plasma on the day side is not always dense enough to take it. Real missions average a fraction of the peak current, which is why the calculator applies a 2/π orbit factor and a 55 % duty cycle, and why we ask for flight data in return.

What we do not claim

We do not sell tether systems as a replacement for a propulsion module on a large satellite, and we do not accept a mission where the tether is the only deorbit mechanism and the customer has no backup plan. The line is a single point of failure and the failure mode is a 5 km conductor in a crowded orbit. Every design we ship can be cut, and every operational plan starts with the assumption that one day we will cut it.

We also do not claim that a tether is a space elevator. A 20 km partial elevator is a different engineering problem with a different failure rate, and we closed our own study on it for exactly that reason. It is on the programmes page.
