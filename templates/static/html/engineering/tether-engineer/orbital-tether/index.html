How a tether mission is built

Station 01 · 400 km

Deploy without a snag

A 5 km line is wound on a spool the size of a coffee tin and comes out at 8 m/s. The deployment is the single most dangerous hour of the mission: any snag, any thruster plume, any residual charge and the line becomes a 5 km whip around the spacecraft. We deploy in a sun-pointing attitude with the contactor cold, and we cut the line if the tension trace ever goes outside a band.

Station 02 · 1 000 km

Make contact with nothing

At 400 km the plasma is thin enough that a bare wire collects current only near the ends; the hollow-cathode contactor does the rest. It has to expel a neutralised plasma without eroding its own insert, at 3 A for hours, in an environment that swings from eclipse to full sun every 96 minutes. The contactor is the part that fails first, and it is the part we test longest.

Station 03 · 35 786 km

Stay in the sky for the hard cases

A tether is also a way to hold an orbit without fuel: at higher altitude the same wire can push rather than brake. We have modelled a 12 km conductor for a geostationary station-keeping role, where the Δv bill is 50 m/s a year and the mass of the propellant is the mass of the spacecraft. It is not a product yet. The cut rate at that altitude, and the charging behaviour during a solar storm, are not good enough to sell.

What we are looking for

Small satellites between 6 and 250 kg in low Earth orbit, with a mission that ends earlier than the hardware does. We do the deorbit and station-keeping analysis, the deployment mechanism and the cut test, and we fly on your spacecraft rather than ours.
