A hull is a compromise between film and pressure.

Eleven people, a 40-metre layup table, two weld robots and a flight-test range over the Bodensee. We do not make the biggest envelopes in Europe. We make the ones whose mass budget closes.

Why the hull always wins the argument

An airship is a lifting bag with an engineering problem attached. The bag has to hold helium at a pressure that varies with altitude, temperature and sun angle, while weighing almost nothing, while not leaking, while staying repairable in a field tent. Every kilogram of laminate is a kilogram of payload you cannot carry, so the whole discipline is the removal of material.

Höhenwerk started in 2016 as a spin-out from a kite-surfing sailmaker's laminate lab — which is why the seams and the load tapes look the way they do. The first envelope was 300 m³ and burst at 2.1 times its design pressure in a test cell in Konstanz. That number, 2.1, is still the qualification factor we quote to customers, and it is written on the wall above the weld table.

Four layers, one weld

Our current skin is a 1,250-denier Vectran weave laminated between a 25 µm PA-11 film and a 12 µm PVDF outer layer. The weave carries the load, the PA-11 stops helium, the PVDF stops UV. Seams are radio-frequency welded in a 12 mm double lap, then taped with a 3M structural adhesive that stays flexible to −80 °C.

The failure mode we watch is not a burst. It is a slow delamination running along a seam after a hundred thermal cycles — invisible from outside, catastrophic at pressure. Every new laminate gets 500 cycles between −70 and +40 °C in the climate cell before it goes anywhere near a hull.

We can predict the burst pressure to within 4%. We cannot yet predict the leak rate of a seam after five years, and neither can anyone else.

What we actually test

  • Coupon burst: 2.1× design differential pressure, five specimens per laminate batch.
  • Helium permeation: mass spectrometry on a 1 m² coupon held at 20 km pressure and −56 °C for 30 days.
  • Seam peel: 90° peel at −70 °C, 40 °C and 20% relative humidity, before and after cycling.
  • Full-hull proof: inflation to 1.25× limit with strain gauges at 96 points, then a 14-day hold.

Where the numbers come from

The sizing bench on the front page is the same model we use for the first customer conversation, with the same atmosphere and the same balance. It is deliberately conservative: it holds the gas 20 K above ambient at float, assumes the superpressure limit is reached at station, and counts the gas mass inside the volume that has to be lifted. Real hulls come out 6–11% larger once the fittings, fins and ballonet are added.

Nine years of envelopes

  1. 2016 — 2017

    The 300 m³ bag

    First envelope in a rented sail loft. Burst at 2.1× design pressure; the number became the qualification factor.

  2. 2018 — 2020

    Coupon programme

    500 thermal cycles on eleven laminate batches. Two suppliers dropped, one weld schedule rewritten.

  3. 2021 — 2023

    First stratospheric hull

    HW-04 Aurora, 30,000 m³, 61 days at 20 km over the Atacama with a 450 kg instrument package.

  4. 2024 — now

    Series production

    Three hulls in build, a 60,000 m³ telecom envelope on the table, and a ballonet that finally survives the day swing.
