Sheet 03 · three things that decide the hull

Buoyancy is the easy part

01

Lift is a subtraction, and the second term is the expensive one

Lift is envelope volume times the difference between air and gas density. At 20 km, air is 0.088 kg/m³ and helium at 600 Pa superpressure is 0.012 — so almost a fifth of your volume is lifting the gas itself. Switch to hydrogen and you buy back 8% of lift, and a whole set of handling rules with it.

02

The day–night cycle is a pressure problem

Sun on the envelope raises the gas 20–30 K above ambient. At constant volume that is 500–800 Pa of extra differential pressure — enough to burst a hull sized only for the cold case. Either the envelope carries the pressure, or a ballonet absorbs it, or you vent gas and lose altitude every night.

03

Film mass scales with the square, volume with the cube

Double the volume and the skin area grows about 1.6×, while lift grows 2×. That is why every serious stratospheric airship is enormous: below about 20,000 m³, the hull cannot lift its own laminate plus a useful payload at 20 km.

Sheet 04 · the ten-kilometre problem

Why 20 km, and what it costs to stay there

Above the jet stream and below the ozone layer, winds are 10–25 m/s instead of 60. Station keeping is possible with 3–6 kW of thrust; below 15 km it is not.
