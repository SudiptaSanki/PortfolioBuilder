The practice · who, and how

A process engineer who ended up in the nose business

Dr Ines Almeida trained as a chemical engineer and spent nine years on process plant — dosing systems, ventilation, instrumentation — before a simulator manufacturer asked a question nobody on their team could answer: how much scent, exactly, reaches the trainee?

It turned out to be a process-engineering question wearing a costume. A scent channel is a dosing pump. A training hall is a vessel with a ventilation rate. A threshold is a specification limit with an unusually honest error bar, because the sensor is a person and people differ. The practice she founded in Cambridge works that seam: process discipline applied to the least disciplined sense.

The work splits three ways. Training and simulation clients need cues that teach and logs that prove the dose. Healthcare clients need delivery so controlled it can be written into a care setting's risk assessment — and a supplier who will not decorate their pilot with claims. Research clients need the thing the field chronically lacks: delivered doses they can put in a methods section without blushing.

The practice is deliberately small: Ines, one bench technician, and a panel of twelve regular “noses” whose thresholds are charted the way other firms chart test equipment — because that is what they are. When a commission needs a bigger team, she says so and names the partners, rather than quietly subcontracting your regulator-facing rig to strangers.

Her standing opinion, printed here so clients can hold her to it: if a channel doesn't change what the trainee does, it is theatre with a cartridge — and theatre is a fine thing to buy, as long as nobody invoices it as engineering.

The working page · how an engagement runs

Five stages, in order, for a reason

The order is the method. Dose design before hardware, hardware before calibration, calibration before anyone is allowed to say the word “immersive”.

  1. 01

    Environment survey

    The room is measured first: true volume, air changes as built, background odour, where noses actually are. Fixed fee, £1,400, findings in writing and yours to keep. Roughly one survey in five ends the engagement here — honestly, and cheaply.

  2. 02

    Dose design

    Compounds are chosen against the printed bands in Table D1, targets set at the nose rather than the nozzle, and ramp, pulse and purge timing designed around adaptation. If the teaching goal can be met with fewer channels, the design says so here, where changing it is free.

  3. 03

    Build & integration

    Channels, manifolds and control are built and integrated with the simulator, building system or rig. Every channel leaves the bench with its own calibration curve, filed in the bench record — not a shared “typical” curve.

  4. 04

    Calibration at the nose

    Delivered concentration is measured where the person is, across the room and across a cartridge's life, and adjusted until the measured dose sits in the design band. This stage is a visit, a set of instruments, and an argument with the building's ventilation that the building usually loses.

  5. 05

    Protocol & handover

    The client receives the dosing protocol: weighing and retirement schedules, purge routines, per-session logging, and the drift checks their own staff run. The practice stays available for re-calibration visits, but a system the client cannot run without its maker is a system that was never finished.

The honesty page · read before commissioning anyone

What this trade's brochures skip

  • Thresholds are bands, not numbers. For a single compound, the most and least sensitive people in one room can differ by a factor of a hundred. Any supplier quoting you one threshold figure to two decimal places is quoting you a fiction with decimals on it.
  • Adaptation is not a malfunction. A constant scent disappears from perception within minutes — the nose is working as designed. Systems that ignore this either over-dose (and draw complaints) or quietly stop being perceived (and draw renewal invoices). Pulsing, purging and timing are the engineering answer; “stronger cartridges” are not.
  • The forty-year forecast. Digital scent has been five years from the mainstream since the 1960s — cinema scent, desktop scent printers, phone scent modules, all launched, all gone. What survives is narrower: controlled dose in controlled settings, for people with a defined job for it. That narrower thing is what this practice sells, and it is enough.
  • No health claims live here. The practice delivers concentrations and keeps records. Whether a scent cue helps anyone's health, mood, memory or recovery is a question for clinicians and trials — and any supplier who answers it on a sales page is telling you something about their evidence standards.
  • The room is part of the instrument. A dose calculated for the hall as specified, delivered into the hall as built, can miss by half. This is why the survey exists, why it comes first, and why its fee is fixed: nobody should be able to talk themselves out of measuring the room.

Engagement terms · the short version, in print

Terms

Survey first. New environments start with the fixed-fee survey (S.01). Its report belongs to the client, including when it recommends spending nothing further.

Quotes in writing. After the survey, build work is quoted as a fixed figure against a written scope. The estimator on the dose sheet is a shape, never the quote.

Payment shape. 30% on commission, 40% at bench acceptance, 30% at calibration sign-off. Calibration sign-off is against measured delivered dose, not against “it smells right to us”.

Consumables at cost. Cartridges and compounds pass through itemised at cost. The practice earns nothing from the chemistry it specifies, so it has no reason to specify more of it.

Claims clause. The practice's name, figures and dose records may not be used to support therapeutic, health or performance claims. Dose records may always be used to support statements about dose.

Regulated settings. In healthcare and safety-training settings, the client's own regulatory duties remain the client's; the practice supplies engineering records to support them and works inside the client's risk assessment, not instead of it.

Availability. One build slot at a time, currently a six-week waitlist for S.03; surveys and audits book inside a fortnight. Cambridge-based; surveys and calibration visits travel.

Start with the room, not the rig

The survey is one day in your space and a written set of numbers. Everything else in this trade is arithmetic built on those numbers — or on guesses.
