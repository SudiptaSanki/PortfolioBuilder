Digital scent engineering — dose · delivery · calibration

Smell is a dose,
not a vibe.

I design scent-delivery systems for people whose work depends on the dose being right: training simulators that teach gas-leak recognition, healthcare environments that cue without alarming, research rigs that must deliver the same concentration on Tuesday as they did in March. The product is never “a smell”. It is a measured amount of a known compound, delivered to a known place, at a known time — with the record to prove it.

Run the dose calculator Read the case files
The honest part, up front: digital scent has been five years away for forty years. Thresholds vary by orders of magnitude between one nose and the next, so every figure on this site is a population band — never a promise about a person. And this practice writes no therapeutic claims: the systems deliver a controlled dose; what a client does with it is their regulated business.

Within band

Steady state assumes perfect mixing and a constant source. Real rooms are neither: in design work the practice applies a mixing factor measured on site, and the first minutes after switch-on are a ramp, not a step. *Hydrogen sulfide is never dosed as itself outside sealed rigs — simulators use a surrogate matched to the band, and the substitution is printed on the rig.

What moves the price

  • Channel count — firstEvery channel is a compound, a cartridge, a manifold leg and a calibration curve. Channels are the price; that is why the audit exists.
  • The environmentA leaky, high-ACH hall can double the emission hardware needed for the same concentration at the nose. The room is quoted, not just the rig.
  • Verification depthA rig that must prove its dose to a regulator costs more than one that must merely smell right to its users. Both are legitimate; they are different products.
  • What does not move itThe findings. “Ship three channels, not twelve” and “do not build this” bill exactly like good news.
