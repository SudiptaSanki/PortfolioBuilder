ENTRY 01

The phase & fringe tool

RUNS ON TABLE 1, BELOW

This is the arithmetic every engagement starts with. Pick one of the three sources on the bench, then change the optical path difference ΔL. The tool works from the two relations printed under it — Δφ = 2π · ΔL ÷ λ and Lc = λ² ÷ Δλ — and shows its working, line by line. Note what ΔL means: the path difference. In a Michelson layout, one mirror moving t changes ΔL by 2t, because the light makes the trip twice.

The fringe strip, modelled live. Step ΔL by one wavelength and the pattern returns to itself — a single fringe reading cannot tell 316 nm from 949 nm, which is why real systems count fringes instead of admiring them. Contrast follows the bench rule printed here: it holds while ΔL stays well under Lc, fades across it, and is gone at Lc. The strip models that as a straight line; real sources are kinder at the start and crueller at the end.

N = ΔL ÷ λ = 316.4 ÷ 632.8 = 0.500 fringes
Δφ = 2π × N = 2π × 0.500 = 3.142 rad
Lc = λ² ÷ Δλ = 632.8² ÷ 0.000002 = 200,217,920,000 nm ≈ 200.2 m

WHAT MOVES THE PRICE · 1

The stability requirement. A nanometre-class specification in a working factory costs environment work — isolation, enclosure, scheduling around the building — not optics. This is priced first because it decides everything else.

WHAT MOVES THE PRICE · 2

Range, then timeline. Long travel and wide bandwidth cost real money in stages and sources. A deadline that forces night work on a live production floor costs more than the same work in a quiet week.

WHAT DOES NOT MOVE IT

The answer. A report costs the same whichever way the measurement lands. A measurement whose fee depends on its result is not a measurement.
