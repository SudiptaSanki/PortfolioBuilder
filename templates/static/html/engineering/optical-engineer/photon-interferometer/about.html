Everything above 100 Hz is noise, and everything below 1 Hz is weather.

Two optical tables on separate foundations, a climate room held to ±0.1 K, and a habit of measuring the same part twice on different days. Interferometry is mostly the art of knowing which of your fringes are real.

Why we measure twice

A phase-shifting interferometer will give you a number for anything you put in front of it, including a part that has moved during the acquisition. The 13-frame sequence takes 40 ms; a bus passing outside takes 200 ms of settling and leaves a 12 nm step in the phase map that looks exactly like a polishing error.

So every measurement is repeated after a 90° rotation of the part, and the two phase maps are compared. What does not rotate with the part is the bench, not the optic — and it comes out of the result. The same part is measured again on a different day, on the other table, by a different person before a certificate is signed.

The chain from laser to number

  • Source. A frequency-stabilised HeNe, 632.8 nm, locked to a iodine cell for the reference bench; a 1 MHz DFB at 1550 nm for coated parts that cannot be touched at visible wavelengths.
  • Reference. A λ/20 reference flat, calibrated annually against a national institute artefact. Its figure is subtracted from every measurement, and its certificate number goes into the report.
  • Acquisition. 13 frames, 5-phase, 1024² camera, 40 ms total, with the piezo calibrated by the fringe count itself.
  • Analysis. Least-squares phase unwrap, Zernike fit to 36 terms, and the residual reported separately from the fit — because a 36-term fit can hide a 40 nm scratch.
A tolerance of λ/100 is 6.3 nanometres. The table settles by more than that when someone leans on it, which is why the room has a rule about leaning.

What we cannot measure

Rough surfaces. If the local slope exceeds about 4°, the fringes close on themselves and the unwrap fails — that is a stylus or a white-light job, and we will say so before you book the bench. We also cannot measure through a coating that absorbs at the wavelength you want to use, and we will not certify a part measured in a different orientation from the one it will be used in.

Fourteen years on the table

  1. 2012 — 2014

    One table, one laser

    A second-hand HeNe and a borrowed camera, measuring flatness for a local mirror grinder. The first report was wrong by a factor of two; the reference flat was upside down.

  2. 2015 — 2018

    Phase shifting

    Piezo stage, 13-frame acquisition and a proper unwrapper. The lab stopped counting fringes by eye and started reporting nanometres.

  3. 2019 — 2022

    Etalons and coatings

    The Fabry-Pérot bench for lidar filters, and a low-coherence gauge for a coating line — the first instrument we built to leave the lab.

  4. 2023 — now

    Space and cold optics

    A cryogenic interferometer for a dilution fridge and the 1.2 m mirror bench, both of which needed the room rebuilt around them.

Working with the bench

Send the part drawing before the purchase order

Slope, coating and size decide whether this is an interferometer job at all. A photograph of the surface and a note of the tolerance usually settles it in one reply.
