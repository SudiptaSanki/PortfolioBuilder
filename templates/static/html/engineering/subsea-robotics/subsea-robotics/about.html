The operator

Sixteen seasons between the quay and the workface.

I came into this trade in 2009 as an ROV trainee on North Sea construction vessels — the person who coils the umbilical, greases the connectors and learns the vehicle one flooded junction box at a time. Pilot-technician by 2012, senior pilot on inspection campaigns by 2016, and inspection lead — the person who writes the plan and answers for the data — from 2019.

Since 2021 I have worked independently, leading campaigns for operators, wind-farm owners, port authorities and aquaculture groups who need inspection data they can defend: to a regulator, to class, to an insurer, or to their own integrity team. I lead every campaign personally and crew spreads from a small bench of pilots and engineers I have worked alongside for years — you are never handed to whoever was available.

Competence is assessed and logged against IMCA guidance for ROV personnel, and every campaign runs under a written dive plan with named decision points. The certificates matter less than the habit they are meant to prove: the plan is written before the vessel sails, and it includes the conditions under which we stop.

The honest summary of the job: the vehicle is the easy part. The work is judgement — what to inspect first, what the picture actually shows, and when the right deliverable is a phone call that says not today.

The working page

How a campaign runs

Five stages, in order, every time. Most inspection failures are born in stage one and paid for in stage four.

  1. 01

    Plan

    The dive plan is written from the asset drawings, the last inspection and the tide tables: task sequence with critical items first, vehicle and sensor fit, current and weather limits, decision points with times on them. You approve the plan — including its stop conditions — before anything mobilises.

  2. 02

    Mobilise

    Spread tested on the quay and again on deck: vehicle, cameras, sensors, positioning, recording. A sensor that fails on deck costs an hour; the same failure at 200 m costs the dive. Mobilisation is a fixed one-off on the rate card, by transit distance.

  3. 03

    The dive day

    Launch inside the planned window. The pilot flies the vehicle; a second pair of eyes events the video live, so gaps are known the same day. I supervise, watch the current against the plan’s limits, and make the stop calls the plan already authorised.

  4. 04

    Data QC

    Every dive’s footage, readings and positions are checked against the plan before demobilisation. If a section is short, it is re-flown while the spread is still on the vessel — never discovered missing three weeks later in the report phase.

  5. 05

    Delivery

    The full deliverable set — evented video log, registers, stills, report in your template — within ten working days of demobilisation, included in the spread rate. Findings that change your plans are phoned through the day they are confirmed, not held for the report.

Plain words

Vehicle classes, without the brochure

Eyes only

Observation-class ROV

Small, tethered, camera-and-lights vehicles. Brilliant for hulls, harbour walls, fish-farm nets and a first look at anything. No manipulators, no real intervention, and they get pushed around above a knot of current. The cheapest honest answer when looking is all you need.

Eyes + hands

Work-class ROV

The offshore industry’s hands: hydraulic vehicles with manipulator arms, tooling skids, cleaning units and measurement tools, flown from a vessel through a long umbilical. They hold station in working currents and do the measuring, cleaning, cutting and turning. Most inspection campaigns live here.

Untethered

AUV

An autonomous vehicle flies a pre-programmed survey route with no tether, carrying multibeam sonar and cameras, and is recovered with its data aboard. Nothing beats it for long cable routes and bathymetry. It cannot stop and look closer — which is why surveys pair it with an ROV for the anomalies it finds.

And divers? Air diving works to about 50 m and saturation diving far deeper, at serious cost and risk. Where a recorded, repeatable survey is the deliverable — or the depth and current say so — the vehicle wins. Where hands genuinely have to feel the work in shallow water, I will tell you to hire divers instead, and say so in the plan.

“We don’t dive today.”

The stop conditions written into every plan I sign. Any one of them ends the discussion:

  • Current at the workface above the vehicle class limit — 1.5 kn is the hard line for fine work
  • Visibility at the target under a metre — pictures that prove nothing are worse than none
  • Vessel dynamic positioning degraded or on a single remaining thruster
  • Lightning within five nautical miles of the launch area
  • Umbilical management compromised — a fouled or over-tensioned tether is recovered first, questions later
  • Any crew member calls it — a stop call needs no rank and no justification on the day
Engagement terms

How the money works

Day-rate basis. Spreads bill per day on station, at the published rate card. Mobilisation is a fixed one-off by transit distance. Report and data delivery are inside the rate — never invoiced afterwards.

Weather standby. Days on station when the weather closes the work bill at 65% of the spread rate. Vessel standby follows its charter terms, which is why the estimator on the home page budgets a weather contingency up front instead of pretending the North Sea is a lake.

Firm prices. The estimator gives planning figures. A firm price follows a written scope — asset, depth, scope of inspection, deliverables — and is fixed for that scope. If the scope changes offshore, the change is agreed on the day, in writing, at the same rates.

Data standard. Positioning, eventing and report formats follow your integrity system’s template where you have one. Raw footage and readings are delivered with the report, not held hostage to it.
