Sheet 03

The engineer

METHOD · SAFETY · TERMS

Dr Adrian Kessler has spent seventeen years around liquid rocket engines: first on turbopump test rigs, learning what cavitation sounds like in data before it announces itself in hardware; then in design offices, building the performance and cycle models that decide what gets built; then — the part that made him a reviewer — sitting in other people’s readiness reviews and watching findings get negotiated away under schedule pressure.

He went independent in 2021 to be the person in the room whose only interest is the finding. The practice takes a small number of engagements a year — reviews, audits, investigations and trade studies — for startups, research groups and suppliers across Europe. He holds no equity in any client and sells no hardware, because a reviewer with a stake in the answer is a salesman with a spreadsheet.

The doctorate is in turbomachinery. The qualification that matters more is the stands: you cannot review a test campaign convincingly if you have never stood behind the glass — at a proper distance, under proper procedure — and watched a start transient go somewhere the sequence did not intend.

What he will not do

Sign off an engine he has not read the data for. Review his own analysis. Take a fee that depends on the review passing. Or describe a class figure as a guarantee: the numbers on Sheet A are what the textbooks say about combinations, and the distance between a textbook band and your engine is precisely the distance this practice is paid to measure.

Method

The working rules

APPLIED TO EVERY ENGAGEMENT
  • R1
    Read before you visit.

    The documents are the engine, on paper. A review that starts at the stand has already missed the findings that live in sequence documents, tolerance stacks and last year’s anomaly reports — Case 01 was found at a desk.

  • R2
    A hot-fire test proves a point, not a life.

    A three-second burn proves ignition, stability and performance at one operating point, on one day, with one article. It does not prove the tenth burn, the hundredth, or the one at the edge of the envelope. Reports from this practice say exactly which of those a campaign has and has not established.

  • R3
    Analysis and test must agree before flight.

    When the model and the stand disagree, one of them is wrong, and flying anyway means choosing not to know which. Case 02 closed a 2% gap to 0.4% before anyone signed — that agreement, documented, is the deliverable.

  • R4
    Numbers are stated as bands.

    Textbook figures are classes, test figures carry uncertainty, and trade-study indices carry their basis with them. A number without its basis is marketing; you will not find one on these sheets.

  • R5
    The review can say no.

    Every engagement letter states it in advance: the finding may be “do not fire yet”, with the reasons in writing. A review that cannot say no is a ceremony, and ceremonies are how paperwork errors reach the stand.

Safety

Safety culture, stated plainly

NOT A SLOGAN — A WORKING CONDITION

Test stands are unforgiving places, and cryogenic propellants add their own account: fluids at −183 °C and −253 °C, oxygen that makes ordinary materials burn with enthusiasm, hydrogen that leaks invisibly and burns nearly so. None of that is exotic knowledge — it is the first chapter of every textbook in the field — but it sets the terms on which this practice works.

Three conditions are not negotiable. Stop-work is real: anyone on a campaign the practice is reviewing can halt a sequence, and the review will treat the halt as data, not embarrassment. Criteria before countdowns: go/no-go criteria — like the temperature criterion in Case 01 — are written down and agreed before the campaign, never negotiated during it. Findings are written: a concern that exists only in a corridor conversation does not exist; if it matters, it goes in the report, where it can be answered in writing too.

The practice reviews analysis, plans and data. It does not direct operations on any stand, and it does not publish procedures: what belongs in a client’s controlled documents stays there. What is published here stays at the level any aerospace textbook prints — fundamentals, class figures, and the discipline of checking.

Terms

Terms of engagement

THE SHORT VERSION, IN WRITING

Scope first. Every engagement begins with a written scope and a fixed or capped price — the rate card is on Sheet 01 — agreed before work starts. If the work uncovers something outside the scope, it is reported and priced separately, never quietly absorbed and never quietly billed.

Independence. One client per engine programme; no equity, no success fees, no introductions taken on commission. If a conflict appears mid-engagement, it is declared the day it appears.

Confidentiality. Client data, drawings and test records stay in the client’s systems wherever possible, are never used in another engagement, and are returned or deleted at close. Case files are published only as anonymised composites — the ones on Sheet 02 included.

Capacity. Two engagements at a time, because reviews done in parallel are reviews done badly. Current booking: Q1 2027, with one December 2026 slot held for the booked thruster campaign.

Bring the paperwork before the propellant.

Reviews are cheapest six weeks before the stand window and most valuable then, too. The enquiry form asks for exactly what a first answer needs.
