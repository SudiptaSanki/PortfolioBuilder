Case files

BRIEF → WORK → OUTCOME

Three engagements, told the way the reports are written: what was asked, what the work actually consisted of, and what changed. Client names are withheld — that is standard in this industry and costs the stories nothing. Figures quoted are the engagements’ own working figures, rounded the way engineers round them in a report.

KP-014

The chill-down finding

Client
Small-launcher startup, DE
Engine
LOX/CH4 first stage
Scope
Test-readiness review, 19 days
Outcome
First firing, first attempt

The brief

A first-stage methalox engine, six weeks from its first hot-fire campaign. The stand window was booked, the investors had been given a date, and the board wanted one thing from an outside reviewer: read everything, and say — in writing — whether this engine was ready to be fired.

What the review did

Read the campaign the way it would be run: the full test plan, the sequence documents and the instrumentation list — 214 documents in all. Most of the review was unglamorous and most of it passed. The finding was in the chill-down sequence. Cryogenic hardware has to be cooled to operating temperature before the start, or the first propellant through the lines flashes to vapour. The plan chilled the engine inlet line before the pump inlet line, and used a fixed elapsed timer as the criterion for “chilled enough” to start. On the plan’s own numbers, that order and that timer could leave two-phase flow — liquid carrying vapour — arriving at the turbopump inlet in the first seconds of the start transient, exactly where a pump is least forgiving of it. The fix cost no hardware: reverse the chill order, replace the timer with a temperature criterion — two readings at the pump inlet, below a stated margin, held for a stated time — and put a hold point in the sequence where a human being has to say the words before the start proceeds.

Outcome

The engine fired on its first attempt and completed its planned burn. The campaign ran eleven firings inside the booked stand window, and the sequence document — with the temperature criterion and the hold point — became the client’s standard template for the campaigns that followed. The review’s fee was a small fraction of one week of stand hire; the finding was in the paperwork the whole time.

KP-017

Two percent, found

Client
Launch-services firm, FR
Engine
LOX/RP-1 upper stage
Scope
Performance investigation
Outcome
Model corrected to 0.4%

The brief

An upper-stage kerolox engine had completed its acceptance firing — and delivered a specific impulse about 2% below the performance model’s prediction. Two percent sounds like a rounding error until it is converted into payload: on an upper stage, it is worth real kilograms to orbit. The client’s question was precise: is the engine underperforming, or is the model wrong? The two answers lead to opposite decisions.

What the investigation did

Worked the discrepancy the way discrepancies are worked: line by line through the model against the test data, checking every term that could carry 2%. Chamber conditions matched. Nozzle behaviour matched. The term that did not match was the injector pressure drop. The model carried the drawing value; the as-built injector, measured on the flow bench, dropped about 18% more pressure than the drawing said. That single term shifted the mixture ratio the chamber actually received away from the value the model assumed — and a shifted mixture ratio costs specific impulse in exactly the direction, and roughly the amount, the test had measured. Rebuilt with the measured pressure drop, the model predicted 336 s against the 335 s the stand had recorded.

Outcome

The engine was accepted at its real performance — it had never been broken; the paperwork had been optimistic. The client re-baselined the mission model on the corrected figures, and the next injector batch’s acceptance test gained the line that would have caught this on day one: flow-bench the injector, and put the measured number in the model. The written report closed the discrepancy rather than explaining it away, which is why it could be signed.

KP-019

The propellant decision

Client
Early-stage startup, ES
Engine
First stage, pre-architecture
Scope
Propellant trade study
Outcome
Methalox selected

The brief

A startup designing a small launcher — 500 kg class to low orbit — with one architectural decision still open and a founding team split down the middle on it: kerolox or methalox for the first stage? The decision locks tank tooling, ground infrastructure and the engine programme for a decade. They did not want an opinion. They wanted the numbers that would make the decision, computed on their vehicle, in a form both halves of the team could check.

What the study did

Ran both combinations through the same stage model at the same stage duty — a Δv of 5.5 km/s — using the class figures from Sheet A. Methane’s higher Isp class (360 s against 330 s) meant the methalox stage needed only 0.84× the propellant mass; kerosene’s higher density (about 1,030 against 830 kg/m³) clawed most of that back in tank volume, leaving the methalox tanks just 1.03× the size. On the whiteboard numbers, it was close to a draw — which is exactly why the deciding evidence was not in the performance table. It was in the business plan: the vehicle only closes its case if the first stage flies again, and kerosene’s coking in cooling passages prices every refurbishment, while methane burns clean. A reusable kerolox stage is not impossible; it is an inspection programme attached to a rocket.

Outcome

The board chose methalox, unanimously, on the study’s deciding line: at this vehicle’s size the performance difference is 3% of tank volume, but the reuse difference is a cost line in every flight’s budget. The study document — assumptions on the first page, arithmetic in the open — is now the reference the engine team designs against. A trade study’s job is not to be right about the future; it is to make the decision auditable after the fact.
