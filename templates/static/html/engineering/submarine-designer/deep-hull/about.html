FR.24 — THE WORK

How the work is done, and what it answers to.

The engineer

Andrew Fell came to pressure hulls the slow way: a marine engineering degree, then nine years on a submarine builder's structures team in Barrow-in-Furness — scantlings, frame design, and the long meetings where a millimetre is argued like a principle — before setting up Deep Hull in 2016 to work for the other side of the table: the operators.

The practice is deliberately one person. The engineer who answers the enquiry is the engineer who crawls the frame bays and signs the report. Barrow is home because the town has built submarines for over a century and still talks about hulls the way other towns talk about weather.

Work is taken for crewed submersibles — research, tourist and inspection — and for the yards and insurers who stand behind them. Naval work is not sought and weapons systems are not touched; the practice is civil, and stays that way.

SCHEMATIC CROSS-SECTION — the arrangement every survey walks: shell plating, the frame ring that stiffens it, the penetrations that interrupt it. Not a vessel.

FR.28 — METHOD

How a pressure hull survey proceeds

01

Paper before steel. Drawings, build records and every previous gauging set are read first. A reading means little until it is compared with the last one — wastage is a rate, not a snapshot.

02

External walk-round. Coating breakdown, mechanical damage, the state of penetrations and closures — recorded photographically before anything is disturbed.

03

Internal, frame by frame. Every frame bay is entered or reached. Lagging comes off where condensation hides; frame toes, welds and doubler edges get the attention, because that is where the water has been sitting.

04

The gauging grid. Ultrasonic thickness readings on a stated grid — 250 mm in suspect bays — each reading logged against frame and position, so the next survey can repeat it exactly.

05

Viewports and penetrations. Acrylic inspected for crazing and creep against the PVHO practice; seats and insert welds examined; every hole in the hull is a designed weakness and is treated as one.

06

The report. Findings, gauging tables and photographs, the calculations where a judgement needed one, and the recommendations in order of consequence. Issued within ten working days of leaving site, with the raw gauging data as a spreadsheet. Photographs and gauging tables are appended, always — a report you cannot re-check is an opinion.

FR.34 — CLASS & RULES

The rules the work answers to

Crewed submersibles are built and kept under classification society rules — the rule sets for submersibles published by the societies — and, for human occupancy, the practice of ASME PVHO-1 (Pressure Vessels for Human Occupancy) governs viewports and the vessels they serve. This practice works to those documents; it does not claim the societies' authority, and class attendance is arranged through the society's own surveyors.

Three depths, kept separate in every report: the operating depth the boat is cleared to, the test depth it is proof-tested to, and the design collapse depth the structure is calculated to survive. The margins between them are set by the rule factor — not by optimism, schedule, or what the boat got away with last season.

The honesty page

In June 2023 the submersible Titan was lost with five people when its carbon-fibre pressure hull failed at depth. Carbon fibre had never been accepted by any classification society for a human-occupied pressure hull, and the vessel operated outside class. Every enquiry since has mentioned it, and it should: the rules this practice works to exist because pressure does not negotiate.

A membrane sum — the explorer on the overview page — is not a collapse calculation. It is the first line of one. Any assessment that stops there will be marked as such in my report, whoever wrote it.

Twice in nine years, a Deep Hull report has recommended do not dive until renewal work was done. Both operators did the work, kept their ratings, and are still clients. That sentence is the practice's whole marketing department.
