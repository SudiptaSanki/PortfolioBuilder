Station 47 · 12°58′S 78°04′W

Maren Okonkwo · analyst & array engineer

I am the person who has to say what the recording means.

Eleven years of moorings, four instrument generations, and a growing pile of evidence that the ocean is getting louder. The work is half mechanical — shackles, batteries, burn-wire releases — and half statistical, because a spectrogram will happily show you a whale that is not there.

From a borrowed recorder to forty-one recoveries

I started in 2014 with a single HTI-99 on a fishing weight, taped to a colleague's mooring in the Bay of Bengal because there was no budget line for acoustics. It recorded for nine days before the battery died, and in those nine days it caught a blue whale call that should not have been in that basin in July. That recording is still the reason I check the calendar against the catalogue before I believe anything.

Now the practice runs arrays of four to sixteen elements, mostly in the 10 Hz to 24 kHz band, with a calibration chain that starts at a pistonphone in a Lisbon workshop and ends at a number in a report. Every sensor that goes into the water is calibrated before and after deployment; if the post-recovery sensitivity has drifted more than 0.5 dB, the deployment is annotated and the affected band is flagged in any analysis that uses it.

A hydrophone does not hear a whale. It hears a pressure change, and the whale is an inference — a very good one, if the calibration holds.

What I actually do on a deployment

  • Site selection from bathymetry, sound-speed casts and the shipping density; the mooring belongs near the channel axis, not wherever the ship can stop.
  • Array geometry — element spacing set by the highest frequency of interest, with the spatial Nyquist written into the plan so nobody is surprised by grating lobes later.
  • Recovery planning: acoustic release, backup timer, and a grapple line rated for the anchor mass. Two of my forty-one moorings have needed the backup.
  • Analysis: detection, classification, manual verification of every automated call in the disputed bands, and a noise budget that separates ships, wind and seismic sources.

What I will not do

I do not report a detection range from a model alone. I do not count a call I cannot see in the raw waveform, even if a detector is confident. And I do not sign a statement that a survey "had no effect" — the honest finding is usually that the effect was measurable at a stated distance, and that the distance is the useful number.

Working with regulators and counsel

Roughly a third of the work ends up in a consent condition, an environmental statement or an expert witness statement. That changes how the data is handled from day one: raw files kept in original format, a chain-of-custody log for every SD card, and analysis code versioned so a result can be reproduced two years later by someone who is not me.

Eleven years, four instruments

  1. 2014 — 2016

    The borrowed recorder

    Nine days of audio in the Bay of Bengal, analysed on a laptop in a hotel room. First blue whale call in the basin; first lesson in checking the calendar.

  2. 2017 — 2019

    Shipping ledger

    Year-long mooring on the Colombo–Singapore lane, matching AIS tracks to narrowband spectral lines. 1,912 vessels attributed to individual noise contributions.

  3. 2020 — 2022

    Canyon and current

    Twelve-element VLF array over a submarine canyon, then the first Southern Ocean winter mooring — 16 months unattended at 4,120 m.

  4. 2023 — now

    Practise-scale arrays

    A purpose-built recorder with on-board detection, four-element arrays as standard, and a calibration chain that survives cross-examination.

Working together

Three deployments a year, and a bench that stays open

If you have a consent condition, a monitoring obligation or a question about what a proposed survey will sound like at 40 km, the earlier I see it the better. Site plans, species lists and a timetable are enough to start.
