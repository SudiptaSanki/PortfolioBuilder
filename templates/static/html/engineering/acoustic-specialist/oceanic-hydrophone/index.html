01 — How sound crosses an ocean

Three numbers decide whether a call arrives

Depth is not a neutral axis. It bends every ray: temperature cools the water until roughly a kilometre down, pressure then compresses it, and the two effects cross at a sound-speed minimum. A call launched near that axis is trapped between the thermocline above and the pressure gradient below, and loses 15 dB per decade of range instead of 20.

Speed, from temperature and pressure

Mackenzie's nine-term equation takes salinity, temperature and depth and returns sound speed to about 0.1 m/s. I keep the cast updated each turnaround: a basin that warms by half a degree moves the axis by tens of metres, and the mooring depth with it.

1,487.0 m/s at the axis, 2023 cast

Loss, from spreading and absorption

Spreading removes 20 dB per decade of range; a trapped ray loses 15. Absorption adds on top and is brutally frequency-dependent: 0.003 dB/km at 20 Hz, 1.6 dB/km at 12 kHz. That contrast is why a blue whale crosses an ocean and a sperm whale click dies inside a canyon.

0.003 → 1.64 dB/km, 20 Hz to 12 kHz

Noise, from weather and traffic

Below 300 Hz the basin is dominated by shipping, and it never stops: I have recorded a 6 dB rise in the 40–80 Hz band across eight years of moorings on the same line. Above 50 kHz, wind and rain take over and sea state sets the floor.
