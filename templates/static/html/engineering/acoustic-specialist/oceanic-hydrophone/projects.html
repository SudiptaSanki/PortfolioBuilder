SL-21 · 2021–2023

The Mawson line

Southern Ocean · 62°14′S 105°E
4,120 m · 16 months unattended

Brief

A seismic survey proposed across a krill ground, and a regulator who wanted to know what the airgun array would sound like where the whales feed, not at the survey boundary. Six elements at 40 m spacing, moored 300 m above the seafloor, on the flank of a ridge that funnels the sound channel past the shelf break.

Method

Continuous 48 kHz recording with a 2 Hz high-pass, duty-cycled to 40 minutes in the hour to fit the power budget. Every element calibrated in Lisbon before shipment and again after recovery; mean drift 0.21 dB, worst 0.4 dB, so no band was annotated. Detections were verified by hand at 10× decimation before counting.

Finding

The survey's 20 Hz energy arrived at the krill ground 41 dB above the pre-survey background, and stayed there for the six days the array was active 220 km away. Blue whale B-call rate dropped from 4.1 to 0.9 calls per hour inside the exposure window and recovered within 36 hours — a correlation I reported as a correlation, with the caveat that a single season cannot separate the survey from a change in prey.

SL-20 · 2020

Canyon shadow

Gulf of Guinea · 03°41′N 06°12′E
2,300 m · 92 days

Brief

Fishermen reported sperm whales inside a submarine canyon that opens onto deep water, and no one could say whether the canyon was a corridor or a trap. A twelve-element vertical line across the canyon axis, three elements on the shelf outside it as a control.

Method

Wideband recording to 96 kHz for click work, with a separate 2 kHz channel for the vessel noise that dominates the canyon mouth. Click trains were localised by time difference of arrival across the vertical line; the geometry is poor for range but good for inclination, which is what the question needed.

Finding

Clicks were detected 3.4 times further inside the canyon than on the adjacent slope at equal source level, consistent with the canyon walls ducting energy downward. The practical consequence is that a monitoring programme placed on the open slope would have underestimated sperm whale presence in the canyon by roughly two-thirds.

SL-19 · 2019

Shipping ledger

Bay of Bengal · 15°02′N 88°40′E
1,040 m · 12 months

Brief

A port authority wanted a baseline before a channel-deepening programme, and a number it could put in a document. One element on the channel axis, one 400 m above it, and an AIS feed covering a 40 km radius.

Method

Narrowband spectra averaged over 10 s windows, then matched to AIS tracks by bearing and arrival time. Only vessels whose predicted cavitation lines fell inside a ±2 Hz band around the observed peak were attributed; everything else went into an "unattributed" residual, which came to 31% of the noise budget.

Finding

1,912 vessels were attributed across the year, with the noisiest 5% contributing 46% of the 40–80 Hz energy. The band rose 6 dB between 2015 and 2023 on the same line, which is faster than the regional fleet growth alone would explain and is now the subject of a follow-up mooring.

SL-18 · 2018

Humpback nursery

Mozambique Channel · 21°08′S 35°30′E
320 m · 5 months

Brief

Whale-watching operators wanted evidence that their approach rules were working, and a marine park wanted a song baseline. Four elements on a shallow bank where the water column is only 320 m deep — the hardest acoustic environment on the list, because every vessel within 30 km arrives at the same level as a singing whale.

Method

Songs were detected with a spectrogram-correlation template and then checked by hand against vessel track logs. Exposure was defined as a vessel within 500 m, and the analysis compared song rate in the 30 minutes before and after each approach.

Finding

Song rate fell 38% during approaches closer than 300 m and recovered within 40 minutes. The park adopted a 300 m stand-off with a 15-minute approach limit. The dataset also caught three cyclones, which is why the mooring frame is now rated for a 2.1 m/s current rather than 1.6.

SL-22 · 2022

Krill soundscape

Antarctic Peninsula · 64°58′S 62°W
640 m · 9 months

Brief

An acoustic baseline for a krill fishery, in a bay where the ice itself is a sound source. The question was whether biological noise could be separated from ice cracking well enough to detect a change in the krill swarm.

Method

Two elements, one under the ice edge and one in open water, recording at 24 kHz. Ice events were classified by their broadband signature and impulsive onset; the residual 2–8 kHz band, which the swarms occupy, was analysed for diel patterns against light level rather than against time.

Finding

The 2–8 kHz band rose by 4–7 dB at dusk and fell at dawn, tracking the vertical migration rather than the fishery. Ice contributed 22% of the total acoustic energy over the deployment but was confined to short, identifiable events that could be masked out — the baseline is now usable, which was the point.

SL-23 · 2023

Loch Erne trial

Sheltered sea loch · 56°10′N 05°30′W
60 m · 11 days

Brief

Not a survey: a test of the in-house ST-3 recorder and a 16-element nested array, in water shallow enough that the array could be recovered by divers and re-deployed the same week. The goal was to prove beamforming and self-noise figures before committing the design to a Southern Ocean winter.

Method

A known source was towed on a measured track while all sixteen channels recorded; beamforming was compared against the GPS track. Self-noise was measured with the array in a weighted, acoustically quiet frame at slack water.

Finding

Bearing error stayed under 1.4° across the track, self-noise came in at 38 dB re 1 µPa²/Hz at 1 kHz — 6 dB better than the design target — and one channel failed its post-recovery calibration by 1.1 dB, which is exactly why the trial was worth running in a loch.
