§2 — The MakingFive stages, no shortcuts recorded

From a seam of clay to a graded tile

A pantile is a simple object made slowly. The stages below are the whole process; anything that fails a stage leaves the process and is crushed back into the next batch as grog.

  1. STAGE 1

    Clay

    The yard works one seam of Oxford clay, dug fourteen miles away and weathered outdoors over winter so frost breaks it down before we touch it. It is then pugged — kneaded under pressure — with 8% grog: our own fired wasters, crushed. Grog is what stops a tile warping and cracking as it dries and fires.

    OXFORD CLAY, ONE SEAM · GROG 8% · WEATHERED ONE WINTER

  2. STAGE 2

    Forming

    Each tile is pressed from a weighed slab of clay and curved over a wooden former by hand, then trimmed to 342 × 241 mm. The nib — the hook that hangs the tile on its batten — is formed in the same pass. Camber is checked against the gauge board as we go, because a pantile that leaves the bench the wrong shape will never sit right on a roof.

    342 × 241 MM · 600–700 TILES A DAY FROM ONE BENCH

  3. STAGE 3

    Drying

    Formed tiles spend three to five weeks on racks in the drying shed, turned twice. This is the stage that cannot be hurried: a tile that enters the kiln still damp in the middle will blow apart as the water turns to steam, and it usually takes its neighbours with it.

    3–5 WEEKS · TURNED TWICE · LOSSES HERE, NOT IN THE KILN

  4. STAGE 4

    Firing

    The production kiln is gas-fired — chosen for control, not romance — and takes a batch of 3,000 tiles to 1,020 °C over about 38 hours, with a two-hour soak at the top and two days of cooling. The iron in the clay does the colouring. Tiles near the burners flash darker, and roughly one tile in eight comes out a tone deeper than its neighbour. That spread is the colour of every old roof in the county. We grade it; we do not pretend it away.

    1,020 °C PEAK · 38 HOURS UP · 48 HOURS COOLING · EVERY FIRING LOGGED

  5. STAGE 5

    Grading

    Every tile is rung with a knuckle as it comes off the kiln furniture. A clear note passes; a dull one means a crack or a lamination, and that tile is crushed for grog. Tiles are sorted into three tones, and one tile in fifty from each batch goes to freeze–thaw testing to BS EN 539-2 — the standard a clay tile has to meet if it is going to survive Lincolnshire winters rather than just Lincolnshire summers.

    RING TEST, EVERY TILE · BS EN 539-2 · 150 CYCLES AT LAST INDEPENDENT TEST, 2024

§3 — MatchingWhy old roofs reject new tiles

Matching an old roof starts with a broken tile

Send us one broken tile from your roof — the largest fragment you have — and we match to it: clay blend, profile, gauge and the spread of colour across the slope. You approve fired samples against your own roof, in daylight, before any order is taken. The match fee is £25, credited in full on orders of 500 tiles or more.

  • 1

    The profile is wrong

    Machine-made pantiles are pressed to a tight, regular curve. On an old roof they sit proud of their hand-made neighbours, rock on their nibs, and let driven rain work underneath.

  • 2

    The gauge does not match

    Old roofs were laid to the tile-maker's own gauge. A modern tile at a different gauge forces a choice between stretched laps that leak and cut courses that look wrong — and that conservation officers refuse.

  • 3

    The colour is flat

    A machine tile is one colour through the whole pallet. An old roof is five tones at once. Flat replacements read as patches for decades; ours are graded into three tones and mixed on the pallet before they leave the yard.
