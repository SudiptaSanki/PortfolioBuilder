The insect is a clock. It runs on temperature, and it does not stop for anyone's alibi

I read blow-fly development and beetle succession to put a window around a death, then defend that window in court. Most of the work is unglamorous: thermometers, rearing cabinets, and a spreadsheet of degree hours. The interesting part is the argument about what the number can carry.

The bench

Accumulated degree hours

Pick the species, the stage on the pathology table, and the mean temperature the body actually sat in. The model divides the stage's degree-hour requirement by the degree hours the scene supplied per day. Below the base temperature, nothing accumulates.

Base temperature 10 °C for C. vicina. Nights under the threshold contribute nothing to development, which is why a cool week stretches an interval far more than most people expect.

This is a single-species, single-strain model with a constant base temperature. Real casework adds a temperature series from the scene, a rearing check on the local population, and a written account of every assumption. An interval produced by a calculator alone is not evidence.

Three clocks

What the insects are actually timing

A body is a sequence of habitats. Each one is colonised on its own schedule, and the entomologist's job is to know which clock is running and which has stopped.

01

Development

The clock that gives a minimum interval. Blow flies arrive within minutes of death in daylight, lay eggs, and the larvae grow at a rate set almost entirely by temperature. Stage the oldest larvae, sum the degree hours, divide. It is the most precise clock and the easiest to overstate.

02

Succession

The clock that gives a maximum. Beetles, mites and moths arrive in waves over weeks and months. When a body has been outside for a season, the arthropod community is the only calendar left, and it is read as a sequence rather than a number.

03

Colonisation

The clock that says whether the body was moved. Species that will not enter a building, species that will not cross a road, and the difference between the fauna on the trunk and the fauna under it, all describe a place as well as a time.

Selected casework

Intervals that held, and one that did not

  1. 2026-041

    A flat above a bakery

    Third-instar C. vicina under a kitchen floor, with a logger reading 26 °C for part of every day. The prosecution wanted four days; the rearing cabinet said the local strain needed 15 % more degree hours than the published table, which moved the interval to six.

    interval extended
  2. 2025-217

    A car in a nature reserve

    Empty puparia and a beetle fauna consistent with six months, not the two the defendant claimed. The succession list did the work here; no single species could carry it.

    succession only
  3. 2024-309

    An interval I withdrew

    I gave a range of 8–11 days, then found the body had been wrapped in a plastic sheet for part of the period, which changed the humidity and the larval growth rate. I withdrew the range in a second report and the court heard both.

    withdrawn

Instruction

If there are larvae on the body, the next hour matters

Collect half into 80 % ethanol and keep half alive in a ventilated container with a little of the tissue they were on. Note the time, the room temperature and where on the body they came from. Everything I do later depends on those three notes being right.
