LOG 06

The scientist

Dr Ruth Calloway, b. 1981
Plymouth, UK

I came to the deep through a freezer. My doctorate was spent sorting benthic samples from other people's cruises — jars of animals from water I had never seen, each label a depth and a date. The work taught me the two habits this practice still runs on: a specimen is only as good as its record, and the ocean does not care what the cruise plan said.

First cruise in 2011, as a watch-standing scientist on a midwater programme. First submersible dive two years later, to 2,900 m, in a sphere the size of a wardrobe with two other people and eight hours of darkness outside the viewport. I have since stood science watches on sixteen cruises, led six as chief scientist, and made forty-one dives in ROV control vans and crewed submersibles — the deepest a working dive to 6,480 m.

Calloway Deep Science exists because expedition science has a staffing gap: ships and vehicles are booked years ahead, but the experienced scientist who can plan the dives, run the sampling, defend the data and tell a funder plainly what went wrong is usually found by word of mouth. This page is the word of mouth, written down.

Training & standing

PhD in marine biology (deep-sea ecology); fifteen years of sea-going research; submersible and ROV science operations on Atlantic, Pacific and Indian Ocean programmes; offshore survival and sea-safety certification current; published across vent ecology, midwater carbon flux and hadal fishes — full publication list supplied with any proposal, because citations are checkable and adjectives are not.

LOG 07

Vehicles & kit I work with

Classes, not brand promises.
The programme charters the vehicle.
Work-class ROV6,000 m class

The default tool. Two manipulator arms, suction sampler, push cores, 4K cameras, 8–12 hour dives flown from the ship. Most survey and sampling work on this site was done by this class.

Crewed submersible (HOV)6,500 m class

The class of Alvin and Shinkai 6500: three people in a pressure sphere, 8–10 hour dives. Booked when human eyes on site change the science — and priced accordingly when they don't need to be.

Baited trap & camera landersFull ocean depth

Free-falling frames that sink, work unattended on the bottom, and return when their acoustic release drops ballast. The only practical way to collect in the hadal zone — and, per Case 02, a system whose releases deserve respect.

CTD rosette & tow-yoFull ocean depth

Conductivity, temperature, depth and chemistry through the whole water column; towed up and down through a hydrothermal plume, it finds vents the way a bloodhound finds a trail — by what the water carries.

Ship multibeam & sonarSurface

The map before the dive. Multibeam bathymetry is drawn first and argued over in the dry lab; no vehicle dives on an unsurveyed target on my watch.

Sampling & chain of custody

Every sample leaves the vehicle with a number, and the number carries depth, time, position, method and permit reference from the seafloor to the archive. Biological material is fixed or frozen on a written protocol within the hour; tissue for genomics goes to −80 °C; the cold chain is logged, and a break in the log is reported, not repaired with handwriting.

Data practice

Cruise data are cleaned, catalogued and delivered as the contracted data package, then deposited in the appropriate public repository when the funder's embargo lifts. Samples collected under permit are accessioned to a museum or institute collection — never to a private shelf. If a client wants the only copy of anything, the engagement does not start.

LOG 08

Safety culture & engagement terms

Stated soberly,
because the deep is.
  • The ocean does not negotiate. Weather windows, vehicle limits and dive aborts are decided by the plan and the conditions — never by the fee, the film schedule or the funder's deadline.
  • Any person can call a stop. On any dive I lead, any member of the watch can halt an operation on safety grounds without needing to win an argument first.
  • Certification before enthusiasm. Nobody works a deck, a winch or a control van on my science party without the certification and the briefing for that station.
  • Incidents are written down the same day. Near-misses included. The report goes to the client unedited — see Case 02 for what that looks like when it costs money.
  • Leave the site as found. Sampling is permitted, minimal and recorded; nothing is taken that the permit does not name.

Engagement terms

Lead time. Ship time is allocated 9–18 months ahead by the operating institutes; science-lead engagements are normally agreed a full season out. Analysis-only commissions (samples already ashore) can start within a month.

Fees. As printed on the Log page — day rates at sea and alongside, per-sample analysis, fixed cruise reports. Mobilisation travel and freight are charged at cost against receipts.

Cancellation. If a cruise is cancelled by the operator or the weather, my sea-day fees fall with the sailing; planning days already worked are invoiced. If a client cancels a booked season inside 90 days, planning fees and a 25% season retainer apply. The vessel's own charter terms are the client's contract with the operator, and they are always the bigger number — read them first.

Insurance & liability. I sail under the operating institute's marine cover as a member of the science party; independent analysis work carries professional indemnity cover, details supplied with proposals.
