Method · channel notes

A forecast is a number with a score attached, or it is a story

Space weather has a public, a deadline and a small set of channels that arrive on different clocks. The discipline is not in predicting the Sun; it is in being specific enough that the prediction can be checked, and honest enough to publish the checks that failed.

01 — Flares

Class from flux, geometry from the magnetogram

The X-ray class is a reading, not a prediction: the GOES 1–8 Å channel gives a peak flux in W/m² and the letter is a decade of that number. A and B are background, C is common, M is interesting, X is the one that makes the news. The class says nothing about the CME that may or may not follow, which is the part everybody actually wants.

For that I use the magnetogram: the free magnetic energy in a region, its complexity, and how fast the polarity inversion line is sheared. Regions that produce X-class flares are almost always complex; complex regions usually do not produce them. That asymmetry is why the false-alarm rate is what it is, and why any claim of a 90 % hit rate without a false-alarm number should be read as a marketing claim.

What the class does not carry

A confined flare with no ejecta can be brighter than an eruptive one. The X-ray class measures the energy released in the corona, not the mass that leaves it, and those two numbers are only loosely coupled.

02 — CMEs

From coronagraph to arrival

A coronagraph gives a speed in the plane of the sky within an hour of the eruption, and that speed is wrong for arrival purposes in two ways: it is a projection, and it ignores the drag of the solar wind. A 2,000 km/s CME measured at the limb may be a 1,200 km/s CME aimed slightly away from us.

The working method is a drag-based model fitted to successive coronagraph frames, with an empirical drag parameter that varies with the solar cycle. It gets the arrival within about six hours for fast events and worse for slow ones, and it fails completely when a preceding CME has cleared the way, which is exactly the case that produces the biggest storms.

  • Projection error: correct with a cone model, and accept that the correction is itself uncertain.
  • Drag: fit to the frames, do not assume a constant deceleration.
  • Interaction: a following CME can be accelerated by the sheath of the one ahead of it.

03 — Loops

Measuring a field you cannot see

The magnetic field in the corona is not directly measurable. It is inferred, from the photospheric magnetogram extrapolated upwards, or from the oscillations of the loops themselves. A loop that is plucked by a flare rings at its kink period, and that period depends on the length and the Alfvén speed: measure the period and the length, and the field strength follows.

The method works and it is fragile. The density is assumed constant along the loop, the length is estimated from an image with a finite resolution, and the oscillation has to be clean enough to fit. When three independent estimates agree within 20 %, as in the 2023 fan-loop set, that is a good day.

04 — Scoring

Publishing the misses

A forecast system is scored three ways: hit rate, false-alarm rate, and the base rate of the event you are forecasting. For M-class flares in a busy year the base rate alone can give a 40 % hit rate with a permanent "yes", which is why every number I publish comes with the base rate beside it.

The 2025 score sheet is public and includes every forecast I issued, including the twelve-hour-early call on the May 2024 event and the two days of false alarms in August. Those entries are the reason the hit rate is 0.71 and not 0.85, and they are the most useful part of the record.

Channels

What the console on the home page leaves out

X

GOES/XRS

One number every second, in two channels. The backbone of every flare catalogue and the only channel with a continuous 40-year record.

M

Magnetograms

HMI on SDO gives the photospheric field every 45 seconds. Complexity metrics derived from it are the best single predictor of flaring.

C

Coronagraphs

LASCO and SoloHI see the ejecta leave, but only from the side. The missing line-of-sight geometry is why arrival times have a six-hour floor.
