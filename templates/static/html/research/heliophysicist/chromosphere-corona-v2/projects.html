PG. 02 Case log

Three entries from the log.

Each case is written the way it was delivered: the brief, the decisions, the outcome — and a fourth entry most portfolios leave out, the limits, stating what the work could not support. Client names are withheld; client types, data and figures are as delivered.

CASE 01 · EVENT RESPONSE

The X-class flare that was not coming here

A bright flare, an anxious operations room, and a finding whose entire value was the word “no” — evidenced.

Client
Earth-observation constellation operator — 38 satellites
Programme
Event response, single event
Data
GOES X-ray flux · SDO imagery · Coronagraph records
Duration
31 hours, first note to signed finding
Deliverable
Signed event finding + frame-by-frame appendix
Status
Completed 2024
THE BRIEF

An X2.3 flare had erupted from a region rotating toward disk centre. The operator’s duty team was preparing to safe-mode the fleet — a day of lost imaging tasking — and wanted a defensible reading before the order went out: was anything on its way?

DECISIONS

The flare’s class was settled in minutes and settled nothing; the work was the eruption question. The associated coronal mass ejection was reconstructed frame by frame from coronagraph imagery. Its bulk propagated roughly 35° south of the Sun–Earth line; the flank that could reach Earth was slow and tenuous. The finding recommended against safe-mode, and stated the confidence, the geometry it rested on, and the single observation — a second viewpoint — that would have strengthened or overturned it.

OUTCOME

The fleet kept its schedule. A weak disturbance arrived two days later and was logged by the operator with no anomalies reported. The finding, frames attached, became the document the operations board used to close the incident.

LIMITS

Single-viewpoint coronagraph geometry carries real uncertainty, and the finding said so on its first page. A direction estimate is not an arrival guarantee in either direction — which is why the recommendation was framed as a judgement with its evidence shown, not as an all-clear.

CASE 02 · CROSS-CALIBRATION CAMPAIGN

Two observatories, two answers

Two respected instruments read the same flares and disagreed. The reconciliation — not a verdict for either side — was the deliverable.

Client
Instrument-team consortium, two observatories
Programme
Cross-calibration campaign
Data
EUV & X-ray records, 14 months of overlapping events
Duration
5 months
Deliverable
Time-dependent correction curve + reconciled catalogue, 212 events
Status
Completed 2023
THE BRIEF

Two instruments’ published flare fluxes disagreed by up to 40% at M class — enough that two literatures were quietly diverging. The consortium wanted one scale both teams would sign, and an honest account of where the gap had come from.

DECISIONS

The comparison was run event by event on jointly observed flares only — no catalogue-level averaging, which hides exactly the structure that matters. Most of the gap traced to one instrument’s ageing-detector correction, applied uniformly where the degradation was not uniform. A time-dependent correction was built, then checked against events neither team had used in fitting it.

OUTCOME

The reconciled catalogue of 212 events was adopted by both teams, with residual disagreement under 8% across the C-to-X range. Both groups published from the same numbers the following season.

LIMITS

A correction curve is a model of an instrument’s history, not a recovery of the photons. The catalogue carries the correction’s uncertainty on every row, and events observed by only one instrument were excluded rather than adjusted into agreement.

CASE 03 · CLIMATOLOGY STUDY

One region, three rotations

A long-lived active region, followed across three solar rotations, counted flare by flare — with the study’s limits printed on its first page, where they belong.

Client
University research group
Programme
Climatology study
Data
81 days of flux & imagery for one active region
Duration
3 months
Deliverable
Study report + complete event table
Status
Completed 2025
THE BRIEF

An unusually persistent active region had survived three rotations of the Sun. Before proposing an observing campaign on its expected return, the group wanted its flare productivity characterised: how often, how bright, and how the rate changed as the region aged.

DECISIONS

Every event at B class and above was counted and classed from the flux record, then cross-checked against imagery to separate the region’s own flares from neighbours rotating into view — the classic way such counts go wrong. Rates were reported per rotation, so the region’s decay stayed visible rather than being averaged away.

OUTCOME

The region produced 46 C-class and 7 M-class events, and no X, with productivity falling by roughly half on each successive rotation. The group wrote its proposal on those numbers — a campaign sized to a decaying region, not to its first-rotation reputation.

LIMITS

One region, in one phase of one solar cycle, describes that region. The study states on its first page that its rates are not a forecasting rule for the region’s return, let alone for any other region — a sentence the client kept, to their credit, in every talk they gave from it.
