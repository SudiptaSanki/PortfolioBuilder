About

I measure how magma behaves when it is too hot to touch and too slow to see

I am a volcanologist at the Nordic Volcanological Field Unit. My work is split between a furnace in Reykjavík, where I melt rock and measure its viscosity, and a ridge in Reykjanes, where I watch the real thing come out of the ground and try to reconcile the two.

How I got here

I grew up in Húsavík, which is not near a volcano but is near enough that the ground shaking is part of the weather. I studied geology at the University of Iceland because I wanted to understand why the ground was hot under my grandmother's garden, and I stayed because the answer turned out to be a conversation between a mantle plume and a spreading ridge that nobody has finished having.

The furnace work started by accident. During my PhD I needed a viscosity for a trachyandesite and could not find a measurement at the right water content, so I built the measurement. A concentric-cylinder viscometer inside a controlled-atmosphere furnace is a fussy instrument: the melt attacks the spindle, the water escapes unless you pressurise the cell, and the whole thing has to be calibrated against a standard glass every week. It has also given me every number I trust.

The two halves of the job

The laboratory half is slow and precise. A viscosity measurement takes a day; a full rheology curve on one composition takes a month; a solubility experiment at pressure takes a season. The field half is fast and approximate. An eruption gives you a window of days, the light is bad, and the radar is 40 km away. The skill is knowing which of your numbers is allowed to be approximate.

I have been lucky with the Reykjanes eruptions: five fissure events since 2021, all accessible, all on a peninsula with a road and a webcam. The 2021 Fagradalsfjall eruption was the first time I could walk to a vent and watch a fountain for a month, and it changed how I teach. Students who have seen 1,200 °C basalt at 20 m understand viscosity in a way that no equation delivers.

Methods

  • Rheology
    Concentric-cylinder viscometry to 1,400 °C in a controlled atmosphere; water-bearing melts in a pressurised cell; calibration against NIST 717a glass.
  • Volatiles
    Fourier-transform infrared on doubly polished wafers for dissolved H2O and CO2; manometry for total volatile content.
  • Textures
    Pycnometry and image analysis for vesicularity; grain-size sieving for fragmentation energy.
  • Columns
    Radar and lidar height, plume trajectory from webcams, and a mass eruption rate from the height scaling.
  • Field
    Thermal camera, gas sensor, and a laser rangefinder that lives in the car and is never where I left it.

What I will not do

I do not forecast eruptions and I do not give dates. I will discuss the monitoring signals and the historical pattern, and I will say clearly that the pattern is not a schedule. I also do not comment on the safety of a specific lava-viewing site; the civil protection authority does that, with better information than I have.

The one thing I will argue about in public is the ash hazard classification for aviation. The current system is conservative in a way that costs airlines a great deal and, in my view, is right to do so; but it is worth saying out loud that the science behind the thresholds is thinner than the confidence of the map.

Current work

A rheology series on the 2024 Reykjanes lavas, and a paper arguing that the crystal cargo in the 2021 flow means the reservoir was recharged twice before the eruption. The second recharge is the interesting one.

Teaching

“Volcanic processes”, second year, and a field course on Reykjanes that runs in February because that is when the weather is worst and the students learn the most.

Reading

Dingwell on melt rheology and Sparks on columns. Both are older than most of my students and neither has been improved on in the way that matters.
