Eruption records

Five eruptions, and the measurement that mattered in each

These are field records, not summaries of the literature: what I measured, what the volcano did, and what I got wrong. The rheology numbers are from my own furnace runs unless the record names another source.

1991 – 2026 11 eruptions attended 1,840 rheology runs
E-01

Fagradalsfjall, 2021

The eruption that changed how the peninsula is monitored. A 200 m fissure opened in Geldingadalir on 19 March after six weeks of earthquake swarms, and for the first weeks the lava produced fountains of 50 to 100 m with almost no ash. Viscosity of the melt at 1,180 °C is 90 Pa·s — fluid enough that the gas escaped quietly and the eruption was effusive.

I was there for eleven weeks. The useful measurement was not the viscosity but the crystallinity: the lava carried 8% crystals of plagioclase and clinopyroxene, which raises the effective viscosity by a factor of three compared with the pure melt. That factor is why the flow fronts were 2 to 5 m thick and not 20, and it is the number I now put in every hazard model for the peninsula.

What I got wrong: the first week I predicted a short eruption. It ran, with pauses, for six months.

E-02

Eyjafjallajökull, 2010

The eruption that stopped European aviation, and the one that taught me why water–magma interaction matters more than the magma itself. The trachyandesite is 58% silica and its melt viscosity at 1,000 °C is about 10⁴ Pa·s, which is not extreme. What made the ash fine and the plume persistent was the meltwater from the ice cap: water flashed to steam, fragmented the magma to a median grain size of 60 µm, and the fine ash stayed aloft for days.

The column reached 9 km, but the hazard came from the ash cloud spreading at 5 km and the regulatory response. I spent that week on the phone explaining the difference between a column height and an ash concentration map, which is a distinction that still gets lost in the news.

What I got wrong: I believed the first day's low ash production would hold. The second phase, with the ice, was ten times worse.

E-03

Grímsvötn, 2011

A basalt eruption under a glacier that produced a 20 km column, which should not happen if you believe the textbook. Basalt has under 1 wt% water, its melt viscosity is around 100 Pa·s, and it normally fountains. Here the ice supplied the water, the vent was constricted by the glacier, and the mass eruption rate was enormous — about 10⁷ kg/s at the peak. High rate plus external water equals a Plinian column from a basaltic magma.

The record matters because it breaks the composition-to-hazard shortcut. Viscosity sets what the magma can do with its own gas; the vent and the external water set what happens next. A hazard assessment that only looks at silica will underestimate a subglacial basalt every time.

What I got wrong: the first radar height was 12 km and I treated it as the maximum. The plume peaked four hours later.

E-04

Hekla, 2000

Hekla is the volcano I respect most, because it gives you forty minutes. The 2000 eruption began with a seismic swarm that lasted less than an hour before the first column, which reached 15 km in the first day. The magma is an andesite at 58% silica with a melt viscosity near 10³ Pa·s and 3 wt% water — enough gas for a sustained column, not enough for the extreme fragmentation of Eyjafjallajökull.

The short fuse is the hazard. Hekla is 40 km from the nearest town and the roads that would be used for evacuation pass within 15 km of the summit. A forty-minute warning is not enough for a full evacuation, which is why the monitoring system on Hekla is denser than the volcano's activity would otherwise justify.

What I got wrong: nothing measurable. I was a PhD student and I was mostly terrified, which is the correct response.

E-05

Pinatubo, 1991

I was not there; I have worked on the deposits and on the rheology of the dacite. It is the reference eruption for this bench because everything about it is at the end of the scale: 65% silica, 5.5 wt% water, a melt viscosity of 10⁶ Pa·s, and a column that reached 35 km and put 5 km³ of magma into the stratosphere in about nine hours.

The climate signal is the part that made it famous: the sulphate aerosol from 20 Mt of SO2 cooled the global mean by about 0.5 °C for two years. The rheology is the part that matters for the eruption: the dacite was viscous enough to retain gas to shallow levels and to fragment efficiently, which is why so much of the thermal energy went into the column rather than into lava.

What I got wrong: my first model of the conduit used a single viscosity and predicted a dome. The deposit says otherwise; the water content and the decompression rate are what decide, not the room-temperature viscosity.
