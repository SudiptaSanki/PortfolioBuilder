01 Rheology

Viscosity is a range, not a value

Basalt at 1,200 °C has a viscosity near 100 Pa·s, which is roughly olive oil. Rhyolite at 850 °C is 10¹⁰ Pa·s or more, which is closer to cold tar, and it stops flowing at all near the glass transition at 10¹². The difference between those two magmas is mostly silica: SiO2 polymerises the melt into chains, and the chains resist shear.

The temperature dependence is not linear, which is why the bench uses a Vogel–Fulcher–Tammann form: log η = A + B/(T − C). As temperature falls toward C, the viscosity runs away. That runaway is why a lava flow can advance for a week and then stop in an hour: the surface crust thickens, the interior cools, and the whole thing crosses the threshold at once.

02 Volatiles

Water is the explosive variable

Water dissolves in magma under pressure, and the solubility rises roughly with the square root of pressure. A rhyolite at 120 MPa can hold about 4 wt% in solution; at the surface it can hold almost none. The difference exsolves as bubbles, and the bubbles expand by a factor of a thousand as the magma decompresses through the conduit.

That expansion is the engine. Basalt at Kīlauea has under 1 wt% water and erupts as fountains and flows; rhyolite at Taupō can have 5 wt% and erupts as a Plinian column or an ignimbrite. The same vent geometry with ten times the gas produces a completely different hazard, which is why the dissolved water is the first number a petrologist measures on a new tephra.

03 Columns

A column is a chimney with a lid

Hot ash and gas leave the vent faster than they can mix with air, so the column rises as a jet until turbulent mixing has entrained enough air to make it buoyant. Above that point it rises as a plume, cooling at about 1 °C per 100 m, until it reaches the level where the atmosphere is the same density. That is the neutral buoyancy level, and it is the height that matters for aviation.

The Morton–Taylor–Wilson scaling gives height as roughly the quarter power of the thermal flux, which is why a factor of ten in eruption rate only doubles the column height. It also means that a small error in the mass eruption rate is a small error in height, and a large error in the volume estimate. That asymmetry is why VEI is defined on volume, not on height.

The Grímsvötn 2011 column is the interesting one for this bench: a basalt with under 1 wt% water, and still 20 km high, because the eruption rate was enormous and the vent was under a glacier. The lesson is that composition sets a tendency, not a fate.
