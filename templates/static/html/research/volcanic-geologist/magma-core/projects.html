Three engagements, written up as delivered

Each file follows the same order my reports do: what was asked, what I decided and why, and what happened. Where a decision went against the client's first hope, it is printed here anyway — that is rather the point of hiring an outsider.

Case 01 — A hazard map that had to move two boundaries

Campi Flegrei is a caldera with a city inside it. Its hazard map dated from the 1990s; the ground, the data and the population had all moved since. The authority asked for a revision it could defend in public.

VolcanoCampi Flegrei, Italy
ClientRegional civil protection authority
Delivered2025
Fee basisFixed — hazard assessment

The brief

The existing map drew its pyroclastic-flow zone from topography alone, on data a generation old. Since it was drawn, the caldera had been through decades of bradyseism — slow ground uplift and subsidence, at times metres of it — and tens of thousands more people lived inside the zones. The authority's question was blunt: which lines on this map can we still defend?

The decisions

I rebuilt the vent-opening probability from thirty years of satellite radar (InSAR) and the geological record of where vents actually opened across the caldera's last 15,000 years — Monte Nuovo, in 1538, being only the most recent. Flow models were re-run over today's topography and building stock. Two boundaries moved: one district came out of the pyroclastic zone, where a ridge the old map ignored channels currents away; one went in, over a vent-opening cluster the old map had smoothed into invisibility.

The outcome

The revised map was adopted without amendment after public meetings in both affected districts — the testimonial on the bulletin page is the client's. Both boundary changes were published with their evidence in full. A line you cannot defend in a public meeting is not a line; it is a rumour with a legend box.

Case 02 — A network built for the wrong volcano

The Reykjanes Peninsula resumed erupting in 2021 after some eight centuries of quiet. Its monitoring network had been designed, patiently and well, for the volcanoes of the previous era. The observatory asked what the new era required.

VolcanoReykjanes Peninsula, Iceland
ClientNational observatory — support contract
Delivered2024
Fee basisFixed — monitoring gap analysis

The brief

Fissure eruptions open along a rift tens of kilometres long, often near roads, a power station and a town. The question was not whether the network was good — it was whether it was good for this: could it find a new fissure fast, size it, and feed a bulletin within the first hour?

The decisions

I audited every station against fissure scenarios walked along the full swarm. Detection of a new opening on the eastern segment rested on two stations; the gas network's scanning plane crossed the prevailing plume path at its edge, so for half the wind year it measured the plume's fringe and called it the flux. The report recommended a phased build — five new seismic stations, two relocated, the gas geometry re-aimed — costed so that phase one fitted a single budget year.

The outcome

Phase one was funded and installed; phase two entered the observatory's own capital plan. In exercises, time-to-locate a new fissure fell from hours to minutes. An audit that ends in a wish list has failed. This one ended in a purchase order.

Case 03 — Whose ash was it?

La Soufrière's April 2021 eruptions covered St Vincent in ash. An insurer holding a portfolio of property claims faced a fair question and a hard one: how much of the grey in the gutters was the volcano's?

VolcanoLa Soufrière, St Vincent
ClientInsurance group
Delivered2022
Fee basisDay rate — expert evidence

The brief

Roof and gutter damage claims arrived from across the island, including districts the ash maps suggested were barely dusted. The dispute had a genuine complication: every summer the Caribbean receives Saharan dust, which is also grey, also collects in gutters, and is nobody's insured event.

The decisions

I built an isopach map — ash thickness, property by property — from satellite imagery, observatory and airport reports, and 140 ground measurements taken before the rains moved the evidence. Where origin was disputed, samples settled it: fresh volcanic ash is mostly volcanic glass; Saharan dust carries a quartz-and-clay signature. Under the microscope, the two do not resemble each other at all.

The outcome

The portfolio was settled on the mapped thickness bands — the honest driver of roof loading and gutter failure — and the report was filed as expert evidence. Its method was not challenged. Some claims grew on the evidence and some shrank; that symmetry is what made the map usable by both sides.
