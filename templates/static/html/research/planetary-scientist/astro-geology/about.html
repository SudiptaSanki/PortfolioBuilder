I date surfaces I will never stand on

Crater counts and reflectance spectra are indirect measurements, and the honest part of the job is stating how indirect they are. Most of my work is calibrating one against the other.

I came to planetary science through geology field camp in Arizona, where the craters are small and the stratigraphy is clear. The first time I counted craters on a lunar image, I was struck by how much of the method is bookkeeping: outline, measure, bin, compare with a production function, and report the uncertainty honestly.

My PhD was on the calibration of crater production functions for the Moon, using the youngest lava flows as a test case. The postdoc at JPL put me on the M³ team, where I learned to reduce hyperspectral cubes and to distrust a single spectrum as much as a single crater count.

The combination is the point. A crater count gives an age with no mineralogy; a spectrum gives mineralogy with no age. Together they can say that a surface is 3.1 billion years old and made of high-calcium pyroxene, which is a statement about a world rather than a pixel.

I still go to analog sites every year. The instrument is heavy, the sun is unreliable, and the ground truth is worth it.

Teaching

Planetary surfaces — graduate course, 14 weeks. Impact cratering, volcanism, aeolian processes and the remote-sensing methods used to tell them apart.

Remote sensing of minerals — a lab course on reflectance spectroscopy, from calibration to continuum removal to a mineral map.

Field analog school — two weeks each summer at a terrestrial analog site, with a spectrometer and a student-led mapping project.

Service

Reviewer for Icarus, Journal of Geophysical Research: Planets and Nature Geoscience. Member of the Lunar Exploration Analysis Group's surface science panel.

I also maintain an open crater-counting tutorial and the calibration code used in the group's papers, because a method nobody can repeat is not a method.
