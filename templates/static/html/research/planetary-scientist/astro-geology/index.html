Planetary geology · crater chronometry · reflectance spectroscopy

A surface is a clock and a mineral map at the same time

Every impact leaves a crater, and the number of craters on a lava flow tells you how long it has been exposed. Every mineral leaves absorption bands in reflected light, and the shape of those bands tells you what the surface is made of. I do both, from orbit and from analog sites on Earth.

01

Two clocks, one surface

Crater counting gives an absolute age if you know the impact flux; spectroscopy gives composition if you know the grain size. Both are indirect, both carry assumptions, and the interesting work is in making them agree.

A lava flow that solidified yesterday has no craters. One that solidified three billion years ago has thousands. The size-frequency distribution of those craters, compared with a calibrated production function, gives an age with an uncertainty that is usually dominated by the calibration rather than the counting.

The method works best on surfaces with a single age and a simple history. It fails on resurfaced terrain, on slopes where craters are removed by mass wasting, and on young surfaces where the statistics are poor. Most of my papers are about those failure modes.

Reflectance spectroscopy is the other half. A pyroxene crystal absorbs at 1 and 2 µm because of iron in its crystal lattice; the exact centre of those bands shifts with calcium content. Olivine has a single broad band at 1.05 µm. Hydrated minerals show a sharp 1.9 µm feature that is almost a fingerprint for water.

Space weathering complicates everything: micrometeorite impacts and solar wind produce nanophase iron that darkens and reddens the spectrum and weakens the bands. Without accounting for it, a weathered surface looks like a different rock.

03

Three places on Earth that behave like elsewhere

Impact and volcanic analogs let us calibrate orbital measurements against ground truth. These three are the ones I return to, with the reason each is useful and the reason it is not a perfect match.

S-01

Lonar Crater, India

A 1.8 km impact crater in Deccan basalt, with a young, well-preserved ejecta blanket. It is the closest terrestrial analog for a small simple crater on Mars, and the basalt gives a spectral match to Martian low-albedo regions.

S-02

Mistastin Lake, Labrador

A 28 km complex crater with a central uplift, formed in anorthosite — the closest match on Earth to the lunar highlands. The anorthosite spectrum is the ground truth for plagioclase-rich regions of the Moon.

Askja, Iceland

A basaltic caldera with active hydrothermal alteration. The hydrated silica and sulfate deposits around the vents are spectral analogs for the altered terrains in Gusev crater and at Mawrth Vallis.

04

Instruments and data

Orbital cameras and spectrometers, a field spectrometer, and a crater-counting pipeline that is deliberately boring so the results can be checked.

01 · imaging

Crater counting

CTX, HiRISE and LROC NAC images, orthorectified and mapped at 1:5,000. Craters are outlined by hand, then re-measured by a second analyst; the disagreement is reported with the count.

12,400 craters · 46 scenes

02 · spectroscopy

Reflectance

CRISM and M³ cubes reduced to atmospherically corrected reflectance, continuum removed, and inverted with a modified Gaussian model. Band depth is measured against a straight-line continuum.

3,800 spectra · 5 instruments

03 · field

Analog calibration

An ASD FieldSpec and a portable camera on nine analog sites. Samples are characterised by XRD and thin section, so the spectral library has mineralogy attached, not just spectra.

9 sites · 640 samples

04 · modelling

Crater and radiative transfer

Pi-group scaling for crater dimensions, and a Hapke radiative-transfer model for spectra. Both are open-source and both are checked against the analog datasets before use.

Python · Hapke · open code
