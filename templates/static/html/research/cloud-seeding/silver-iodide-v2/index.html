Glaciogenic cloud seeding · winter orographic programmes

Seeding is a nudge with an honest error bar.

I am Dr Nathan Cole, an atmospheric scientist in Boise. Water authorities and reservoir operators hire my crew to seed winter clouds over mountain catchments with silver iodide — and then to evaluate, in the open, whether it did anything. The field's own position, printed here because it belongs up front: the effect of a well-run programme is modest, and attributing it is genuinely hard. Reported seasonal increases most often fall between 0 and about 15%, and the argument about where in that range any given programme sits is not settled. I sell careful operations and straight evaluation. I do not sell rain.

01

The Seedability Check

Table 1 is the instrument;
the toggles are the morning's cloud

Every sortie this practice flies — and every one it refuses — is decided against the same five criteria, written down before the season starts. Table 1 prints them exactly as the operations log carries them. Set each criterion the way the morning's evidence reads and the log returns its verdict by the printed rule. Try leaving one unknown: the honest answer is not always go.

Table 1 — Sortie decision criteria, glaciogenic (silver iodide) seeding
RefCriterionWorking standardEvidenceWhy it gates
C1Supercooled liquid waterPresent in the target cloud at seeding levelAircraft icing report, radiometer, soundingLiquid droplets below 0 °C are the raw material. No SLW, nothing for the nuclei to grow on — the commonest no-go of any season.
C2Temperature in the AgI band−5 °C to −25 °C at seeding levelSounding and aircraft probeSilver iodide begins nucleating ice at about −5 °C and works better colder. Warmer, it barely acts; colder, the cloud is usually glaciating on its own.
C3Cloud depth & lifetimeOrographic deck roughly 1 km deep or more, developing or holdingRadar, satellite, pilot reportA thinning, dying cloud wastes agent and bills flight hours for nothing.
C4Targeting winds5–15 m/s at seeding level, direction inside the target sectorForecast and observed winds aloftThe plume has to arrive over the catchment — not the next county, and not an hour after the cloud has gone.
C5Airspace & flight safetyClearance in hand; icing and turbulence within aircraft limitsATC clearance, pilot judgementA hard gate. It is never traded against the other four, and no client's deadline changes it.
The rule, exactly as flownAll five criteria MET → GO. Any one criterion NOT MET → NO-GO. Any criterion UNKNOWN, with none failed → INSUFFICIENT DATA — the sortie waits for evidence, not for optimism.
C1Supercooled liquid water

Is there liquid cloud water below freezing at seeding level this morning?

C2Temperature in the AgI band

Does the sounding put seeding level between −5 °C and −25 °C?

C3Cloud depth & lifetime

Is the deck deep enough, and is it holding or building rather than dying?

C4Targeting winds

Will the plume ride 5–15 m/s winds into the target sector?

C5Airspace & flight safety

Clearance in hand, and weather within the aircraft's limits?

What a season costs

Estimate, not a quote —
the split is the point

A season is priced from the practice's printed rate card — the same figures an engagement letter carries. Two lines deserve attention before the total does: standby, because a crew that only flies on good evidence spends days on the ground at the client's expense, and evaluation, because it is a real line with a real fee, not an afterthought folded into the flying.

Flight hoursaircraft, crew, AgI flares & agent included 40 h
Ground-generator daysmountain-top AgI generators, agent solution included 30 days
Standby dayscrew held ready, criteria not met — billed as printed 20 days

Standby is what honesty costs. On a typical season roughly a third of held days end in a no-go — the cloud was too warm, too thin, or gone. Those days are billed at the standby rate, never quietly converted into flights that should not have flown. A programme that promises you zero standby is promising to seed bad clouds.

Rate card, printed in full: flight hour $4,850 · ground-generator day $420 · standby day $650 · evaluation & season report $38,000 fixed · mobilisation & demobilisation $12,500 fixed. A feasibility & climatology assessment is $14,500 fixed. What moves a price: flight hours and standby days first, catchment distance from base second, the evaluation design third.

Start with the feasibility conversation

Every enquiry gets the same first step: a straight look at whether your catchment's clouds are seedable often enough to justify a programme. Sometimes the answer is no, and the assessment says so at the same fee — that answer, in writing, has saved more than one authority a seven-figure mistake.
