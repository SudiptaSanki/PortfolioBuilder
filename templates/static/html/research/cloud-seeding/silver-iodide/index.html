Operational note 04.1

Eleven winters of putting silver iodide into clouds that were going to snow anyway

Winter orographic seeding is the least glamorous and best-measured form of weather modification. You place generators upwind of a mountain range, wait for a storm with supercooled water, and try to show that the snowpack downstream gained more water than it would have. The trying is the hard part.

Will this plume reach the ridge while the cloud is still cold enough?

Silver iodide works between about −4 °C and −20 °C, best near −10 °C. A ground generator releases it into the boundary layer and the mountain does the lifting. Set the release and the weather; the panel returns what the ridge would see.

A workable release: the plume reaches the ridge with enough active nuclei to matter, and the cloud is cold enough to hold them.

Gaussian plume with Pasquill–Gifford dispersion, uniform wind, no terrain enhancement. Real operations add 30–50 % uncertainty and a lot of local knowledge. Ice nuclei per gram from a fitted curve to published AgI generator tests: 10¹³ at −5 °C rising to 10¹⁵ at −20 °C.

Operations

What a seeding night actually looks like

  1. 16:00

    Forecast and go / no-go

    A storm is worth seeding if it has supercooled liquid water, a westerly flow of at least 6 m/s, and a cloud base below −5 °C. The model runs at 06:00 and 15:00; the second one decides the night.

    Decision
  2. 18:30

    Generator start, upwind line

    Generators G-01 through G-07 start in sequence so the plume does not stack into one narrow ribbon. Each is checked for flame colour and solution flow; a yellow flame with a blue tip is correct.

    Release
  3. 20:00

    Verify the cloud is still liquid

    Radar reflectivity above the ridge says whether the cloud has started to glaciate on its own. If it has, the generators go off; seeding an already-glaciated cloud is expensive theatre.

    Verify
  4. 02:00

    Snow gauge and chemistry check

    Twelve snow gauges and two silver sensors. The silver concentration in fresh snow tells you whether the plume arrived; the gauge tells you whether the cloud gave up more water.

    Measure
  5. 06:00

    Log it, including the miss

    Every night is logged with the forecast, the actual flow, generator hours and gauge totals. Eleven seasons of these logs are the only reason any of the numbers on this page mean anything.

    Record

Programme review

Seeding does not make weather. It nudges a cloud that has already decided to snow.

The honest number across eleven seasons is a median gain of 8.4 % in snow-water equivalent over the target watershed, with a confidence interval that includes 2 %. That is worth the generator fuel and nowhere near enough to end a drought.
