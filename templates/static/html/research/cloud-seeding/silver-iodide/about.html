Method note 02 — operation and evaluation

Why silver iodide, why mountains, and why the statistics are the whole job

Cloud seeding is a small physical effect wrapped in a large evaluation problem. The physics takes a paragraph. The evaluation has taken eleven winters, a randomized design, and a great deal of arguing with people who would rather have a bigger number.

The physics

Lattice match, temperature window,
and the water budget

Silver iodide has a hexagonal crystal lattice that is almost the same size as ice: 4.59 Å against 4.52 Å. An AgI particle at the right temperature is therefore a template, and water vapour deposits onto it as ice rather than waiting for a chance collision. The effect is strongest between −8 °C and −15 °C, present but weaker down to −4 °C, and gone above it.

In winter orographic seeding, the target is a cloud that already contains supercooled liquid water and is being lifted by terrain. The generators add ice nuclei upwind; the mountain lifts the plume into the cloud; the extra crystals grow at the expense of the droplets and fall out over the watershed. Nothing is created: the same water falls earlier over a chosen place, and downwind there is slightly less.

Seeding does not add water to the atmosphere. It moves a fraction of it a few kilometres and a few hours earlier.

One gram of AgI dispersed at −10 °C yields on the order of 10¹³ active nuclei, which sounds enormous until it is divided by the volume of a plume at twelve kilometres. The planner on the front page exists to make that division visible.

Evaluation

Randomised crossover,
and the honest error bar

You cannot tell whether one storm produced more snow because you seeded it. Storms vary by a factor of five in water content, and the snow gauge network has its own error of 10 to 20 %. So the programme runs a randomised crossover: each storm is assigned in advance to be seeded or not, and the analysis compares target-basin snow-water equivalent against a control basin selected by historical regression.

Across eleven seasons and 214 seeded storm periods, the median gain is 8.4 % in snow-water equivalent over the target, with a bootstrap confidence interval of 2.1 % to 14.7 %. The lower bound is the number that matters: a programme that cannot show a positive lower bound is a research project, not an operational one.

The design is not perfect. There is downwind depletion, there is a possibility that the control basin is not truly unaffected, and there is the fact that the person deciding to start generators knows the forecast. That last one is why the randomisation is done by an office that does not talk to the field crew until the period is over.

Rules

Permits, silver mass,
and what we do not do

Seeding in the United States requires a state permit, a documented target area, and reporting of silver mass released. The programme releases about 12 kg of silver per season across 1,840 km², which is roughly the silver in 4,000 teaspoons. Snow chemistry monitoring at the target basin has never shown a concentration above the detection limit of the routine method.

We do not seed convective storms, we do not seed for hail suppression, and we do not claim augmentation in a season where the storm count is below the design threshold. Those are not scientific limits so much as decisions about what a public programme should be willing to say.

Downwind effects are real and are reported. The programme's own analysis suggests a 1 to 3 % reduction in precipitation 30 to 80 km downwind, which is within the noise but is not zero and should not be described as zero.

  • No seeding above −22 °C cloud base: the cloud is usually already glaciated.
  • No seeding when the boundary layer is stable enough that the plume cannot reach terrain.
  • No claims from single seasons, and no claims from any season whose randomisation was broken.
