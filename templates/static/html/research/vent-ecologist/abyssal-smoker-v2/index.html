Vent-field ecology, planned like a dive and logged like one.

Dr Tomás Ribeiro runs hydrothermal-vent survey and sampling programmes for research institutes and oceanographic centres — multibeam first, then dives, then samples you can defend in print. Fourteen seasons on mid-ocean ridges; every cruise on this site is written up the way it actually went, including the ones the weather cut short.

Most cruise plans are written by people who have never watched a dive get cancelled. Mine are written for the weather you will actually get.

Four hundred degrees, two degrees, one centimetre apart.

Vent fluid leaves a black smoker chimney at 350–400 °C and meets bottom water at roughly 2 °C. Almost everything that lives at a vent lives inside that gradient — in the narrow band where hot, chemical-rich fluid mixes with cold seawater. Survey work is therefore measurement work: you map the gradient first, and the animals second, because the animals are where the gradient is.

Nothing down there eats sunlight. The food web runs on chemosynthesis — bacteria oxidising hydrogen sulfide and other reduced chemicals in the vent fluid, and larger animals farming or hosting those bacteria. It is a complete ecosystem with no connection to the surface except the ship above it, which is why the ship's time, and how honestly it is planned, decides what science is possible.

Entry 04 — The community

Who lives there, in plain terms

The register this practice works from. Named examples are the field's textbook cases, given as examples — every ridge has its own cast, and part of a first survey is finding out who is actually there.

Giant tube worm — Riftia pachyptila

Textbook example · East Pacific Rise · Diffuse flow

Grows in thickets where warm water shimmers out of the cracks. It has no mouth and no gut: bacteria living inside it oxidise vent chemicals and share the proceeds. Among the fastest-growing marine invertebrates known — a thicket can establish within a few years of a new flow opening.

Vent shrimp — Rimicaris exoculata

Textbook example · Mid-Atlantic Ridge · At the shimmering boundary

Swarms in thousands at the exact band where vent fluid meets seawater, farming bacteria on its own mouthparts and harvesting them. It has no image-forming eyes; a light-sensitive organ on its back is thought to read the faint glow of the vents themselves.

Vent mussels — Bathymodiolus spp.

Genus-level entry · Most ridge systems · Beds at diffuse flow

Form the beds that structure whole fields — shelter, surface and food for much of the smaller fauna. Their gills host symbiotic bacteria, and a bed's edge on a photograph is often the sharpest map of where the warm water reaches.

Microbial mats — the foundation

Not a species · Everywhere the fluid is · The base of it all

White and yellow veils of bacteria oxidising hydrogen sulfide — the primary production of the entire ecosystem. Everything else at a vent eats the mats, eats what eats the mats, or hosts the bacteria directly. A survey that ignores the mats has missed the point of the place.

Entry 05 — From the cruise log

The resurvey after the eruption

Field K-3, a mussel-bed field on a northern Mid-Atlantic segment at 2,350 m, was surveyed by this practice in 2021. Between that cruise and the 2024 resurvey, the segment erupted. The brief was simple to write and hard to do: go back, re-shoot the same photoquadrats, and say exactly what had changed.

What had changed was the map, not the story. The main bed had shifted roughly 40 metres along the fissure, following the new flow; the old ground was pavement and ash. The full account — decisions, quadrat counts and the monitoring interval we recommended — is in the cruise log.
