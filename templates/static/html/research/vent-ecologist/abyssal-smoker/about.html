About

I began with a camera and a question about where the animals were standing

I am a marine ecologist at the Azores Deep-Sea Ecology Group in Horta. I work on the animals that live on hydrothermal chimneys, mostly by looking at them: frame by frame, on footage from vehicles that cost more per day than my annual budget.

How it started

My first vent dive was in 2010 on Lucky Strike, in a submersible that smelled of diesel and sandwiches. I had been studying intertidal invertebrates and had never seen a place where the animals were arranged by temperature rather than by light. The pilot put the manipulator arm into a mussel bed and the whole mat lifted and reseated itself, and I understood that the bed was not on the rock; it was on a membrane of its own making, in a flow.

The question I took away from that dive is the one I still work on: what sets the boundary? A chimney face has a temperature gradient of about 150 °C across two centimetres, and every species has a different place on that gradient. Something decides where the tube worms stop and the alvinellids start, and it is not only temperature.

The work

Most of my data is imagery. A remotely operated vehicle flies a transect at a fixed altitude and speed, and the frames are mosaicked into a strip; then the strip is annotated, species by species, at a scale of centimetres. A single 400 m transect takes about three weeks to annotate properly. The result is a map of a community that would have been destroyed by the sampling that an older approach would have used to describe it.

The rest is chemistry and fluid flow. I collaborate with geochemists who measure the fluid at the orifice and with modellers who compute the mixing. My contribution is to connect the two: a fluid sample at 350 °C is not a habitat until it is diluted, and the dilution is a property of the chimney's shape as much as of the ocean.

Methods

  • Imaging
    ROV transects at 2 m altitude, 1,280 × 1,024 stills, mosaicked to 1 mm per pixel; annotation in a custom database with a fixed species list.
  • Fluid
    Titanium major samplers and gas-tight samplers at the orifice; temperature logged continuously with a probe on the manipulator.
  • Samples
    Push cores and mussel clumps, fixed in ethanol or frozen at −80 °C for stable isotopes and microbial sequencing.
  • Mixing
    Discrete samples diluted in the lab to build a mixing line, then compared with the model used on the bench above.
  • Monitoring
    Temperature loggers bolted to the chimney for a year, recovered by the next cruise, for the seasonal signal in the flow.

What I will not do

I do not endorse seabed mining, and I will not provide a baseline assessment for a licence application on the basis of a single cruise. A vent field's community changes on a scale of years, and a baseline needs at least three. I am happy to say that in writing to anyone who asks, including a regulator.

I also do not identify animals from a photograph to species level unless the diagnostic feature is visible. Half of the “Riftia” in older video archives is a different siboglinid, and the error propagates into every paper that reuses the archive.

Current question

Whether the mussel bed at Lucky Strike is one population or a mosaic of clones, and whether that matters for how fast it recovers after a chimney collapses. The genetics suggest mosaic; the imagery suggests one continuous mat.

Field kit

Two loggers, a conductivity probe, a spare manipulator wrist, and a notebook that stays in the vehicle. The notebook is the only thing on the list that has never failed.

Reading

Van Dover's The Ecology of Deep-Sea Hydrothermal Vents, and the 2022 review on alvinellid thermal tolerance that argues with it. Both are on the shelf above the microscope.
