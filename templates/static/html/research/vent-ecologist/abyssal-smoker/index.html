Lucky Strike field · 1,685 m

Life here runs on sulphur, in the two metres where the fluid gives up

A black smoker discharges fluid at 350 °C and pH 3 into seawater at 2 °C and pH 8. Nothing survives that fluid. Everything lives in the mixing zone at its edge, where the temperature falls through the range that a tube worm can tolerate and the sulphide is still concentrated enough to be food.

01

Chemosynthesis is a local economy

There is no sunlight at 1,700 m. The primary producers here are bacteria that oxidise hydrogen sulphide, hydrogen or methane and fix carbon dioxide into biomass. The energy budget is small and sharp: about 798 kJ per mole of sulphide oxidised, delivered only where reduced fluid meets oxygenated seawater. A vent field's total production is set by the fluid flux and the sulphide concentration, not by the area of the seafloor.

The animals are a mix of strategies. Riftia pachyptila has no mouth and no gut; it houses the bacteria in a specialised organ and passes them sulphide and oxygen bound to haemoglobin that carries both. Bathymodiolus azoricus is a mussel that keeps the same bacteria in its gills and also filter-feeds. Rimicaris exoculata is a shrimp that farms them on its own mouthparts. Each is a different answer to the same problem: get the bacteria to the interface, and keep them there.

02

The habitat is a few centimetres thick

Thermal gradient at the chimney wall

On a black smoker, the temperature goes from 350 °C inside the chimney to 2 °C a few centimetres away, through the boundary layer where the fluid is sheared and diluted. Alvinella pompejana builds tubes in that layer and tolerates the largest gradient of any animal measured, with the tube base near 80 °C and the plume end near 20 °C. Whether the worm itself experiences those temperatures or sits in a cooler micro-layer is still argued about, and the answer changes what “thermophile” means for an animal.

For a survey, the practical consequence is that a vent field cannot be mapped at the scale of a chimney. The community map is a map at the scale of ten centimetres, and the instrument that resolves it is a camera on a frame, not a sonar on a ship.

03

Why the fields are where they are

The Mid-Atlantic Ridge south of the Azores is a slow-spreading ridge, 2 to 2.5 cm a year. Slow ridges produce fewer vent fields than fast ones, but the fields are larger and longer-lived, because the crust is thicker and the hydrothermal circulation reaches deeper. Lucky Strike sits on a seamount at the ridge axis; Rainbow is on an ultramafic massif off-axis, and its fluids are hotter, more acidic and richer in methane and hydrogen because the water is reacting with mantle rock rather than basalt.

That difference is why the two fields have different fauna. Rainbow's fluid chemistry supports a community dominated by shrimp and by methane-based production; Lucky Strike's basalt-hosted fluids support dense mussel beds. Same ridge, same depth range, different chemistry, different ecosystem.
