About — the physicist

Trained on collisions.
Employed by the arithmetic.

Dr Sofia Lindgren — PhD in particle physics, Uppsala University, 2011. Eight years inside large collider research programmes — trigger operations, simulation production, two analysis coordination roles — then independent since 2019, doing the same work for groups that need it for ten weeks rather than ten years. Based in Uppsala; on-site across Europe when the run schedule demands it.

The story, briefly

From the control room to the consultancy

The doctorate was on trigger efficiency measurement — the unglamorous question of how you know what your detector threw away. It set the pattern for everything since: the physics is in the counting, and the counting is only as good as your knowledge of what you discarded. Postdoctoral years split between simulation production — generating the synthetic collisions every measurement is compared against — and analysis work on Run 1 and Run 2 datasets at 7, 8 and 13 TeV.

By 2019 the pattern was clear: the groups with the hardest problems were rarely short of physicists. They were short of one specific pair of hands for one specific quarter — a menu review before a run, a simulation campaign before a deadline, an outsider to write the discrepancy report nobody inside could write without it becoming politics. That is the practice I built. It stays small on purpose: two engagements at a time, each with a written scope and a written ending.

The facilities and experiments whose public data, documentation and open results this work draws on are the field's shared infrastructure — referenced here as context, the way any practitioner would. I claim no post, membership or affiliation with any of them; my clients are the groups and institutes named by type in the case files, under their own terms.

The honesty page

Most of the job is software and statistics

Popular accounts put the physicist at a control-room console watching collisions. The truth is more useful to a client: a working week is code review, batch queues that failed at 3 a.m., generator tunes, likelihood fits and meetings about systematic uncertainties. The detector time is real but rare; the statistics are constant. Engagements are priced and scheduled for the job as it actually is — which is why simulation and preservation, the two least glamorous services on the register, are the two most booked.

Two more honesties. Open data: this field publishes — collision data from the LHC experiments is released publicly through the CERN Open Data Portal, and a good part of my methods work is done on those public samples, where any client can check my arithmetic. And blind analysis: where a measurement demands it, the signal region stays hidden until the method is frozen. I will not unblind early because a deadline is loud, and I say so in the scope, before anyone has paid for anything.

The method, in plain words
Eventone crossing, read out

Each time bunches of protons cross inside a detector, the electronics record one snapshot — the tracks and deposits on the home page. An event is the atom of the field: everything else is counting them.

Triggerthe bouncer

Crossings arrive at 40 MHz; storage accepts roughly a kilohertz. The trigger is the two-stage filter — fast hardware, then software — that decides in real time which events exist for ever after. A wrong trigger decision is unrecoverable, which is why menus are budgets and deserve audits.

Cross-sectionσ · measured in barns

The effective target size of a process: how likely it is to happen. Rare processes have femtobarn cross-sections; the total proton–proton cross-section is some 10¹⁴ times larger. Nearly every argument in the field is, underneath, an argument about a cross-section.

Luminosityℒ · the exposure

How many collisions a machine delivered, integrated over time. Events = cross-section × luminosity — the equation on the home page, and the first line of every plan I write.

Simulationthe synthetic control

Events generated by computer, from the physics model down through a virtual detector, so data can be compared against what a theory predicts. When simulation and data disagree, one of them is teaching you something — Case 02 is about finding out which.

Engagement terms

How work is taken on

  • Written scope before work. Every engagement starts with a scope naming the question, the dataset, the deliverable and the acceptance test. If it isn't in the scope, it isn't quietly billed.
  • Two engagements at a time. The register shows live status; when both slots are full, the honest answer is a start date, not a queue position.
  • Authorship follows contribution. I do not ghost-write results, and I do not take authorship on work I only reviewed. Credit is agreed in the scope, in the field's own conventions.
  • Confidentiality. Client names, unpublished data and internal documents stay inside the engagement. Case files here are anonymised with the clients' knowledge.
  • Open by default. Where a client permits, code and methods from an engagement are released openly — the field's data is public, and its tooling should trend the same way.
  • Compute at cost. Grid or cloud usage is passed through with accounting attached, never marked up. Rates are on the home page, in print, before any call.
