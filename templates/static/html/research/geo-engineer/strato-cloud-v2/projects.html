Case Study 01 · 2024Published in full · Recommendation: do not proceed

The assessment that said not yet

The brief

A northern-European research council was preparing to fund a small outdoor stratospheric aerosol experiment — a balloon-borne release at research scale — and asked this practice a narrow question: would the experiment, as designed, produce knowledge worth its risks and its precedent? The council wanted an answer it could publish either way.

What was done

Nine months of evidence assessment. Two hundred and fourteen papers screened against a protocol published before the screening began; the detection-and-attribution literature read against the experiment's stated measurement plan; two workshops, one with the proposing team and one deliberately without them. The central finding was technical, not rhetorical: at the proposed scale, the expected signal sat below what the planned instruments could distinguish from natural stratospheric variability — the experiment would have flown, and learned little. The governance review found the second gap: no framework existed for consent, liability or stopping rules, because none had been built.

Decisions taken

The report recommended against proceeding at that stage, and said what would have to change: measurement capability first, a governance framework second, and only then a fresh look at outdoor work. It named the conditions under which its own recommendation would expire.

Outcome

The council paused the outdoor line and redirected the funds to the measurement capability the report had identified as the binding constraint. The report — including the recommendation the proposers disagreed with — was published in full. It remains the document this practice is most often asked about, and the one it is proudest of: an assessment that can only say yes is a marketing document with references.

Case Study 02 · 2023Published · Analogue study, limits attached

Pinatubo, re-run honestly

The brief

When Mount Pinatubo erupted in June 1991, it placed a sulfate aerosol layer in the stratosphere that measurably cooled the planet — peak global-mean forcing is assessed at roughly −3 to −4 W m⁻², with global surface cooling of about half a degree over the following two years. It is the best natural analogue the field has. A university consortium asked: how well do current models reproduce it when they are all given the same eruption?

What was done

Four models, one shared protocol: the same prescribed aerosol evolution, the same diagnostics, the same comparison against the observational record. Three of the four bracketed the observed forcing range. The fourth ran warm — its forcing too weak — and the audit traced the divergence to its aerosol microphysics: the particle size distribution it grew from the same sulfur burden was shifted small, and small particles fall out faster and scatter less per unit mass.

Decisions taken

The divergent model was named in the report, with its configuration, rather than quietly dropped from the ensemble — an ensemble that hides its outliers manufactures confidence. The consortium adopted the shared protocol as its standing benchmark.

Limits, as printed in the reportAn analogue constrains; it does not certify. Pinatubo was one eruption, in one season, at one latitude, of one composition. A model that reproduces it has passed one examination, on one paper. Nothing in this study licenses a claim about a sustained, managed layer — different duration, different size distribution, different everything that matters.

Case Study 03 · 2025Published · The disagreement was the deliverable

Where the models disagreed

The brief

An academy's standing committee needed an uncertainty statement it could defend in public: if the stratospheric aerosol literature says a layer of a given size would produce a given forcing, how wide is the honest spread around that number — and what drives it? The committee did not want a consensus figure. It wanted the map of the disagreement.

What was done

Six models ran a prescribed-layer intercomparison: identical optical depth, identical diagnostics, each model's own microphysics left as its authors built it. The global-mean forcing spread came out at roughly a factor of two between the highest and lowest model. Rather than averaging that away, the study decomposed it: assumed particle size distribution accounted for the largest share of the spread, injection height the second largest, and the treatment of coagulation most of the rest.

Decisions taken

The deliverable was the decomposition itself — a published statement of which assumptions a reader must interrogate before believing any single number in this field, including numbers from this practice's own teaching model on page 01.

Outcome

The academy adopted the decomposition as its reference uncertainty statement, and two of the six modelling groups revised their default size distributions in its light. A factor-of-two spread, published and explained, is worth more to a decision-maker than a point estimate with the spread swept under it — the committee's words, in its foreword, not this practice's.
