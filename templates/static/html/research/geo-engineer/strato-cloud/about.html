Method · sheet 03-B

You cannot model what you have not counted

Most of my work is measurement of a stratosphere nobody has modified: the background sulphate layer, volcanic injections, and the particle size distribution that decides how much light a given mass of sulphur actually scatters. The modelling follows from the counting, not the other way round.

01 — Instruments

Three ways to weigh a haze

Optical particle counter

A small, cheap, awkward instrument flown on a rubber balloon to 24–26 km. It draws air through a laser and sizes individual particles by the light they scatter, giving a size distribution between 0.15 and 3 µm. Two counters flying together is the only honest way to use one: the disagreement between them is your uncertainty.

Filter sampler

Air pulled through a membrane for a known volume, then leached and analysed for sulphate by ion chromatography. It is the ground truth for mass, and it is slow. The counter gives the shape of the distribution, the filter gives the mass, and neither is much use without the other.

Lidar

Backscatter at 355 and 532 nm from the ground, which gives the vertical structure continuously and the size only indirectly. A lidar sees a layer arrive; a balloon says what is in it. The campaign in 2024 was an attempt to make the two agree within a defensible error bar, and it took a month to get from 30 % to 8 %.

02 — The model

What the box model does, and what it hides

The bench on the home page solves a steady state: sulphur in equals sulphur out, with two removal pathways. Sedimentation follows Stokes' law with a Cunningham slip correction, which matters at these sizes because the particles are close to the mean free path of the air. The Brewer–Dobson circulation is a single exchange time of 1.2 years. The shorter of the two timescales dominates, and at typical sizes the circulation wins.

Optical depth comes from mass, density and radius with the usual 3M/2ρrA scaling, and forcing from a fixed efficiency of about 25 W/m² per unit optical depth. Temperature follows from a climate sensitivity parameter of 0.8 K per W/m², which is a rough transient number and not an equilibrium one.

  • No coagulation: small particles are removed too slowly and scatter too well.
  • No ozone chemistry: the heterogeneous reactions on sulphate are the largest known side effect.
  • No stratospheric heating: absorbing infra-red changes the circulation that the model assumes fixed.
  • No regional pattern: a global mean forcing says nothing about the monsoon.

Those four omissions are why the model output is presented as a range in the papers and as a single number here, with the caveats printed under it. A benchmark is allowed to be simpler than a study as long as it says so.

03 — Governance

Where the science stops

I am often asked whether solar radiation management is a good idea. That is not an empirical question and I do not answer it as one. What I can say is that the technical literature now supports three claims: the forcing is real and cheap, the side effects are real and unevenly distributed, and stopping abruptly is worse than not starting. Everything else is a decision about who gets to run the experiment and who bears the risk.

Since 2022 there has been a non-use agreement in circulation among researchers, and the London Protocol was amended to cover marine geoengineering, which leaves atmospheric intervention in a legal gap that several states are quietly filling with national rules. My contribution has been descriptive: mapping which instrument would be needed to verify compliance if a programme ever existed, because a measurement network built after the fact is a measurement network built too late.

I do not take funding from parties with a commercial interest in deployment, and I do not sit on advisory boards for field experiments that release material. That position costs me work occasionally. It also means the numbers on this site are the only thing I am selling.
