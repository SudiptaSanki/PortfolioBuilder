LOG 06 · The station

The forecaster, and the rules she works under.

Dr Rachel Donovan took her PhD in atmospheric science at the University of Oklahoma, in the town the National Weather Center calls home, and spent fourteen field seasons with university mobile-radar teams — close enough to supercells to measure them, far enough to come home. The doctorate was in radar meteorology; the education was in what a rotating storm does to a plan.

Supercell Radar, founded in 2016, is the commercial half of that training: the same reading of the same data, sold to people whose decisions can't wait for the evening news. Festival safety officers, claims teams, county emergency managers, growers. She is a National Weather Service–trained storm surveyor and a SKYWARN spotter trainer, and she still teaches the winter courses herself.

The practice has one house rule, printed here because clients quote it back: a forecast is a probability, not a promise — and anyone who sells you certainty about a thunderstorm is selling you something else.

LOG 07 · Instruments & limits

What the station reads — and what it cannot see.

Every briefing names its sources. Every source below is listed with the limit that matters, because a client who knows what the tools can't do makes better decisions at 20:47 than one who was told the radar sees everything.

The instruments

  • NEXRAD (WSR-88D) network — the national Doppler radar mosaic: reflectivity, velocity, and dual-polarisation products that can tell rain from hail from debris.
  • GOES-East satellite — visible and infrared imagery at one-minute cadence in storm mode: the view that shows a storm being born.
  • Surface observations — ASOS stations and the Oklahoma Mesonet's 120 sites: the ground truth the radar is checked against.
  • Guidance — Storm Prediction Center outlooks and the HRRR model, refreshed hourly: where the day is expected to go, before it goes there.
  • The log — every briefing, call and trigger decision timestamped and kept. It is the deliverable nobody asks for and everybody eventually needs.

Field rules — surveys and fieldwork

  • Downed lines are live until the utility says otherwise. Survey routes are planned around them, not through them.
  • Nobody enters a damaged structure alone, and nobody enters one that is still moving, creaking or shedding.
  • Never stop under an overpass. It is not shelter; wind accelerates through it and it blocks the road for everyone behind you.
  • When thunder roars, go indoors. Fieldwork stops at first thunder and resumes thirty minutes after the last.
  • Research driving is not chasing. Escape routes are chosen before position, and position is abandoned before the route is.

The limits, in print

  • The radar beam climbs. Earth curves; the beam doesn't. A hundred kilometres out, its centre is roughly two kilometres up — low-level rotation can hide underneath it.
  • Reflectivity is not rainfall. dBZ says how much came back, not what reached the ground. Hail inflates it; the reader on the home page exists because of this.
  • Models are guidance, not scripture. The HRRR is wrong about individual storms regularly. It is used for the day's shape, never for a single address.
  • A warning is a decision under uncertainty. Some warned storms will not produce; some unwarned minutes will. The practice reports its own busts in the same document as its wins — see Case 03.

How warnings are worded here

Plain words first, numbers second. Every alert this practice issues answers four questions in order: what is coming, where, when, and what to do. Ratings are never inflated to be believed — an EF scale is a damage finding, and calling a storm “the big one” is not a forecast product. If a client needs the crowd calm, the truthful version is the calm version: specific beats dramatic, every time.

Engagement terms

  • Deposit — 30% holds a date; the balance is due on delivery of the final briefing log or survey report.
  • Cancellation — full deposit back at 30+ days; half at 7–29 days. Weather itself cancelling your event is not a penalty event: one reschedule is carried free.
  • Deliverables — written briefings, a timestamped decision log, and for surveys a signed report with the graded damage map.
  • Data — survey data belongs to the client; an anonymised copy enters the research archive unless the client opts out in writing.
