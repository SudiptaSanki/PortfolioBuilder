Arguments about quantum mechanics are settled by fringes, not by arguments.

I am Iris Kovač, a physicist at the Jožef Stefan Institute. I have spent fifteen years building interferometers that are slightly better than the last one, because that is how the questions get answered.

This page is about how the group works: who is here, what we can take on, and what we refuse.

How I got here

My first experiment was a Michelson interferometer built from a laser pointer and two microscope slides, in a basement in Ljubljana in 2006. It did not work, and the reason it did not work — thermal drift, vibration, a mirror glued with the wrong epoxy — is the reason I still do this. Interferometry is a discipline of removing excuses.

I did my doctorate in Vienna on single-photon sources, then three years in Delft on a circuit-QED project that taught me what I did not want to do: manage a cleanroom. In 2016 I came back to Ljubljana with a starting grant and one bench. The second bench arrived in 2021, paid for by a detector grant.

The people

Six of us. Two doctoral students — Meta on the phonon-bath experiment, Tunde on the which-path detector. One postdoc, Luca, who joined from a fibre-optics group and rebuilt our phase lock in four months. One technician, Bojan, who has kept the Ti:sapphire laser alive since 2017. One master’s student each year, usually from the Ljubljana physics programme. And me.

That is the whole group. We do not have a cleanroom, a nanofabrication line, or a theory student. When an experiment needs any of those, we collaborate and we say so in the paper.

Openings

One doctoral position funded from October 2027. Two master’s projects every year, usually advertised in March.

The benches

Bench A is a fibre Mach–Zehnder with a 4 m path imbalance, a piezo stretcher and two superconducting nanowire detectors at 810 nm. Bench B is a free-space double slit with a movable mask and a single-photon camera, used for teaching and for the which-path calibration.

Both sit on a floating slab in a room held at 21 ± 0.2 °C. The temperature control matters more than the optics: a 0.5 °C drift moves the fringe by a quarter period over an eleven-minute run, which is exactly the kind of systematic that ruins a result quietly.

Detectors

Two SNSPDs, 78% efficiency at 810 nm, 40 Hz dark counts, 45 ps jitter. The jitter sets the visibility floor at 99.1%.

What I teach

Quantum optics II in the winter semester, which is mostly about what a photon is not, and a laboratory course in the spring where students build a Hanbury Brown–Twiss interferometer from parts and then have to explain why their g²(0) is not zero. The answer is usually that they are measuring the detector.

I do not supervise more than three students at a time, and I do not take a student who has not spent a day on the bench before asking. Both rules are selfish: a student I cannot see every week is a student I am failing slowly.

Course

Lecture notes are public. The problem sets are not, because they are reused and the answers are on the internet within a day anyway.

What we take on, and what we refuse

We take one external collaboration at a time, usually a metrology group that needs a calibrated single-photon source or a company that needs an honest visibility measurement. We refuse work that requires a claim we cannot support with a fringe, and we refuse to put our name on a result that has not been reproduced on both benches.

The funding is a mixture of a national programme, one European grant and the detector grant. That is enough for six people and two benches, and not enough for a third bench. I have decided not to grow, twice, and I would decide it again.

Rule

If a result needs a model to be interesting, we publish the model and the data separately so a reader can disagree with one and keep the other.
