Why the hold is stiff

A diamagnet levitating over a magnet is unstable: Earnshaw's theorem says a static arrangement of charges or magnets cannot rest in equilibrium, and every diamagnetic demo you have seen is stabilised by something else — a gradient, a feedback coil, a rail. A pinned superconductor is different. Flux that entered the film while it was cooled below Tc is trapped at defects, and moving the puck means bending those flux lines. Bending costs energy in every direction, so the puck sits in a three-dimensional potential well.

That is why the bench reports a stiffness and a resonance rather than just a force. A puck on a 0.35 kg load at a 2 mm gap with a 3 mm decay length oscillates vertically at about 9 Hz. You can hear it if you tap the table: a dry, high knock, not the slow wobble of a diamagnetic float.

Note 1

Φ0 = 2.068 × 10⁻¹⁵ Wb. A 60 mT field through a 1 cm² film holds about 2.9 × 10⁵ vortices, each one a candidate pinning site.

The two fields that matter

Hc1 is the field at which the first vortex is allowed in. Below it the film expels everything — the Meissner state — and the lift is purely from screening currents. Above it the film enters the mixed state: vortices thread the film in a lattice, each carrying one flux quantum, and the film stays superconducting as long as the vortices are pinned and the current is below Jc.

Hc2 for YBCO along the c-axis is above 100 T at low temperature, which is why a 300 mT bench feels nothing like the limit. The practical limit is Jc: push current through the film and the Lorentz force on the vortices exceeds the pinning force, the lattice starts to flow, and the hold collapses. In the model that appears as the field limit, not the temperature one.

Note 2

At 77 K the measured Hc1 of our films is 22 to 31 mT depending on oxygen content, well below the 60 mT we run at. The mixed state is the working state.

What actually kills a film

Not the deposition. The films come out of the chamber at Tc 91 K routinely. What kills them is oxygen stoichiometry drifting during cool-down, and the contact. A 200 nm silver pad sputtered in situ gives a contact resistance under 1 µΩ cm²; a pad put on afterwards, after the film has seen air, gives ten times that, and every four-point measurement then includes the contact.

The second killer is twin boundaries. They pin well, which is good, until the current density is high enough that the vortices hop between twin families and the angular dependence of Jc develops a memory of how the film was grown. Half of our measurement time goes into angular sweeps that exist only to prove a film is not doing that.

Note 3

δ in YBa2Cu3O7−δ: 6.85 to 6.95 is the window. At 6.80 the film is still tetragonal and insulating, and the run is scrap.

Cryogenics is the schedule

The cryostat holds 63 L of liquid helium and boils off 1.5 L an hour with the stage cold, so a fill buys 41 hours and a measurement campaign is planned backwards from that. A full angular sweep of Jc at three temperatures takes 11 hours. A levitation run with a load sweep takes 4. If the transfer line ices, the fill takes an extra hour and the campaign loses a sample.

Recovering the boil-off matters more than it sounds. Our recovery line returns about 70% of the helium, which is the difference between a sustainable lab and one that spends its budget on cryogen.

Note 4

4.2 K is 1 atmosphere of helium. Below 2.17 K the film sits in superfluid, and the thermal link changes completely.
