Specimen records

Six records, including the two that failed

Each record is the short version of the lab file: what was grown, how it was measured, and what it taught. Numbers are as measured at 77 K unless the record says otherwise. The failures are here on purpose; they are the records other groups ask for.

R-01

BaZrO3 nanocolumns at 6 vol%

The growth that produced the bay record. Columns were grown by adding 6 vol% BaZrO3 to the target and holding the substrate at 780 °C; below 770 °C the columns do not run through the film, and above 790 °C they coarsen into islands that no longer match the vortex core radius.

Jc at 77 K and self-field reached 3.4 MA/cm², and — more usefully — stayed above 0.6 MA/cm² at 3 T, where the undoped control had already fallen to 0.04. The angular sweep shows the expected peak when the field lies along the columns, but only 1.3× rather than the 4× reported for aligned films. Our columns are not perfectly straight, and the record says so.

R-02

Levitation puck for a museum demonstrator

A 60 mm puck, 8 mm thick, field-cooled over a NdFeB stack at 77 K. It carries 1.4 kg at a 1.8 mm gap and rings at 8.6 Hz when tapped. The measurement that mattered for the museum was not the load but the decay: after 400 field-cool cycles with a school group handling it, the trapped field had fallen 12%, mostly from a corner chip.

The puck now sits under a bell jar in the science gallery at the Jawaharlal Nehru Planetarium. The label says what it is; the visitors mostly ask whether it is magnetic. It is not — that is the point, and the difference between a magnet and a pinned superconductor is exactly what the bench on the home page lets you see.

R-03

An 18 nm film, for someone else's detector

The thinnest film we have made that still superconducts at 77 K. A transition-edge sensor group in Grenoble needed a low-current film with a sharp transition, which is the opposite of what we usually optimise: they wanted weak pinning, and they wanted the transition width under 0.4 K.

At 18 nm the film is barely two unit cells of strain accommodation away from the substrate, and Tc drops to 84.2 K. The transition width came out at 0.31 K, which satisfied them. The useful lesson was that our standard 45-minute anneal over-oxidises a film this thin; 20 minutes at 500 °C was enough, and the oxygen content is now measured by X-ray rather than assumed.

R-04

The angular anomaly that was not a mistake

For six weeks a batch of films showed a Jc peak at 45° to the columns, which makes no sense for columnar pinning. The obvious explanation was a measurement artefact: a misaligned sample holder. It was not. The films had been grown on a mis-cut substrate, and the columns had inherited the off-axis orientation, so the field was meeting them at an angle the recipe did not intend.

The record is here because the resolution changed how we mount samples: the holder now has a flat and a pin, and every mounting is photographed. The measured angular dependence is in the 2024 paper, along with a warning that a 45° peak in this system probably means your substrate, not your physics.

R-05

Four specimens lost to a contact

Four films in a row measured 40% low on Jc with an odd curvature in the voltage–current curve. The films were fine. The silver pads had been sputtered after the chamber had been opened for a different job, and the interface had a 12 µΩ cm² contact resistance against our usual 0.8. Every four-point measurement was carrying the contact in series, and the 1 µV/cm criterion was being crossed early.

The fix is boring: the silver is now deposited in the same pump-down as the film, always, with no exceptions for schedule. The record exists so that the exception does not get made again. It is also the reason the levitation bench reports Jc as a modelled quantity with a stated field dependence rather than as a single number: single numbers hide this kind of error.

R-06

The oxygen window, mapped

Twenty-two films, one anneal temperature each, from 450 to 600 °C at 1 bar oxygen. The window where δ lands between 6.85 and 6.95 is 24 °C wide, and its centre moves with film thickness: 535 °C for 300 nm, 505 °C for 100 nm. Outside the window Tc falls by more than 10 K, and on the low-oxygen side the film turns tetragonal and insulating.

This record is the one most often requested by other labs, because the annealing recipe in the literature is usually quoted for a bulk ceramic and does not transfer to a thin film on sapphire. The full table is in the appendix of the 2023 thesis, which is available on request and will be a PDF as long as the university keeps the server up.
