What I actually do

I grow thin films of YBa2Cu3O7−δ by pulsed laser deposition on sapphire and lanthanum aluminate, then measure the four things that decide whether a film is any good: the transition temperature, the critical current at 77 K, how that current falls off with field, and how the film behaves when you put a magnet near it. The first three are standard. The fourth is where the interesting arguments are.

Most of the lab's output is not a paper. It is a specimen record: a growth recipe, a measurement file, and a note saying what went wrong. Those records are the actual asset. A film that fails tells you more per hour than a film that works, as long as you wrote down the oxygen pressure.

The films

YBCO is a layered cuprate with a transition temperature near 92 K that depends on how much oxygen sits in the chain layers. Get δ to 6.9 and you have a superconductor at liquid-nitrogen temperature, which is the whole industrial point: nitrogen costs a fraction of helium. Get δ to 6.8 and you have a tetragonal insulator with a nice colour.

Our standard film is 300 nm on c-cut sapphire with a 200 nm in-situ silver contact. We add BaZrO3 nanocolumns at 3 to 6 vol% because a column that grows through the film thickness is a nearly ideal pinning centre: it is the right size for a vortex core, it runs the full film thickness, and it does not destroy the epitaxy if you keep the growth temperature in a narrow window.

Methods, plainly

  • Growth
    Pulsed laser deposition, 248 nm KrF, 780 °C substrate, 0.4 mbar O2, then a 45-minute anneal at 550 °C in 1 bar O2 to set the oxygen content.
  • Structure
    X-ray diffraction for c-axis alignment and phase purity; rocking curve width under 0.3° is the pass mark.
  • Transport
    Four-point van der Pauw and bridge geometry, 1 µV/cm criterion, currents from 10 µA to 10 mA.
  • Field
    Superconducting solenoid to 9 T, angular sweep from 0 to 90° in 5° steps at three temperatures.
  • Levitation
    Load cell under a NdFeB stack, gap measured by a laser triangulator to 10 µm, field-cooled and zero-field-cooled.
  • Cryogen
    63 L helium dewar, 1.5 L/h boil-off, 70% recovery through a reliquefier.

What I will not take on

I do not certify magnets, and I do not do failure analysis for a dispute. Both need an accredited lab and a chain of custody I cannot offer. I also turn down film growth for a device that has not been designed yet: the recipe depends on what the film has to survive, and guessing costs six weeks.

What I am good for is a measurement that has to be right at 77 K, and a specimen record you can hand to someone else and have them reproduce.

Current collaborator

A cryogenic detector group in Grenoble, on a 12 µm film for a transition-edge sensor. They need low Jc and low noise; we usually optimise the opposite, so it has been instructive.

Teaching

One course a year, “Superconducting materials and their measurement”, for third-year physics students. The lab visit is the point of the course: they cool a puck, put a magnet on it, and stop asking whether levitation is fake.

Reading

Giordano and Rodrigues on flux creep, and anything by Blatter on vortex matter. The 1994 review is still the clearest thing written on the subject.

If you have a specimen and a question, that is enough to start.
