ABOUT & METHOD · KESSLER LOW-TEMPERATURE LABORATORY

Trained in the city where helium first liquefied.

I am Dr Sabine Kessler. I read physics and took my doctorate in low-temperature physics in Leiden — the city where Kamerlingh Onnes liquefied helium in 1908 and found, in 1911, that mercury’s resistance does not fade toward zero but drops to it. This science began in a laboratory a short walk from mine, and its founding lesson has not changed: the material is under no obligation to match the announcement.

Before founding K-LT in 2016 I spent six years in the test laboratory of a superconducting wire manufacturer, which is where I learned the trade’s open secret: datasheets quote the best length on its best day, and everyone downstream designs against it. An independent characterisation lab exists to close that gap. Manufacturers, materials groups and museums send samples to Leiden because the report they get back is the measurement — distribution, uncertainty, cooldown log and all — whether it flatters the sample or not.

My own rule, learned the expensive way and now printed on the run sheet: every superconductor is a liar until its third cooldown. The first cooldown is hope, the second is habit; the third is data. Thermal history, contact resistance and a dozen small accidents live inside any single run, and a lab that reports from one run is publishing its accidents alongside its physics.

The laboratory is deliberately small: two cryostats, a load-cell force rig, a Hall-probe mapping stage, one technician, and me. Small is a measurement decision — every sample in the log has been mounted, cooled and measured by the same pair of hands, which is why the log’s numbers can be compared across nine years.

METHOD

How a characterisation runs

FOUR STAGES, NO SHORTCUTS
1

Intake — and the question

Every engagement starts with the decision the number has to carry: a grade to set, a contract to qualify, an exhibit to keep standing. If no decision hangs on the measurement, the honest advice is not to spend helium on it, and that advice is free. The intake fixes the field and temperature range, the sample’s form factor, and what “answered” looks like — in writing, before anything gets cold.

2

Mounting & instrumentation

Four-probe contacts made and checked warm; thermal anchoring sized to the sample; the magnet’s field mapped with a Hall probe before the sample goes anywhere near it — because a force or a Jc quoted against an assumed field is two measurements pretending to be one. Sample File 03 exists because a client’s array had never been mapped.

3

The cooldown protocol

Controlled cooling rate, soak at base temperature, and a measurement sequence logged against time and temperature — the cooldown log is part of the result, not lab housekeeping. Anything that will be reported is measured across a minimum of three cooldowns; field-cooled force work logs the cooling position and gap for every run, because pinning remembers how it was frozen.

4

The report

Curves, not slogans: the full distribution across the batch, the uncertainty budget with its terms itemised, the method, and the cooldown log. The conclusion states what the data support — including, when true, “inconclusive at this range, and here is the measurement that would settle it.” A report from this lab has never contained a number its own log could not reproduce.

THE HONESTY PAGE

What this lab will not say

PRINTED, NUMBERED, BINDING

  1. One cooldown is an anecdote. A single run holds the physics and the accidents in the same trace, and no one — not the client, not the lab — can separate them afterwards. Nothing is reported from one cooldown; single-run data, when a client insists on seeing it, is stamped PRELIMINARY — ANECDOTE GRADE.

  2. A demonstrator demonstrates pinning, not the future of transport. Flux pinning is stable, stiff and genuinely astonishing, and it is lossy: hysteretic losses, cryogenic overhead and refill intervals are printed on every exhibit this lab builds. No label written here has ever used the word “revolution”, and none will.

  3. On room-temperature claims, the lab’s position is a queue, not a press release. This field’s recent history includes room-temperature superconductivity papers retracted after scrutiny and a much-publicised 2023 material that did not survive replication — it was not a superconductor. The pattern is always the same: announcement first, cooldowns later, correction last. K-LT inverts it. A claimed material joins the replication register (S.05), gets measured cold by a lab with no stake in the answer, and the result goes to whoever commissioned it, in full, either way. Replication before celebration is not scepticism about the future; it is how the field earned the past.

  4. The ceiling is not the force. P = B²/2μ0 is a bound set by field pressure. Measured pinning forces in this lab’s log run at 8–31% of it. Anyone quoting the ceiling as a working force — in a brochure, a pitch or a headline — is out by a factor of three to twelve, and the lift pressure tool on the run sheet prints the derating beside the arithmetic so you can watch it happen.

  5. Helium is a cost and a responsibility. It is finite, it is priced by the litre, and it escapes to space when vented carelessly. The lab recovers where the rig allows, credits recovered helium on the invoice, and will push back on any measurement plan that spends 4.2 K on a question that 77 K could have answered.

TERMS

Engagement terms

THE SHORT VERSION, IN FULL
  • Fixed quote after intake. The rate card on the run sheet is the price shape; the quote fixes it against your sample, field range and cooldown count before work starts. The conclusion never moves the price — a failing sample costs what a passing one costs.
  • Cryogens at cost. Helium and nitrogen are billed at the lab’s cost on the day, itemised by the litre, with recovered helium credited at the same rate.
  • Three cooldowns minimum for anything reported. Fewer are available only as stamped preliminary data.
  • Your data are yours. The lab publishes nothing about a client’s material or a live claim. Sample files appear in public only anonymised, and only where NDAs allow.
  • Demonstrators carry their margins. Force margins, working gaps and refill schedules are part of the deliverable, and exhibit text is subject to the lab’s honesty review — the lab will not write “antigravity”, “ frictionless”, or “the future of transport” on a label.
  • Replication takes the queue’s front. S.05 work displaces commercial bookings. If that delays your characterisation, the lab will tell you it was a replication that did it, and nothing more about it.
