01 · Air

CO2 is a shape, not a number

In a passage with one entrance, the air is a balance between what the rock and the soil produce and what the entrance exchanges. Deep passages in this karst produce carbon dioxide from limestone dissolution and from organic material washed in during floods. The exchange is driven by the temperature difference between the cave and the outside, which means the same passage is well ventilated in January and dead in July.

At 5,000 ppm a person notices breathing at rest. At 30,000 ppm the breathing reflex is driving hard and a headlamp starts to feel heavy. The 8-hour occupational limit used in Vietnam for confined spaces is 5,000 ppm; cave rescue teams in Europe work to 1% (10,000 ppm) with a hard stop at 3%. The bench above uses those thresholds, and it deliberately does not model the transient: a slug of CO2 coming down a passage in a flood is a different problem.

02 · Radon

Radon is the slower hazard

Radon-222 comes from radium in the limestone, diffuses out of the walls, and decays with a 3.8-day half-life. In a sealed passage the concentration builds until production equals ventilation, and the equilibrium can be thousands of becquerels per cubic metre. Nothing about it is felt. The dose arrives as alpha particles into the lining of the lung, and it accumulates over a career.

Guides who work a show cave for 1,500 hours a year are the exposed group, not the visitors. The number that matters for them is the annual dose against the 20 mSv occupational limit; a passage at 2,000 Bq/m³ for 800 hours gives about 24 mSv, which is over. We measured 3,100 Bq/m³ in the lower series at Hang Én in 2023 and the management plan changed the guide roster because of it.

03 · Survey

Compass, tape, inclinometer

A cave survey is a traverse: each leg has a length, a bearing, and an inclination, and the station coordinates are the sum of those vectors. The arithmetic is simple; the discipline is not. The compass is read to the half degree, the tape is held taut at both ends, and the inclination is read with the instrument pointing down the leg, not at the floor.

Closure is the check. Walk a loop and return to a known station; the difference between where you think you are and where you started is the error. Under 1% of the loop length is a good survey. Over 3% and the loop gets re-surveyed, which in a wet vertical cave means a second weekend and a lot of rope.

If you are surveying a system with one entrance, measure the air before the second trip.
