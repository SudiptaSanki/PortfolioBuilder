About

I came for the rope work. I stayed for the air

I am a cave surveyor and, since 2019, the person in the massif who carries the CO2 meter. The two jobs turned out to be the same job: both are about knowing where you are in a place that does not give you any landmarks.

Sơn Trạch, Quảng Bình Surveying since 2014 186 km of passage surveyed

How I started

I studied geology in Huế and spent a summer mapping karst springs for a hydrology project. The springs came out of the massif at a steady 19 °C in every season, which meant the water had travelled a long way underground, which meant there was a way in. Finding it took two years and a lot of conversations with local men who had been inside as boys.

The first vertical cave I descended was 41 m and I was terrified for most of it. The man who taught me rope work was a carpenter from Sơn Trạch who had never had a formal lesson in his life and who could rig a rebelay faster than anyone I have met since. He also taught me the rule I still use: if you cannot explain the rig to someone at the bottom, you have not finished rigging.

Why the air

In 2019 a survey team came out of a lower passage with headaches and one person vomiting. Nobody had a meter. I bought one the next month, and the first measurement in that passage was 24,000 ppm — 2.4% CO2, close to the point where a headlamp feels heavy and judgement goes. The passage had been surveyed twice before. Nobody had thought to measure it because it did not feel deep.

Since then every trip in the massif carries a CO2 meter, and the deeper systems carry a radon detector as well. The numbers go into a spreadsheet and the spreadsheet changes where we take visitors and which passages get a second entrance opened for ventilation. It is unglamorous work and it has changed more management decisions than any of my survey data.

How a survey is run

  • Instruments
    Suunto tandem compass and clinometer, 30 m fibreglass tape, and a backup digital inclinometer. Distances to the nearest centimetre, angles to the half degree.
  • Teams
    Three people: one on the instrument, one on the tape, one recording. The recorder is the most important and the hardest to keep.
  • Notes
    Pencil on waterproof paper, station sketch for every leg, and a comment column that is allowed to say "draft" or "smell".
  • Reduction
    Compass and tape to station coordinates, then a loop closure check. Under 1% is good; over 3% goes back to the cave.
  • Air
    CO2 at every station, radon at the deepest and at the entrance, and a note of the outside temperature and the wind.
  • Grade
    BCRA grade 4 or better for anything published. Grade 3 surveys are working drawings and are labelled as such.

What I will not do

I do not guide tourist trips into the wild caves, and I do not give access directions. The massif is a national park with a permit system, and the accidents that happen are almost always parties who went in without one. If you want to see a cave, the show caves at Phong Nha are extraordinary and the guides there are better than me at showing them.

I also will not sign off a passage as safe on the basis of one measurement. Air changes with the season and with the weather; a single number in July tells you about July.
