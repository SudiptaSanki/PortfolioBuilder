Five instruments, and what each one is bad at.

Every payload in the log has flown at least three times. Each entry gives the mass, the power, the data rate and the failure I had to fix — because a specification without its failure mode is a brochure, not a datasheet.

I-05

Electrochemical ozone sonde

14 flights
Mass
1.2 kg
Power
2.0 W
Data
1 Hz, 4 channels
Range
0–35 km
Accuracy
±5% of reading
Flights
14

A cathode cell with a small pump that draws air through potassium iodide at a fixed 30 ml/min. The current is proportional to ozone partial pressure, which is the number the stratospheric community actually uses. It is old technology and it still beats a solid-state sensor above 25 km, where the air is too thin to cool anything.

What it is bad at. The pump is the failure point. Below 40 mbar it cannot hold flow, so the useful ceiling is about 30 km, and the cell drifts with ambient temperature. I fly two sondes per flight, one heated and one not, and use the difference as the error estimate.

I-04

Optical aerosol counter

6.0 W
Data
0.1 Hz, 16 bins
Range
10–30 km
Size bins
0.3–10 µm
Flights
8

A laser at 660 nm crosses a sheath-flow nozzle and a photodiode counts the light scattered by individual particles. Sixteen size bins from the pulse height. The whole thing sits in a heated enclosure because the nozzle ices up first at the tropopause, and a blocked nozzle reads exactly like clean air.

What it is bad at. It cannot tell sulphate from soot, and it undercounts below 0.3 µm, which is where most of the stratospheric background sits. The 2024 flights agreed with the lidar at 20 km within 15% on the accumulation mode and disagreed by a factor of two on the nucleation mode, which is in the report.

Flights

A fixed camera looking at the horizon, not down. The images are for the outreach that funds the programme and for a serious purpose: the curvature of the horizon at altitude is a good independent check on the GPS altitude when the receiver loses lock above 30 km, which it does.

What it is bad at. Contrast. Shooting into a black sky with a bright limb blows the exposure, so the camera brackets three stops and the middle one is usually the only usable frame. The window heater draws more power than the camera and is the reason the payload needs 30 W rather than 12.

I-02

Electric field mill

5 flights
Mass
0.9 kg
Power
1.5 W
Data
10 Hz, 1 channel
Range
0–35 km
Span
±50 kV/m
Flights
5

A rotating shutter over four sensing plates, chopping the ambient field into an AC signal that a synchronous detector recovers. It measures the vertical component of the atmospheric electric field, which is a fair proxy for charge on the balloon itself — a problem when the envelope becomes charged and drags the measurement with it.

What it is bad at. The balloon. Every flight shows a step when the envelope passes through a charge layer, and there is no way to separate the atmosphere from the balloon after the fact. The 2023 campaign is the one exception: a tethered reference on the load line let us subtract it, and the result is the only data set I would publish.

I-01

Standard radiosonde

41 flights
Mass
0.3 kg
Power
0.6 W
Data
1 Hz, 7 channels
Range
0–35 km
Link
403 MHz, 10 mW
Flights
41

The one instrument on every flight. Pressure, temperature, humidity, GPS position and wind, telemetered at 403 MHz to a ground station that has not changed much since the 1990s. It is the ground truth for the ascent model and the only thing that tells the recovery team where the payload is.

What it is bad at. Humidity above 12 km, where the sensor is too cold to respond and reports a dry bias that everyone knows about and nobody corrects in the archive. The GPS also drops out above 30 km by design, which is why the camera and the barometer are the backup altitude sources.

No instruments in that category yet. The February campaign adds a second aerosol channel.

If it fits in 3 kg and 30 W, it can fly.
