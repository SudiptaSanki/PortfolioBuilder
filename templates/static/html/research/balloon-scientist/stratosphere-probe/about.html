I build the small, cold, patient end of atmospheric science.

Fourteen seasons of balloon work, most of them at 67° north where the launch window is short and the recovery is long. My instruments are light, my models are honest about their error bars, and my payloads come home.

Sena Okonjo · Nairobi, Stockholm, Kiruna · 41 flights since 2013

A balloon is an expensive way to be cold for two hours — unless the instrument is worth the cold.

I came to the stratosphere through ozone. My first degree was in physics in Nairobi, where the interesting atmospheric problem was the recovery of the ozone layer over the tropics and the measurement stations that were, at the time, mostly in the northern hemisphere. I spent a summer recalibrating an old Dobson spectrophotometer and discovered that I liked the instrument more than the theory.

That took me to Stockholm for a master’s in atmospheric science, and then to Esrange as a payload engineer, which is where I learned how a flight actually runs: the 04:00 fill, the radiosonde that will not talk to the ground station, the parachute that has to be repacked because someone stood on it. In 2019 I went independent so I could spend more time on instrument design and less on launch logistics. The logistics still take half the year.

What I actually do all day

The work divides into three parts that never quite balance. In winter I design and build: circuit boards, thermal enclosures, and the calibration rig that tells me whether a sensor is measuring the atmosphere or itself. In spring and autumn I fly. In summer I reduce data, write the reports that fund the next season, and repair everything that came back wet.

Most instruments arrive as a university group’s prototype with a mass budget of “about a kilogram” and a power budget of “USB”. Making that fly is unglamorous: a DC-DC converter that survives −60 °C, a heater that does not cook the sensor next to it, and a data format that a graduate student can read at 3 a.m. after the flight.

How a season is planned

Two launch windows a year: February for the cold, stable stratosphere, and September for the turnover. Each window has six flight slots, and I take two external payloads per season. That number is deliberately small. A flight costs two days of preparation plus the recovery, and an instrument that has not been through my bench does not get a slot.

The bench test is where most payloads fail: 18 hours in a thermal chamber at −65 °C, a vibration sweep to the launch environment, and a vacuum test at 5 mbar to catch the corona discharge that only appears above 20 km. Roughly one in three prototypes fails the first time, usually at the cold soak.

What I will not fly

  • Anything that needs pointing. A balloon rotates and swings; a telescope that needs arcsecond stability needs a gondola I do not have.
  • Lithium cells above 100 Wh. The paperwork and the fire risk are not worth it on a platform that lands in the snow.
  • Payloads with no recovery plan. If you cannot come and help look for it on the tundra, it does not fly in February.
  • Untested flight software. A payload that reboots at altitude has produced nothing, and I have wasted a window finding that out once.

Where the numbers come from

The ascent model on the front page is the same one I use for flight planning, with one difference: in planning I use the measured wind profile from the morning sounding rather than an average drift. It is calibrated against fourteen flights where I have both the predicted and the actual burst altitude; the mean error is 800 m and the worst case is 2.1 km, which is why we brief three landing zones.

If you want the source, the model is in the methods section of the 2025 paper and the code is available on request. I would rather you checked it than trusted it.
