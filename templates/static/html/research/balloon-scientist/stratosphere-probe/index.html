The stratosphere is a laboratory with a closing time.

I fly instruments to 35 km on zero-pressure balloons — ozone sondes, aerosol counters, and cameras that see the tropopause from above. The altitude is the easy part. The ninety minutes you get up there, and the wind that decides where you land, are not.

0 km

Launch, and let go

We fill on the tarmac at first light, because the wind is lowest before the ground warms. The envelope is held by four people and a release pin until the free lift is verified twice: once by the fill calculation, once by a spring scale under the load train. If the two disagree by more than 100 g, we do not launch.

The first minute is the riskiest. A zero-pressure balloon leaves the ground under-filled and wrinkled, and it needs a clean release to avoid dragging the payload across the gravel. We have lost one camera that way, in 2021, and now the release is a two-person operation with a countdown read over the radio.

12 km

The tropopause is the hard part

At 11 km the air is −56 °C and the balloon crosses from the troposphere into the stratosphere. The temperature stops falling and the wind reverses direction. That shear is what turns a tidy vertical ascent into a drifting flight path, and it is why the recovery team is briefed on three landing zones rather than one.

The payload sees the coldest soak on the way up, not at altitude: the air is denser, the convective cooling is stronger, and the batteries are still cold from the ground. Every board I fly is tested at −65 °C for two hours before it is allowed on the load train.

24 km

Above the weather

At 24 km the pressure is 3% of sea level and the sky above is almost black. The balloon has grown from 2 m to 8 m and the gas inside is expanding fast enough that the envelope looks taut even from a camera 30 m below. This is where the ozone sonde does its work and where the aerosol counter stops seeing dust and starts seeing the sulphate layer.

There is no turbulence here worth the name, which is the point: a stratospheric platform is the cheapest way to put a stable instrument above 99% of the atmosphere and leave it there for two hours.

36 km

Burst, and come home

The envelope fails at its designed diameter, usually with a sharp crack that the microphone picks up. The payload falls under a 5 m parachute at 6–8 m/s, and the radio beacon starts transmitting the moment the load train separates. Recovery is a snowmobile and a hand-held yagi on the tundra, sometimes the next morning.

I fly a cutdown as a backup: a nichrome wire that severs the load line on command or after 4 hours, whichever comes first. It has fired twice in 41 flights, both times because the balloon did not burst where the model said it would.

The altitude markers on this page are the real boundaries a flight passes through, not a decorative scale. A balloon that bursts below 20 km has failed; one that passes 34 km has done what it was built for.
