Three flights, told in full

Every one of the 47 flights in the log has an entry like these. Three are printed here because they teach the most: a success that looked like a failure, a recovery that took three days, and a flight I ended early myself. The losses — STR-09, STR-19 and STR-41, payloads never recovered — have entries too, and any client may read them before booking.

The brief

An instrument team had a new aerosol inlet bound for an Arctic aircraft campaign. It had passed every bench test. What it had never done was be cold — genuinely stratospheric cold, at low pressure, for hours. They asked for a qualification flight, and admitted they half-expected a pass stamp.

The decisions

Two calls shaped the flight. First: fly the prototype as it stood, not the polished version due in six weeks — a test you cannot fail teaches nothing. Second: add thermocouples at every joint of the inlet, at my insistence and the team’s expense of one telemetry channel. If it failed, it would fail informatively.

The outcome

The inlet froze at 19 km, at minute forty-one. The thermocouple trace showed the cold arriving at one unheated joint — not the pump, not the lines, one joint. The team redesigned that joint over the winter, flew the fix on STR-34, and took it north. A pass would have sent the flaw to the Arctic, where the same discovery costs an aircraft season.

The brief

Launch four of a five-flight sampling series: whole-air canisters to be filled across a target altitude band and returned sealed, with the chain of custody unbroken from fill to laboratory. The forecast put the landing in open farmland. Forecasts are where this story starts, not where it ends.

The decisions

The payload came down instead in commercial forestry — dense, trackless, and private. The decisions that mattered were unglamorous: stop at the boundary and get the landowner’s permission rather than climb a fence with a beacon pinging; bring the second beacon’s slower fix into the search grid; and tell the client on day one, not day three, that two time-sensitive samples would not survive the wait.

The outcome

Recovered on day three, intact, canisters still sealed — the custody log held. But two samples with short holding times had degraded past use, and that loss is printed in the report with its cost, not smoothed over. What changed afterwards: dual independent beacons became standard on every flight, and landing-zone bias — choosing ascent profiles that favour open country — became a written step in flight planning.

The brief

A profile flight with a client sensor riding the train. The evening forecast was clean: a long float, a descent into open country, a straightforward recovery. The flight plan was signed, the permissions were in, the client was on site with coffee going cold.

The decisions

The morning sounding and the updated model run disagreed with the evening’s calm: the wind field had shifted, and the descent corridor now crossed a town. Not near a town — over it. At 06:40, before the balloon was even fully laid out, I set the plan in reverse: launch, take the profile we could get, and terminate at 24 km over farmland while the corridor was still ours to choose. The client heard the reasoning before the balloon left the ground, not after.

The outcome

A partial dataset — the lower profile complete, the float science lost — and a payload home by lunch instead of a headline by evening. The flight was re-flown three weeks later at the campaign’s field-day rate, not a second launch fee; that is the written policy when the termination call is mine. The reasoning, the two forecasts, and the corridor maps are all in the report. A flight director who will not end a flight should not be allowed to start one.
