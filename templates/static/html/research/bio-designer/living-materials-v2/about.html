The studioOne biologist, two technicians, one incubation room

A fermentation scientist who got tired of throwing the interesting part away

Dr Femke Jansen trained as a synthetic biologist and spent seven years in industrial fermentation, where the organism was the workforce and the product was whatever it excreted. The mycelium itself — the dense, self-assembling network doing all the work — was treated as waste. Hypha, founded in Rotterdam in 2019, is the studio she built to make the network the product.

The studio is deliberately small: Femke, two lab technicians, one incubation room, one kiln, one press. Every batch is logged by hand in the growth log this site is modelled on — substrate delivery, sterilisation cycle, inoculation, daily checks, drying curve, test results. When a client asks what their panel tested at, the answer comes from that log, not from a datasheet written for a material we did not grow.

The opinion the studio is known for, and will defend in any room: a grown material should be sold the way a harvest is sold — by grade and range, with the season admitted — not the way a plastic is sold, by a single number on a datasheet. Clients who need the single number need a different material, and we say so early.

How a programme runsFive stages, none of them skippable

From first call to batch card

  1. Strain & substrate selection

    We match the organism and its food to the job: hemp-hurd for panels and inserts, flax for finer surfaces, your own agricultural residue if you have one worth testing. Selection is a week of small grows, not a meeting.

  2. Moulding

    The substrate is pasteurised or sterilised, inoculated, and packed into moulds — yours, or built here. Mould finish decides surface finish; there is no sanding your way out of a bad mould.

  3. Incubation

    Dark, warm, humid, and checked daily. The batch card records temperature, humidity and what the growth front looked like each day. This is the stage the growth planner prices in days, and it takes the days it takes.

  4. Drying

    Kiln-dried to stable weight, which stops the growth and fixes the properties. Rushed drying warps panels; the four printed days are the floor, not the target.

  5. Testing & the batch card

    Twelve coupons per batch are pressed to failure so the delivered pieces never have to be. Results are written on the batch card as the range they fell in, and the card travels with the work.

The honesty pageWhat this studio will not claim

Ranges, seasons, and the end of a panel's life

Ranges, not points

A living material's properties are a distribution, not a constant. Substrate varies by delivery, the organism varies by mood and microclimate, and the honest document is the range with the test method beside it. If a supplier of grown materials shows you a single strength figure with no range and no method, you are reading marketing, not measurement.

Living supply chains have seasons

Hemp-hurd after a wet harvest behaves differently from the same variety after a dry one, and our ranges quietly widen in autumn until the new crop is characterised. Programmes with fixed launch dates are planned against the slow end of every range on this site. That is not pessimism; it is the planner working as intended.

End of life, with its conditions attached

An uncoated H-7 panel is home-compostable in roughly 45–90 days in a working heap, and faster in industrial compost. Both halves of that sentence matter: the conditions and the coating. A waxed, lacquered or resin-skinned panel is only as compostable as its coating, and we print the coating on the batch card. The worst end for a grown panel is landfill, where it degrades without oxygen — if your waste stream is landfill, we will tell you the honest benefit is smaller than the brochure version.

What the materials are not

Not structural. Not weatherproof. Not fire-rated. Not food-contact certified. Not identical from batch to batch. A studio that starts with this list earns the right to be believed about the rest.

Engagement termsPrinted before you ask

How working with the studio starts

Start small, on purpose. New clients begin with the two-week feasibility sprint (€3,400 fixed) or a paid sample batch. Production is quoted afterwards, as a range with a ceiling; the ceiling is the number we are held to.

Deposit and balance. 40% on booking a production slot, the balance on delivery of the batch card and the work. Sprint fees are paid in full at booking — the sprint's output is a verdict, and a verdict cannot be repossessed.

The contamination clause. If a batch is lost to contamination in our room, it is remade at our cost and your slot keeps its priority. You will be told the day we know, with the batch card attached — case file 02 on the programmes page is this clause, exercised.

Lead times are ranges. We quote lead times the way the planner computes them — a booked figure and an honest range. If your date cannot survive the slow end of the range, we will say so before taking the deposit.
