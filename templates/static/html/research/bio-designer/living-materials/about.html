Practice — a small workshop in Grefsen

Six square metres of growing room, one kiln, and a press borrowed in 2019

I am a materials designer, not a mycologist. The difference matters: I am interested in what a fungus does to a waste stream under a deadline, not in describing the organism. The studio is four people, a laminar bench and a lot of shelves with numbered samples on them.

How the material is made

Process notes
revised 03.2026

The substrate is agricultural waste: hemp hurd from a farm near Hamar, wheat straw from the Østlandet plateau, sawdust from a joinery two streets away. It is pasteurised, not sterilised. A fully sterile substrate grows a cleaner panel and costs four times as much; a pasteurised one carries a small bacterial population that keeps the mould in check and has not, in five years, ruined a batch.

Spawn is mixed at 8–12 % by mass. The filled moulds sit in a dark room at 24 °C and 85 % relative humidity for two to four weeks, depending on the species and the thickness. Pleurotus ostreatus is fast and forgiving. Ganoderma is slower and makes a harder skin, which matters for anything that will be touched.

Then the panel is dried to stop growth. This is the step that decides the material. Dry too fast and the surface checks; dry too slowly and the panel fruits, turning its energy into mushrooms instead of binder. The kiln runs at 60 °C with a 14-hour ramp, and the schedule has not changed since 2023.

A mycelium panel is a composite whose matrix was alive for three weeks. Everything interesting about it comes from what the matrix did while it was alive.

Finally the panel is pressed. Pressing closes the pores and raises density; density raises strength and lowers insulation. You cannot have both, and the bench on the front page exists so that clients stop asking for both.

Measured, not claimed

Test standards
and what they miss

Every batch is tested in compression on a 100 mm cube, at 12 % equilibrium moisture content, following the spirit of EN 826 rather than its letter. The studio does not have a certified press; the numbers are comparative, and are published as ranges because the material varies by about 18 % between panels from the same batch.

Thermal conductivity is measured on a guarded hot plate at the Oslo Metropolitan University laboratory, twice a year, on the three densities the studio actually sells. Fire performance is tested by an external laboratory and is, for the unfaced material, class D. That is honest and not good enough for a public building above two storeys, which is why the acoustic panels are always installed behind a class B facing.

The carbon figure includes stored biogenic carbon only. It does not include the energy used to pasteurise, dry and press. If it did, a 176 kg/m³ panel would come out around 40 % less impressive, and the studio would still argue that it is better than extruded polystyrene.
