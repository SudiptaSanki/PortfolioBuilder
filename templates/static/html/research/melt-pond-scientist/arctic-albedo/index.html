The ice is white until it isn't

01

One surface, three albedos

Fresh snow reflects about 80% of the sunlight that hits it. Bare ice reflects around 55%. A melt pond reflects between 10% and 30%, depending on how deep it is and what is under it. The pond is a window into the ocean, and the ocean absorbs almost everything.

The Arctic Ocean loses its snow cover earlier than it did in the 1990s, and the melt season starts sooner. Once ponds form, the floe's area-averaged albedo falls from roughly 0.7 to 0.4 in a matter of weeks. That is not a small change in a radiation budget: over 24 hours of polar daylight, it can double the energy the surface absorbs.

Ponds are also self-organising. Water collects in depressions, melts the ice beneath it, and the pond deepens and widens. Drainage through a crack can empty a pond in hours, which is why pond cover is patchy and why a single aerial survey is not enough.

My work is mostly measurement: pond depth and extent from drone photogrammetry, spectral albedo from a mast on the ice, and the energy balance from a station that runs all summer. Models are only as good as the pond fraction they are given, and pond fraction is the least constrained variable in most of them.

The consequence matters beyond the Arctic. A darker summer surface warms the ocean mixed layer, delays autumn freeze-up, and feeds back into the following melt season. The loop is short enough to see in a single floe and long enough to matter for the whole basin.

With 31% of the floe ponded, the surface albedo is 0.58 and the floe absorbs 112 W/m², about 55 W/m² more than the same surface with no ponds. Over 60 days that surplus is enough to melt 0.92 m of ice, if all of it went into melting rather than warming water or the air.

04

How the numbers are made

Spectral albedo

An ASD field spectrometer on a 3 m mast, 350–2,500 nm, up- and down-facing. One spectrum every 15 minutes, with a reference panel swap each morning.

3,140 spectra, 2020—2024

Pond geometry

Drone photogrammetry at 3 cm ground resolution, processed to a digital surface model. Pond depth from the water surface to the ice surface beneath, not from a staff gauge.

41 flights · 62 ha mapped

Energy balance

A SIMBA-type mass balance buoy and a four-component radiometer on the same floe. The radiation sum is checked against the spectral measurements every clear day.

5 buoys · 3 seasons

Modelling

One-dimensional radiative transfer (DISORT) for pond albedo, and the Los Alamos sea-ice model CICE for the basin-scale feedback. Pond fraction is prescribed from the surveys, not tuned.
