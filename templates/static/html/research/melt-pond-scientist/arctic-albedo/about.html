I measure the surface that decides how much heat the ice takes

A pond looks like a puddle. On a floe it is a change in the radiation budget that runs for weeks, and the difference between ice that survives September and ice that does not.

I grew up in Qaanaaq, where the fast ice is a road for five months of the year and the colour of the ice is something people read the way a farmer reads a field. My grandfather could tell you where the ice was thin from the shade of blue. I went to university to learn why, and came back with a spectrometer.

Understanding the optical properties of ice is a physics problem with a field component that cannot be simulated: you have to be there when the first pond forms, watch it deepen, and catch the day it drains through a crack. Since 2015 I have spent between six and eleven weeks a year on ice, usually in spring and late summer, with a team of two to five people.

Most of my published work is on the gap between measured and modelled albedo. The models are not wrong about the physics; they are wrong about the surface, because pond fraction is patchy, changes fast, and is expensive to measure. I have spent a decade making that measurement cheaper and more frequent, including training local observers to photograph ponds from fixed points.

I am not a climate communicator by trade. When I speak publicly I try to show the measurement and let it carry the argument, because the numbers from a single floe are more convincing than another projection.

Teaching and field training

Sea-ice optics field school — a week on the Qaanaaq fast ice each April, run with the Arctic University of Norway. Students learn the spectrometer, the reference panel and the error budget, then produce one albedo transect each.

Local observation programme — training for eight hunters and two school teachers in Qaanaaq who photograph fixed pond sites through the melt season. Their images now calibrate the satellite pond-fraction product.

Service

Reviewer for The Cryosphere, Journal of Geophysical Research: Oceans and Elementa. Member of the Arctic Sea Ice Working Group and the MOSAiC data legacy panel.

I also answer questions from students in Greenland about how to get into field science, which is not in any contract and is the most useful hour of some weeks.
