CASE 01 · 2024

The Fram Strait pond survey

Delivered · rebooked

The ask

A sea-ice modelling programme was assimilating satellite data with an assumed melt-pond fraction and a single flat pond albedo. It needed both measured, across one drift box, in one melt season — with uncertainty it could propagate, not just admire.

What we did

Twenty-six days on the ice. Fourteen walked transects totalling 38 km, albedometer stops every 100 m, spectral checks at class boundaries, and three aerial passes classified into surface classes and then ground-truthed on foot against the transects — the step that separates a pond fraction from a plausible picture.

The outcome

Pond fraction across the box: 0.31 ± 0.04, with pond albedo delivered as a distribution, not a point. The programme replaced its flat assumption with the measured distribution and rebooked the survey for 2026 before the report was a week old.

CASE 02 · 2023–24

The station that ridged

Partial delivery · told straight

The ask

An institute wanted a full winter of autonomous albedo and radiation data from a floe off the Svalbard coast: deploy in September, service by satellite, recover in spring. Thirty-six weeks of data was the plan.

What we did

Deployed on schedule with a power budget sized for polar night. In November a pressure-ridging event worked through the floe and sheared the instrument mast. The position beacon kept transmitting; the radiation sensors did not survive, and no honest servicing visit could have saved them.

The outcome

Nine of the planned thirty-six weeks of data were delivered, and the report says so on its first page — partial delivery, priced as agreed for exactly this risk. We recovered the logger the following April. The redesigned mount, with a sacrificial shear section that lets a mast fall without taking the logger, is now the standard build, and the institute renewed — for two stations.

CASE 03 · 2025

The number that moved a route

Delivered · plan changed

The ask

A polar logistics operator was planning a late-season resupply traverse and wanted the surface along its planned line measured before committing vehicles: ice class, pond coverage, and how much solar energy the surface was taking up.

What we did

A nine-day survey: six transects across the route corridor, class fractions and albedo at each, thickness spot-checks at crossing points, all reported against the corridor segments the operator actually had to choose between.

The outcome

The eastern leg measured as heavily ponded, thin first-year ice — the wrong place for a loaded traverse in August. The operator moved the route about 40 km west and left a week later than first planned. The traverse was completed on the revised line. A survey that changes the plan is the cheapest kind there is.
