01 — Activation

Every cloud is an argument between water and what it lands on.

A cloud droplet needs a particle to condense onto, and almost any particle will do at low enough temperature. The interesting question is which ones win. I measure the aerosol that reaches the Arctic coast and the supersaturations that decide which of it becomes cloud.

02 — Bench

The Köhler curve, plotted for the particle in your hand

A dry particle in rising air meets ever-higher relative humidity. It takes on water, and two forces disagree: curvature makes small droplets evaporate, dissolved solute makes them grow. Where the sum of those two turns over is the critical supersaturation — the point past which the droplet grows without limit.

03 — Field notes

Four things the coast has taught me

Eleven campaigns between 2015 and 2026, mostly in weather that made the instruments unhappy.

  1. i

    Sea salt is not the whole story

    In winter, 60 % of the CCN number at 0.2 % supersaturation is sea salt by mass but only 30 % by number. The rest is aged combustion aerosol that travelled four days to get here and still activates first.

    2023
    Bjørnholt

  2. ii

    Curvature matters more than textbooks suggest

    Below 30 nm dry diameter, the Kelvin term moves the critical supersaturation by 0.1 percentage points — enough to decide whether a particle activates in stratus at all.

    2022
    Lab

  3. iii

    The drizzle threshold is a moving target

    Clouds with the same liquid water path drizzle or do not, and the difference tracks the width of the droplet spectrum, not its mean. Number concentration is a poor proxy.

    2021
    Coast

  4. iv

    Ship emissions are visible in cloud

    A 2020 fuel-sulphur change reduced CCN in ship tracks measurably, and the cloud optical depth followed within two years. It is the cleanest aerosol experiment we have accidentally run.

    2020
    Open sea
