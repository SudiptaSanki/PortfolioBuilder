Work / Method

Method — instruments, closure tests, and honest uncertainty

What a cloud chamber measures, and what it only appears to

I have spent eleven years comparing two numbers: the cloud droplets that a parcel of air actually contains, and the droplets that its aerosol size distribution says it should contain. When they agree, the closure is satisfying. When they do not — which is most of the time — the disagreement is the result.

The measurement

Instruments
and their habits

Number size distributions come from a scanning mobility particle sizer between 10 and 800 nm, with an optical particle counter above that. The CCN counter supersaturates a sample stream and counts droplets; a temperature gradient inside the column sets the supersaturation, and the column has to be within 0.02 K of its set point or the derived supersaturation is wrong.

The instrument is honest, but it is a point measurement in a moving world. A one-hour average of a cloud that formed in ten minutes is a statistical object rather than a physical one. Most of my method is about keeping that distinction visible in the data.

Cloud droplet number and effective radius come from a forward-scattering spectrometer probe mounted on the aircraft, and from a microwave radiometer when the cloud is too shallow to fly. The two instruments disagree by up to 30 % at 0.03 liquid water path, which is exactly the regime where Arctic stratus lives.

Closure

Where theory
meets a duct

A closure study takes the aerosol size distribution, assigns a hygroscopicity κ to each mode, predicts the activated droplet number at the measured supersaturation, and compares. The κ-Köhler framework on the front page is the tool I use for that prediction; it is one line of algebra and a surprising amount of argument.

Typical disagreement is 20 to 40 % in number. About half of it is mixing state: an aerosol mode described as internally mixed is often an external mixture of a soluble accumulation mode and a hydrophobic one, and the hydrophobic one activates later, if at all. The other half is the small fraction of aerosol that is surface active, which lowers the effective surface tension and shifts the critical point.

A closure within 10 % usually means the instruments are mislabelled, not that the atmosphere is simple.

I publish the closure residuals alongside every campaign, not only the campaigns that closed. The residuals are what a modeller downstream actually needs.

Limits

What I do not
claim

  • No statement about cloud feedback from a single campaign. Two weeks of data constrains a parameter, not a climate.
  • No ice nucleation work. I measure liquid clouds and refer ice questions to people who freeze things on purpose.
  • No κ for organic aerosol without a composition measurement. The literature range is 0.05 to 0.3 and the middle of it is not a measurement.
  • No extrapolation below 10 nm. The instrument does not see it and the activation theory is less certain there.

Tables of every campaign's closure statistics, including the failures, are available on request. They are not beautiful, but they are the actual product.
