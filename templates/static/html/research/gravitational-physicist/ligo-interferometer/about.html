Sheet 03 · The scientist

Dr June Castellano

An instrument scientist by training, a sceptic by job description. The short version: I spent my research career on the noise side of gravitational-wave detectors — the side that has to be understood before the signal side can be believed.

§ 01 · TRAINING

How a physicist ends up hunting compressors

My doctorate was in experimental gravitation, on the thermal noise of mirror suspensions — the question of how the heat in a hanging mirror's own material blurs the measurement it exists to make. It is a good apprenticeship for this trade: you learn early that the instrument is a physical object in a physical building, sitting on physical ground, and that all three talk back.

From there I worked in instrument science — the teams that commission detectors, chase the noise floor down between observing runs, and staff the characterisation effort while the runs are live. Detector characterisation — detchar, in the field's shorthand — is the unglamorous half of the discovery papers: for every claim that a signal is a pair of colliding black holes, someone has to have shown it wasn't a truck on the highway, a train on the logging line, a magnetic storm, or a compressor cycling in a utility room.

I now do that work as an independent practice, for the groups and institutes who need a characterisation specialist for one problem, one run, or one cohort of new analysts — rather than a permanent post. The practice is deliberately small: four services, taken on one at a time, each ending in a written log that the client keeps.

A note on what I am not: I am not a spokesperson for any observatory or collaboration, and nothing here is written on their behalf. The detectors at Hanford and Livingston, the events, and the open data are public facts of the field; the engagements described on this site are this practice's own illustrative log.

§ 02 · METHODS

The working page, in plain words

left standing

M1The strain channel, h(t)

The detector's one output that matters: a single stream of numbers recording how the difference between the two arms' lengths changes over time. A gravitational wave appears in it as a chirp; so, unfortunately, does almost everything else. All characterisation starts from the fact that h(t) alone cannot tell you which it was.

M2Witness channels

Around the strain channel sit hundreds of auxiliary sensors — seismometers, magnetometers, microphones, accelerometers, photodiodes watching scattered light. They are the detector's witnesses: if a disturbance appears in a witness and in h(t) together, with the right delay and shape, the disturbance is local and explained. If no witness saw anything, the trigger earns a second look.

M3Glitches, and why most triggers are noise

The detectors are so sensitive that the environment writes in the strain channel constantly: ground motion, weather, machinery, electronics, light scattering off moving surfaces. These transients — glitches — outnumber astrophysical signals by orders of magnitude. The honest prior for any single trigger is that it is noise; the work is finding which noise, or failing to, carefully.

M4What a veto is — and its one rule

A veto is a rule that discards strain data around times when a witness channel proves a local disturbance was present. Built well, it costs a fraction of a percent of livetime and removes a glitch family entirely. The rule that outranks all others: never veto on the strain channel alone — a veto that looks at the signal to decide whether the signal counts will eventually eat a real event.

M5Hardware injections

The field's lie detector for its own tools: fake signals, with known properties, are physically pushed into the detector by moving its mirrors. Any veto, flag or pipeline that would have caught a real event catches these instead — in rehearsal, where the mistake is cheap. A veto that has not been attacked with injections is a hypothesis, not a result.

M6Tools of the trade

Python throughout, with the field's open-source stack — GWpy for time-series work, the public data products of the Gravitational-Wave Open Science Center, and the client's own pipeline tooling where an engagement runs inside it. Everything delivered is documented code the client's team can re-run without me.

§ 03 · OPEN DATA

The field gives its data away

Gravitational-wave astronomy does something still unusual in science: after a short proprietary period, the detector data — strain, witnesses, the lot — is published openly through the Gravitational-Wave Open Science Center (GWOSC), for anyone to download. The first detection's data is a schoolchild-accessible download. This practice is stated proudly here because it shapes the work: my training runs entirely on public data, my methods are reproducible by construction, and a client can check my reasoning against the same data I used.

It also shapes the ethics of the log. Engagement findings about a client's detector belong to the client; but anything I teach, publish or demonstrate publicly is built on the open data, never on a client's run.

Engagement terms

  • Lead time: investigations book 6–10 weeks out; audits must be scheduled against run calendars, so earlier is better.
  • Deliverables: vetoes, flags and audits arrive as documented, re-runnable code plus a written log entry — not a slide deck.
  • Data: client data stays client-side. Work runs on your infrastructure or on public data; nothing is retained after handover.
  • Verdicts: an investigation may conclude the glitch is innocent or the channel is fine. That conclusion is the deliverable, and it is charged the same.
  • Fees: as published on the Log page, reviewed each January. Fixed scopes are fixed.
