About the herbarium

A pressed sheet is a slower kind of memory

I am Nila Varghese, an ethno-botanist trained in plant taxonomy and then in pharmacognosy, which is an unusual order to do it in. Most people arrive at the chemistry first and treat the plant as a source; I learned the plants first, in the forest, with people who had names for parts I could not yet see.

01 — The path

Taxonomy, then a bench

My first degree was botany at the University of Kerala, where the herbarium was a room with a fan and a smell of naphthalene that I can still summon at will. I spent a postgraduate year pressing everything I could identify and failing to identify about a third of it, which is the most useful thing that can happen to a taxonomist early. A plant you cannot key is a plant that will teach you something.

The doctorate was at the Trans-Disciplinary University in Bengaluru, in a programme that put Ayurvedic practitioners and analytical chemists in the same corridor. My thesis was on the alkaloid content of Rauvolfia serpentina across elevation in the Nilgiris, and the honest finding was that the pharmacopoeial figure everyone quotes is a midpoint of a range so wide that it is nearly useless for a harvester.

After that came the part that is not taught: two years of field seasons funded by small grants, a Kew herbarium techniques course, and a long apprenticeship in a pharmacognosy laboratory in Leiden learning to run an HPTLC plate until the bands were reproducible. The website is the file I wish someone had handed me at the start.

02 — The order of operations

Consent is not a formality, it is the method

India's Biological Diversity Act and the Nagoya Protocol both require prior informed consent and mutually agreed terms before material leaves a community, and both are usually treated as paperwork at the end of a project. Done properly they change the research design, which is the point. If the agreement says the community shares in any commercial outcome, you cannot casually ship a sample to a partner lab. If it says the knowledge stays with a named healer, your paper cannot describe the preparation in a way that reconstructs it.

In practice this means my field notebook has two kinds of page. The botanical pages carry locality, coordinates, altitude, substrate, phenology and the collector number. The consent pages carry who was present, what was explained, what was agreed about the material and the knowledge, and who signs for the community. Both are deposited with the voucher, and neither is published in full.

Benefit-sharing has been small and concrete so far: a coppice plan and a harvest calendar for the Cinchona stand, duplicate sheets for the Peradeniya and CAL herbaria, and a stipend for two local collectors who now do the monitoring transects themselves. No royalties. It would be dishonest to present a portfolio of a dozen such agreements as a model for anything larger.

03 — The bench

What happens after the press closes

Determination. Every sheet gets a name, a family, and the name it was given in the field, which is often more useful than the Latin. Determinations I am unsure of are marked with a question and sent to a specialist; the sheet is not filed as certain until it comes back.

Fingerprinting. An HPTLC plate in three solvent systems, read under white light and 366 nm after derivatisation with Dragendorff reagent for alkaloids or vanillin–sulfuric acid for terpenes. It is cheap, it is fast, and it tells you whether two populations are the same plant chemically before you spend money on quantification.

Quantification. HPLC with a diode-array detector against a certified standard, three injections, a calibration curve rebuilt every session. Where no standard exists, the result is reported as a relative peak area and labelled as such, because an absolute number without a standard is a guess wearing a decimal point.

Identity check. DNA barcoding with rbcL and matK, and ITS2 where the genus is difficult. The barcode is attached to the voucher number, so a future study can re-extract from the same sheet rather than trusting a label.

04 — Limits

What this work cannot tell you

A voucher with 5.8 % quinine in the bark does not establish that a decoction cures malaria. Traditional use is a hypothesis about where to look, not evidence of effect, and the gap between them is a clinical trial that nobody in this portfolio has run. I say that at the start of every talk because the alternative is a slide that gets quoted out of context.

Content varies with season, soil, altitude, age of the part and how it was dried. A single figure on a label is a measurement of one collection on one day, and I try to always print the range beside it.

Taxonomy is not finished either. At least three of the species in my cabinet sit inside genera that are being split, and the names on the older sheets will change. The accession number will not, which is the entire argument for keeping one.
