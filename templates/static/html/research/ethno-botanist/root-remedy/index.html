The bench

Voucher to yield, in one sheet

Pick a specimen from the cabinet, then set the mass, the solvent and the number of partitions. The model is the textbook acid–base extraction: a geometric recovery per partition, then a Henderson–Hasselbalch split to see how much of the target is still free base and willing to move into the organic phase.

Planning arithmetic, not a validated assay. Real yields depend on particle size, moisture, temperature, solvent saturation and the plant's own season; the alkaloid figure on a voucher label is a range measured on one collection, not a constant of the species.

Method

Three rules, in the order they bind

Fieldwork is the easy part to photograph and the hardest part to do honestly. The order below is the one I defend in a review, because each step makes the next one possible and no step can be skipped by working harder later.

i

Ask before you collect

Who owns the plant, who owns the knowledge of it, and what they get from the paper that follows. Consent is a conversation with the community and the forest department, recorded in writing before a cutter touches a stem, and it can be withdrawn after the paper too.

ii

Press it twice

One sheet for the herbarium, one for the person who showed me the plant. A voucher without a duplicate is a claim, not a record. Every sheet carries the collector, the number, the locality, the part used and the name in the language it was given in.

iii

Then measure

Content varies fourfold between populations of the same species. A traditional preparation that works in one valley may be pharmacologically empty in the next, and the only way to know is a validated method on a determined voucher with the collection data attached.
