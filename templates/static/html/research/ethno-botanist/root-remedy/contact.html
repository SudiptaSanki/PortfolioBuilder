05 — How a sheet travels

From the forest floor to a cabinet drawer

In the field. Cut with a bud, a flower and, where possible, fruit on the same branch, because a determination from leaf alone is a guess. Note the habit, the height, the smell, the latex colour and the local name on the spot; none of those survive pressing.

In the press. Newspaper changed daily for the first three days, corrugated board between bundles, and a fan if the air is wet. A sheet dried slowly is a sheet that goes brown and loses the DNA you will want in five years.

In the post. Mounted sheets travel flat between boards, never folded, with the label on the front and a duplicate number on the back. Silica-dried leaf material for extraction goes separately, in a sealed bag with its own collector number, because the sheet and the sample must be able to find each other again.

In the cabinet. Filed by family, then genus, with the accession number in the corner. Frozen on arrival for a week. Checked twice a year for frass, which is the only evidence of beetles that matters.
