The measurer, and her methods.

A short training record, the working methods in plain words, and the page most portfolios skip: exactly how honest this practice is, and where its honesty runs out.

The story

PhD, Leiden Observatory, 2012 — a thesis on photometric calibration: the unglamorous question of how you know a telescope's “how bright” is true. It turned out to be the question the whole field stands on, because every distance past the first rung is a brightness, interpreted.

The years after were survey science: calibration teams on wide-field imaging projects, learning where pipelines quietly lie — a flat-field that isn't, a zero-point that drifts with the weather of the instrument. In 2019 I went independent, because the people who most needed a careful measurer — planetariums printing numbers for a decade, museums, small survey teams without a calibration scientist — could not hire one full-time.

The practice is deliberately narrow: distances, calibration, and the communication of both. If your problem is galaxy formation theory, I will happily argue about it over coffee and then refer you to someone better. If your problem is a number that has to be right, with its error bar attached, that is the log's home ground.

Data & code practice

Everything this practice ships is reproducible: versioned code, archived notebooks, data cited by DOI. A result you cannot re-run is a rumour. Negative results — the calibration that failed, the figure that turned out unmeasurable — are written up in the same log as the successes, because the next project deserves to know. Client data stays client data; the methods are published, the results are yours.

Methods, in plain words

About · The working page

Photometry — counting light, carefully

Measuring how much light arrives from a star or galaxy, and knowing exactly how much your instrument invented along the way. It sounds like bookkeeping because it is — and every standard candle in the ladder is a photometry result wearing a costume.

Spectroscopy — spreading the light out

Split light into its colours and it confesses: what the source is made of, how hot, and — stretched toward the red by the expansion of space — how fast it recedes, which is how the far rungs get their distances.

Statistics — the error bar is a measurement too

Bayesian inference, mostly: state what you assumed, update on the data, and report the spread honestly. A distance quoted without an uncertainty is not a measurement; it is a guess with good typography.

Calibration — the discipline underneath

Tying every instrument, field and epoch to the same reference, and tracking what the tying itself costs. This is the part of the work clients actually buy; the rest is arithmetic.

The honesty page

About · Read before hiring

What a model is — and isn't

A cosmological model is a machine for turning observations into distances. Feed it light; it returns numbers. The machine is very good — and it is still a machine built from assumptions: that the universe is the same in every direction, that supernovae behave at redshift 1 as they do nearby, that H₀ is one number waiting to be found.

The Hubble tension is what it looks like when the machine's two best calibrations disagree: 67.4 ± 0.5 from the early universe, 73.0 ± 1.0 from the distance ladder — a gap of about five standard deviations that has survived a decade of scrutiny. It may be an undiscovered error in either route, or the first crack in the model itself. This practice does not pick a side for you. It prints both numbers, labels them, and tells you which of your figures depend on the answer.

That is the whole product, really: numbers whose limits are printed on them.

Three promises

  • Every figure sourced. Each number I deliver traces to a primary measurement or a named catalogue — never to “it is generally known”.
  • Every distance carries its ±. If a client asks for the uncertainty removed, the engagement ends there. A bare number is how galleries shrink Andromeda.
  • Corrections are published. If a figure I signed off is later revised, the correction is logged in the same place the figure appeared — mine, publicly, first.
