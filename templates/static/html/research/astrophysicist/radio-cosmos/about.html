About the observer

I came for the telescopes and stayed for the bookkeeping

Radio astronomy is mostly subtraction. You remove the sky, the receiver, the ionosphere, the mobile phone tower eleven kilometres away, and what is left is a signal so faint that a single stray watt would erase it. I have spent twelve years learning what to subtract.

From a school rooftop in Bloemfontein to sixty-four dipoles

My first antenna was a bicycle wheel and a length of copper pipe, built to catch the hiss of Jupiter's decametric storms. It did not work for three months. When it finally did, the noise was not a sound I could point at, and that was the lesson: the sky arrives as statistics, not as an image.

I read physics at the University of Cape Town, then drifted into instrumentation because somebody had to keep the receivers alive. A receiver is a promise that the voltage at the digitizer means something. Most of my early papers are really about keeping that promise through heat, dust and lightning.

Since 2021 I have led the 21-cm cosmology programme at the Halcyon Array, a sixty-four-station low-frequency array in the Karoo. We are looking for the neutral hydrogen that sits between galaxies at redshifts 0.2 to 0.6, when the universe was assembling its clusters. The signal is roughly a millikelvin against a foreground of tens of kelvin, so the work is calibration, flagging and simulation, in that order.

The other half of my time goes to pulsar timing. Millisecond pulsars are clocks with a stability that embarrasses most laboratory standards, as long as you know exactly where the Earth is and how much plasma the pulse crossed. I build the timing models that answer the second question, and I argue with people about the first.

How I work

Three habits that show up in everything I publish.

Keep the failures. Every observing block writes a record whether or not it produced data. Wind stow, RFI storms, a receiver that drifted two kelvin — those records are how the next campaign avoids the same night.

Simulate before you believe. Before a detection is announced, the same pipeline runs on a synthetic sky with the same noise statistics. If the pipeline finds the signal in a sky where it was not inserted, the pipeline is the discovery.

Publish the code. Calibration is an argument, and an argument that cannot be re-run is a rumour. Everything I use is on an open repository with the version tag written into the paper.

Correspondence

Collaborations, observing proposals, and arguments about calibration.

I am especially interested in proposals that need long integrations on faint, extended sources, and in students who like the instrument as much as the sky.
