About

Twelve stations, one dirt road, and a habit of writing things down

I am a seismologist at the Universidad de Concepción. I locate earthquakes on the Chilean margin, and I spend an unreasonable share of my year driving to stations with a spanner and a laptop.

Concepción, Chile At the network since 2014 11 of 12 channels reporting

The first earthquake I located by hand was a M 4.2 near Lebu in 2012. It took me most of an afternoon with a ruler and a transparent overlay, and I put it 18 km too deep. My supervisor did not tell me the answer; she told me to do it again with a different station set, and the second answer was worse. That was the lesson: the number is not the point, the uncertainty around it is, and you only learn to state the uncertainty by watching your own answers move.

What I do now

I run the southern part of a twelve-station broadband network between Concepción and Tirúa, and I maintain the catalogue that comes out of it. The routine work is automatic: an association algorithm picks arrivals, a location code solves them, and a magnitude is assigned. The human work is the review, and it is most of the value. About one event in nine needs a manual repick, and the ones that need it are exactly the ones a catalogue user will care about.

The research question I keep coming back to is the boundary between locked and creeping parts of the megathrust. The 2010 Maule earthquake ruptured about 500 km of the margin, but it left patches that did not slip much and patches that slipped a great deal without much shaking. Those patches are not noise. They are the places where the next rupture will start or stop, and we can only find them by watching the margin between the great events.

The field kit

A station visit is a day of driving for two hours of work. The kit is a broadband sensor in a sealed aluminium can, a datalogger, a GPS antenna, a solar panel, two batteries, and a concrete pier that someone poured in 2015 and that has to be re-levelled every few years because the ground moves. The sensor sits in a pit lined with insulation; the temperature inside must not swing more than a degree a day or the long-period noise eats the signal.

The unreliable parts are the ones you would expect: solar controllers, antenna cables, and the person who locks the gate. The sensor itself has never failed. We have replaced four dataloggers, eleven batteries, one solar panel destroyed by a falling branch, and one GPS antenna that a horse ate.

What I will not do

I do not predict earthquakes, and I do not give a probability for one happening on a named day. I will give a long-term rate from the catalogue and a statement about which parts of the margin are locked, and I will say clearly that neither is a forecast. I also will not certify a building. That is a structural engineer's signature, and the ground-motion numbers I can supply are an input to it, not the answer.
