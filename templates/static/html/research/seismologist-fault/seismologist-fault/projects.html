Field campaigns

Five deployments, and the mistake in each one

Campaign reports are usually written as successes. These are written as records: what was installed, what was measured, and what I would do differently. The last item is the one I use when planning the next deployment.

2016 – 2026 5 campaigns 41,208 located events

Arauco slow-slip array

Eight temporary stations over the Arauco peninsula, run for 26 months to catch a slow-slip episode that GPS had been promising since 2020. The slip arrived in March 2025: 4 cm of aseismic displacement over six weeks, with a tremor burst that the array recorded as continuous low-amplitude energy between 1 and 4 Hz.

The stations were spaced about 18 km apart, which was too wide to locate the tremor tightly. We could say it was migrating north-east; we could not say whether the migration was a line or a patch. Three stations from the permanent network filled the gap in the middle, which is the only reason the result is publishable.

The mistake: we sized the array for the slip, not for the tremor. Tremor location needs a tighter aperture than slip does, and we knew that before we went.

Ocean-bottom seismometers off Lebu

Four broadband ocean-bottom instruments, deployed for five months in 2023 in 800 to 1,100 m of water, to close the gap between the coastline and the trench. The instruments record on a clock and come back with a hard disk; there is no telemetry, so the only thing that matters is that they come back.

Three did. The fourth released from its anchor but its flotation did not surface, and it is still down there in 940 m of water, 31 km offshore. The data from the other three changed the depth distribution of interface events in the 30–50 km band: events we had placed at 25 km were 15 km deeper, and the megathrust is steeper than the land network alone suggested.

The mistake: one acoustic release, no redundancy. Every subsequent deployment has had two.

The Chillán borehole

A 40 m borehole sensor in the city, installed in 2019 because the shallow basin under Chillán amplifies shaking in the band that matters for two- to five-storey buildings. The borehole records at depth and a surface sensor records at the same site, so the site response is measured rather than modelled.

The amplification comes out at 2.4 between 0.6 and 1.8 Hz, which is consistent with the basin model and with the damage pattern from 2010. The useful part is not the number; it is that the municipality now has a measured number it can put in a building bylaw instead of a borrowed one from another city.

The mistake: the surface sensor shares a power line with a street light. Every evening at dusk there is a 50 Hz transient that we filter out and that should not be there in the first place.

Chiloé aftershocks, 2016

Six weeks after the M 7.6 Chiloé earthquake on 25 December 2016, we deployed six stations over the southern end of the rupture to record the aftershock sequence. The sequence was offshore, so the stations sat on the coast and the azimuthal gap stayed near 200° for the earliest events, which is why the first catalogue has depths with 10 km error bars.

What came out of it was a clear picture of the rupture edge: aftershocks concentrated where the slip patch ended and where the 1960 Valdivia rupture begins. That boundary has held for every subsequent event we have recorded, and it is now the working hypothesis for where the next southern rupture will stop.

The mistake: we arrived six weeks late and missed the largest aftershocks. A standing deployment of four instruments in the area would have caught them.

Microzonation of the Concepción conurbation

Ninety-two noise measurements across the city, four hours each, to map the fundamental period of the ground. The result is a map with three zones: the rock of Cerro Caracol at 0.2 s, the fluvial terraces at 0.4 to 0.6 s, and the reclaimed ground near the river at 1.1 s.

The map is not a substitute for a proper geotechnical survey, and I say so in every presentation. What it does is tell a planner where a survey is needed most, which is a cheaper question to answer and the one that gets acted on.

The mistake: two of the ninety-two sites were measured during a construction crane's working hours, and their spectra are useless. The field sheet did not have a place to write “crane”, so nobody did.
