01

Depth is the hard part

A hypocentre has four unknowns: two horizontal coordinates, depth, and origin time. A station gives you one arrival time. Four stations, four measurements — but they are almost colinear in their effect on depth, so the solution is a long thin ellipsoid, not a point. That is why an automatic location can move 20 km in depth between two revisions and both be honest.

The S–P time helps because it removes the origin time: the difference depends only on distance. Three S–P times give three distances, and three distances give a horizontal position and a depth. The catch is that S–P is a small number — at 50 km it is 6 seconds — so a 0.2 s pick error moves the epicentre by a couple of kilometres. On the bench, the picks carry exactly that kind of error, and the solve lands close but not on the truth.

One trace, two arrivals. The interval between them grows with distance: 0.119 s per kilometre for a 6.0 km/s P wave and a 3.5 km/s S wave.

02

Magnitude is a family, not a number

ML was built for local earthquakes recorded on a Wood–Anderson torsion seismometer in California in 1935. It saturates: above about M 6.5 the amplitude stops growing with the size of the rupture, so a great earthquake reports the same ML as a moderate one. mb saturates earlier. Ms saturates around 8. Mw was introduced because the seismic moment keeps growing: it is the product of shear modulus, rupture area and mean slip, and it scales with the energy released rather than with the shaking at one station.

Mw = (2/3) log10 M0 − 6.07, with M0 in newton-metres. Each unit of Mw is about 32 times more energy, which is why the difference between 6.4 and 7.4 is not a rounding error: it is the difference between a building that needs repair and a town that needs rebuilding.

03

What the network is for

Twelve stations, eleven of them free-field broadband sensors on concrete piers, one in a borehole at 40 m. The borehole station exists because the ground under the city amplifies shaking by a factor of two to three in the 0.5–2 Hz band, and building codes need to know that before they need to know anything else.

The network is not a warning system. Chile's warning comes from the coastal strong-motion array and from the offshore GPS, and the useful warning time in Concepción is measured in seconds to tens of seconds depending on where the rupture starts. What this network adds is the slower product: a well-located catalogue, focal mechanisms for the interface, and a record of how the margin behaves between the great earthquakes.

“The margin is not locked everywhere. That is the sentence that keeps the field programme funded.”
