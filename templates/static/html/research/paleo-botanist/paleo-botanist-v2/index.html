The deep-time panel

Two measures every client should be able to check

Age claims are where amber collecting goes wrong, so the arithmetic this practice works by is printed here, where anyone can test it. Both tools read only the figures in their own tables.

1 · The Period Measure

Pick a period of the geological timescale. The sheet works out how long it lasted and what share of the Phanerozoic — the eon of visible life — it takes up.

Duration: 79.0 million years
Share: 14.7% of the Phanerozoic
145 − 66 = 79.0 Myr · 79.0 ÷ 538.8 × 100 = 14.7%

Duration = start − end (ages in Ma, millions of years ago).
Share = duration ÷ 538.8 × 100, where 538.8 Ma is the base of the Cambrian — the full span of the table below.

Cretaceous row highlighted in the table — the same figures the sheet reads.

2 · The Radiocarbon Limit

Carbon-14 decays with a half-life of 5,730 years: every 5,730 years, half of what remains is gone. Step through the half-lives and watch the signal die.

0half-lives
Remaining: all of it — 1/1
100.0% of the original ¹⁴C
0 years elapsed

Fraction remaining = (½)n after n half-lives.
Years elapsed = n × 5,730.

Each step halves what is left. Step to 8 or beyond and the signal reaches the practical limit of the method.

The point of this panel: amber is tens of millions of years old. By 10 half-lives — 57,300 years — radiocarbon is effectively exhausted, and amber is older than that by a factor of hundreds. Anyone offering you a carbon-14 date for a piece of amber is selling fiction. Amber is dated by the rocks it sits in and the fossils around it, never by its own carbon.

Then the state of the existing labels: a collection with no register takes longer to open than one with a wrong register, because a wrong register at least says where to start checking.
