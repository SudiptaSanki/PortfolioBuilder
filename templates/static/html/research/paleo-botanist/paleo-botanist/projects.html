SP-01 · 2020—2022

The inclusion census

412 pieces · 9,412 inclusions · 61 arthropod families

The museum's amber collection had never been fully catalogued. We photographed every piece at three angles, described each inclusion to family where possible, and imaged the opaque pieces with synchrotron micro-CT. The census raised the deposit's arthropod family count from 34 to 61 and produced the first Iberian Cretaceous records of nine families.

SP-02 · 2022—2024

An Aptian CO₂ curve

1,860 Frenelopsis shoots · three horizons · 14,600 stomatal counts

Frenelopsis is a cheirolepid conifer with small, scale-like leaves arranged in whorls. It grew in coastal environments across the Cretaceous and it is abundant in the Escucha and Utrillas formations, which makes it the best available recorder of Aptian CO₂ in Iberia. We counted stomata on 1,860 shoots and calibrated against the nearest living relatives and the published experimental response of conifers to CO₂.

The absolute values depend on the calibration, but the shape of the curve does not: a rise into the early Aptian, a dip at the oceanic anoxic event, and a plateau afterwards. Two independent leaf floras from Teruel give the same shape.

SP-03 · 2024—2025

The oldest flower in the drawer

MCNA-3140 · 4.1 mm · 38 pollen grains

The piece was catalogued as "indeterminate plant material" until a micro-CT scan showed five petals and an ovary. The flower is 4.1 mm across, with in-situ pollen that places it among the basal eudicots. It pushes the group's appearance in Iberia back by about six million years.

The pollen is tricolporate with a finely reticulate exine, which is the combination that places the flower among basal eudicots rather than magnoliids. A second flower from the same piece is still being reconstructed.

SP-04 · 2023

What survives in resin

Laboratory experiment · 240 resin drops · 18 months

Not everything that falls into resin is preserved equally. We embedded known insects, pollen and plant hairs in fresh conifer resin and aged the samples under UV and heat for 18 months, then compared the survival of different tissues. Soft tissue survived best where the resin had polymerised quickly; membranous wings were the first to go.

Table SP-04 — Tissue survival after artificial ageing
TissueSurvival at 6 monthsAt 18 monthsMain failure mode
Chitinous cuticle96%91%decolouration

The experiment explains a bias in the fossil record: the amber assemblage over-represents hard-bodied insects and under-represents soft-bodied ones, not because they were absent but because they did not survive the resin.

SP-05 · 2020—2021

Fire made the amber possible

Charcoal counts · 6 sections · Escucha Formation

Resin production in modern conifers increases after damage, and fire is the most common cause of damage in a seasonally dry forest. We counted charcoal in six sections through the Escucha Formation and found that the richest amber horizons sit directly above charcoal-rich beds, with a lag of less than a metre of sediment.

The pattern does not prove that fire caused the resin, but it does predict where to look: the next field season will sample above two newly logged charcoal beds to test it.
