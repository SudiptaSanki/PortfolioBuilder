The story

Fourteen years of rooms

Marcus Thorne did his first open mic in a Manchester pub basement in 2012, five minutes in front of eleven people, four of them other acts. The route since has been the unglamorous one and the one that works: open spots, then paid tens, then twenties, then closing — over 900 club sets across the UK circuit before the first hour was attempted.

The hour, “Second Helpings”, was built the slow way too: fourteen ticketed previews, nine minutes cut, an opener thrown away at preview three. It now tours as the headline format. Alongside the circuit work sits the trade most comics keep quiet: corporate clean sets, written and guaranteed to a definition agreed in advance — sixty-odd engagements for firms that cannot risk their Christmas party becoming a story.

For two years he has also held the compère residency at The Brickhouse's monthly new-act night — the job that teaches a comic the whole bill, not just their own twenty minutes of it.

Based in Manchester; tours anywhere the rail network reaches, and most places it doesn't.

Long bio — for programmes

Marcus Thorne started, as almost everyone does, in a pub basement — five minutes, eleven people, four of them other acts waiting to go on. Fourteen years on, the count is past 900 club sets: open spots to paid tens, tens to twenties, twenties to closing, in rooms from 40 seats to 600 across the UK circuit.

His first full hour, Second Helpings — family, debt and turning forty, built across fourteen ticketed previews — premiered on the festival circuit and now tours. In parallel he has built the quieter half of the trade: corporate sets written and performed to a written definition of clean, with bespoke material about the client agreed in advance; sixty-odd firms have booked it, most more than once.

He holds the compère residency at The Brickhouse's monthly new-act night, where his job is the whole evening rather than his own slot in it. He is based in Manchester and is, by promoter consensus, pathologically incapable of overrunning.

Content guidance

What “clean” means here — precisely

“Clean” is the most abused word in corporate comedy, so on this site it is a definition, not a vibe. It is the same definition written into corporate contracts.

The clean guarantee

A clean set from this act contains: no profanity (including mild swearing and blasphemy), no sexual content, no material about protected characteristics, no humiliation of audience members — crowd work stays on volunteers and stays kind — and nothing about named individuals at the client unless they have signed it off in advance. A topic kill-list from the client is agreed in writing before the night and is binding. If a booker needs it cleaner than that, say so at the briefing call; the answer will be honest either way.

Technical rider

What the room must provide

Stand-up's rider is famously short. This is all of it — if a room cannot meet a line, say so at booking and a workaround is usually possible.

Bespoke writing

Material written for your night

Beyond the six bespoke minutes included in a corporate booking, full writing commissions are taken: award-ceremony scripts, after-dinner sets built entirely around one company, and hosting scripts for galas — from £300 a day, with two revision rounds and approval of every line before the night. Material written for a client belongs to that night; it is not recycled into the club set.

Booking terms, in plain terms

Confirmation — a date is held for 7 days on enquiry and confirmed on a signed one-page agreement. Deposit — 30% confirms corporate and festival bookings; club sets run on the circuit's usual terms. Balance — 7 days before the event for corporate; on the night for clubs. Cancellation — by the booker: deposit retained inside 30 days, 50% of the fee inside 14. By the act: has not happened since 2016; a named, equivalent replacement is offered first, full refund if declined. Travel — as the rate card states: inside 60 miles of Manchester included, beyond that at cost, agreed before signing.
