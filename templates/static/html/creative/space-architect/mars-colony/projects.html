Case studies · Three of fourteen

The work, with its working shown.

Each study is told the way it was delivered: the brief as received, the decisions that cost arguments, and the outcome — including the findings the client didn't ask for. Two of these exist only on paper, and are labelled so. One was built and slept in, on Earth.

Case SH-01 · Agency concept programme

Ares Yard — a surface habitat for 500 sols

Crew6 · Duration500 sols · SiteElysium Planitia study area
Driving constraintRadiation · DeliveredRev C study, 214 pp, plan & section set
StatusConcept — nothing built

The brief

Design a six-crew surface habitat for a 500-sol stay whose mass budget closes inside one heavy-lander delivery class, with a radiation solution that doesn't depend on materials shipped from Earth. The client's opening sketch showed a glazed observation cupola. It is not in the study, and §4 of the report explains why, in numbers.

Decisions

Berm before crew. Two rovers spend the 90 sols before landing day pushing 2.5 m of regolith over the pressure vessel — roughly 1,900 tonnes of the one building material Mars delivers free. The berm, not the hull, is the radiation design.

Sleep inside the shelter. The storm-shelter core — wrapped in the habitat's water store for a second layer of shielding — doubles as the sleeping quarters. Dose is dominated by where you spend eight hours a sol, so the berths go where the shielding already is.

Two airlocks, no exceptions. The client's single-lock plan was returned with a failure analysis: one jammed outer door, with a crew outside and dust in the mechanism, is a fatal single point of failure. The study carries a suitport lock and a cycling lock, and pays for them in the mass budget openly.

Outcome

Rev C issued with the mass budget closed at 8% margin and modelled dose at the berth under 50 mSv per year, against roughly 240 mSv per year unshielded at the surface. Two client requirements were formally declined inside the report — the cupola, and the single airlock — each with its arithmetic attached. The study remains a concept; no hardware exists.

Case SH-02 · Analogue station operator

Cinder Flats — the refit that got slept in

Crew5 · Duration30-day isolation campaign · SiteUtah desert, Earth
Driving constraintNoise & privacy in 68 m² · DeliveredBuilt refit + crew debrief report
StatusAnalogue-tested — occupied 30 days

The brief

An analogue station whose crews kept finishing campaigns “frayed” — the operator's word. The building worked mechanically; people were the failing system. Refit the interior of an existing 68 m² station for five crew and thirty days, without changing the shell, and prove the result with a full isolation campaign.

Decisions

Berths away from the galley. In the old layout the sleep alcoves shared a wall with the galley and its 05:30 start. Berths moved to the quiet end; the exercise bike — the loudest object in any small habitat — went into the airlock vestibule's acoustic shadow.

One private volume per person. Not rooms — there is no area for rooms — but a curtained, ventilated, acoustically damped nook per crew member, sized for a call home and nothing else. Analogue crews consistently rank a door that closes above almost every other comfort.

A table that seats five, facing out. Meals are the mission's social engine; the old four-person table exiled someone every night. The new one seats the whole crew with elbows down.

SOL 04 — First full day. Noise at berth during galley start: 41 dB, down from 58.
SOL 11 — Crew have named the privacy nooks. Anything named gets used; anything used gets defended in the debrief.
SOL 23 — Commander logs “the building has stopped being the loudest personality on the crew.”

Outcome

The campaign completed 30 days with the crew's own friction log running at roughly half the entries of the previous campaign in the same building — a soft metric, reported as one. The debrief also produced two corrections to my own standard details (nook ventilation sizing, vestibule acoustics), which are now Rev F of the studio's analogue details and credited to that crew in the document.

Case SH-03 · Commercial lander programme

Meridiani Precursor — designing for nobody

Crew0 · Duration26 months unattended · SiteMeridiani Planum study area
Driving constraintDust & thermal cycling · DeliveredPDR document pack
StatusConcept — nothing built

The brief

An uncrewed station that lands twenty-six months before its crew, makes and stores oxygen and water, and must still be alive when they arrive — through two dust seasons, with nobody there to brush off a panel, reseat a connector or notice a slow leak.

Decisions

Nothing horizontal. Dust settles on flat surfaces and stays. Radiators stand vertical, arrays tilt at 35°, and every external ledge in the design was hunted down and sloped. The study's dust chapter is mostly a list of surfaces that no longer exist.

Electrostatic dust removal, flagged honestly. Specified for the arrays — and recorded in the pack as the least mature technology in the design, with a degraded-mode plan that assumes it never works at all.

Design for −110 °C nights. Thermal cycling, not cold alone, is the killer: two hundred freeze-thaw swings on every seal before the crew even launches. Materials and connector choices are argued from cycle counts, not temperature ratings.

Outcome

PDR pack delivered. Its most important page is a refusal: battery mass for riding out a 14-sol global dust storm at full load broke the budget, and the study said so rather than quietly shrinking the storm. The client chose a reduced keep-alive load for storm periods; the residual risk is written into the pack in plain language, where a future reviewer will find it.
