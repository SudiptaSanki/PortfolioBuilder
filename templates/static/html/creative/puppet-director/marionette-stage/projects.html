Case files · How the work was made

Three productions, told from the bench

Every show in the register began as a problem in a room: a sightline, a shoulder joint, a school hall with windows. These are the three that taught the company the most — brief, decisions, outcome, and the numbers we ended up with.

Case 01 · Touring production

The Salt Road — built to be read from the back row

A tabletop-and-rod telling of the salt traders' routes, made for studio theatres of 120–250 seats and a company of four who also drive the van.

The brief

Three venues co-commissioned a family show that could tour mid-scale studios without a fit-up day: get-in and show on the same day, out the same night. The story — a mule train crossing a desert of salt — had to read as clearly in row twelve as in row one, and the whole production, set included, had to travel in a single long-wheelbase van.

The decisions

The first puppets were built at 30 cm, the workshop's usual tabletop scale, and failed the sightline test in a raked 200-seat room: past row eight, faces disappeared behind heads. The cast was rebuilt at 45 cm, with heads and hands oversized by a further tenth — puppet proportions forgive what human proportions would not. The set became three stacking crates that are simultaneously the desert, the market and the transport boxes, so nothing travels that does not perform. After the first tour, the rod mechanisms were changed from pinned leather hinges to nylon bushings: leather stretched in hot venues and two wrists went slack mid-run.

The outcome

Thirty-eight performances in eleven venues across two tours, with the three-hour get-in held at every venue including two with no dock and a passenger lift. Rebooked by all three commissioning venues; the Mill Arts Centre testimonial on the Programme page is from this show. One puppet — the lead trader — has been repainted twice and restrung once, which is recorded in its bench file like everything else we make.

Case 02 · Commission for another company

The Harbour Fox — a shoulder built for two hundred shows

A 90 cm rod puppet commissioned by Harbour Light Theatre for their harbour-town fable — played by their company, built at our bench, guaranteed like our own.

The brief

Harbour Light needed a fox who could carry a forty-minute monologue, be operated by one performer from below for the whole show, and survive a two-year schools-and-studios tour. Their previous fox, bought from a prop maker, had a leather-hinged shoulder that tore at performance sixty and a head heavy enough to give its operator a wrist injury by the interval.

The decisions

The shoulder was rebuilt as a ball-and-socket joint in beech, with the tension cord replaceable from outside the body — a two-minute job with a hex key, not a workshop return. The head was carved in lime and hollowed until the whole figure came in under 1.1 kg, balanced so its resting weight sits on the rod, not the wrist. The tail got a counterweight in the body rather than a second rod, because the operator's other hand works the mouth. We delivered the fox with a printed bench file: materials, cord map, and the torque setting for the shoulder screw.

The outcome

More than two hundred performances in two years. Maintenance to date: one restring (year two, done under the commission's first-year service terms) and no joint failures. Harbour Light has since commissioned a second figure — a heron, on the same joint system — which is the outcome we count: a mechanism a touring company trusts twice.

Case 03 · Schools redesign

Paper Boats — the show that gave up the dark

Our schools show began life as a shadow piece that needed a blackout no school hall could give it. This is the redesign that tripled its bookings.

The brief

The original Paper Boats was a 50-minute shadow show. It was good, and it was unbookable: school halls have windows along one wall, curtains that have not closed since 1998, and forty minutes between the van arriving and the first class sitting down. In its first year it played nine schools and was turned away, kindly, by dozens more.

The decisions

Every shadow sequence was re-staged as tabletop and object theatre inside a portable booth (3.5 × 2.5 m) with its own backdrop, so daylight stopped mattering. Colours were re-cut in daylight-readable stock; the running time was trimmed from 50 to 40 minutes to fit a single lesson period with the workshop afterwards. The get-in was choreographed like a scene change — floor cloth, mats, booth, figures — and rehearsed against a stopwatch until it held at 45 minutes with two people.

The outcome

Sixty-plus school days a year, most of them repeat bookings, and a teachers' pack (story text, vocabulary, a make-your-own-boat worksheet) that travels with the show. The lesson the company keeps: the constraint was never the enemy of the show. The daylight version is warmer, closer and funnier than the shadow original — the children are three feet from the boats, and they can see our hands, which turns out to be the point.
