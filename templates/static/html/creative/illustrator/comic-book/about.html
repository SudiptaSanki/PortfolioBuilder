The artist

I draw the page I would have wanted to read at fourteen.

Rhea Mascarenhas, b. 1991, Mumbai. Eleven years of serial comics, four of them monthly. I work from a converted bedroom in Bandra with a light table, a scanner older than my career, and too many bottles of ink.

How I got here

I studied applied art in Mumbai because it was the only course my family could describe to relatives as a job. Illustration was the part of it I kept doing after the exam. For four years I drew storyboards for advertising — six frames, a product, a smile — and learned to make a moment legible in a 16:9 box. That skill turns out to be most of comics.

The first real comic came in 2019, a nine-page short for a zine printed on a photocopier in Dadar. The second was rejected by three publishers before Gallows Lane took a chance on Night Shift. That book is the reason I now ask for the printer’s stock before I accept a page rate: my line weights were drawn for a magazine and printed on newsprint, and half the work disappeared into the paper.

A comic panel is not an illustration. It is an argument with the panel beside it.

What I am like to work with

I answer mail in two batches a week, not continuously. I will tell you when a script beat does not survive being drawn — usually because two characters are saying the same thing in different words. I do not do revisions after ink unless I made the error. I do send pencils early and often, because changing a page at the pencil stage costs a phone call and changing it at the ink stage costs a day.

On collaboration: I want the writer in the room for the thumbnails, and then out of it while I ink. If you are drawing your own script, I will ask you which panel you would cut if the page had to lose one. There is always one.

Teaching

Saturday mornings, six students at a time, a lettering and page-composition class at a studio in Bandra. We start with a page of dialogue and no images, then build the smallest number of panels that can carry it. Half the class quits in week three, which is about the right rate for anything worth learning.

Awards and the rest

Bhairavi’s Bastion was shortlisted for a national comics award in 2024, which was nice and paid for nothing. Salt Winter was picked by two library lists in the UK. I have never had a book optioned and I would like to keep it that way for a while.

Straight line

Eleven years, abbreviated

  1. 2014
    First paid page

    A five-page strip for a transport union newsletter. Paid in cash and a bag of mangoes.

  2. 2017
    Storyboards, full time

    Four years of advertising boards taught me to stage a scene in six frames and no dialogue.

  3. 2019
    Night Shift, issue one

    Twenty-four pages, 100 lpi, newsprint. The lesson: draw for the paper, not for the screen.

  4. 2022
    First monthly schedule

    Four issues in eleven months. Learned to keep two pages at pencil stage at all times.

  5. 2024
    Bhairavi’s Bastion

    Six issues, a shortlisting, and a cardboard fort that is still on the shelf.

  6. 2025
    Salt Winter

    First book I wrote as well as drew. Ninety-six pages, and the quietest lettering I have done.
