The Case Files

Three books, told whole

Each file follows the same order — the brief, the decisions, the outcome — and keeps the parts studios usually file off: the late week, the other hands, the rejected thumbnails. Page counts are given throughout, because on a book, the page count is the truth everything else hangs from.

CASE FILE 01 · GRAPHIC NOVEL · FULL ART

The Salt Meridian

148 pages · Written by Ana Beltrán · Drawn & coloured by Lena Moreau · Lettered by J. Mbeki, engaged by the publisher · Halloway Press, 2025 · Status: published

Splash · The Lighthouse Road, p.61

The mid-book turn: the keeper walks the cliff path she has refused to walk for ninety pages. Drawn as a wordless double-page sequence — eight panels, no captions — because the script's silence was the bravest thing in it.

The Brief

148 pages, one winter

Beltrán's script: a lighthouse keeper, a decommissioned light, and the last winter before the automation crew arrives. Halloway Press wanted full art — thumbnails to colour from one hand — inside a 40-week production window for an autumn list.

The planner's arithmetic said 148 pages at full art runs 10 + 15 + 8 + 5 = 38 weeks. It fit, with two weeks of slack. That slack is the whole story of this file.

The Decisions

Silence, and a fixed sea

The sea was given one palette and one horizon line for the entire book, so the reader feels the winter narrowing. The storm sequence — 22 pages — was thumbnailed three times; the third pass cut six pages of weather and kept the two panels of the keeper's hands.

Eleven pages were redrawn at the artist's own call after pencil approval, unpaid, because the keeper's face had drifted between chapters.

The Outcome — Told Whole

One week late. Ours.

In week 31 the studio's one pair of hands was stopped for nine days by illness. The publisher heard it from the studio first, with the new date and the contract's late-delivery discount — 5% of the fee — already applied to the invoice.

The book landed in week 41 of 40, was published that autumn, and is the file this studio shows first. Not despite the late week: because the late week is in it.

CASE FILE 02 · SERIES ARC · ART ONLY

Night Freight

4 issues · 88 pages · Written by D. Mensah · Pencils & inks by Lena Moreau · Colours by R. Tanaka · Letters by the publisher's studio · Meridian Comics, 2024 · Status: published

Splash · The Viaduct Run, Issue #8, p.3

The courier of the splash page on the home page is this book's runner, three years earlier. Series work is a long conversation; the design of a character has to survive other people's deadlines as well as your own.

The Brief

Art only, full script

Meridian Comics needed an artist for a four-issue arc of an ongoing night-train thriller, working from full scripts — every panel described, dialogue placed. 88 pages, monthly issues, no schedule float at all: a monthly book is a train too.

The scope was pencils + inks. Colour and letters belonged to the publisher's regular hands.

The Decisions

Draw for the colourist

Working art-only means drawing pages another artist will finish. Night scenes were inked with open blacks — solid shadow left unhatched wherever Tanaka's colour was meant to carry the dark — and a one-page style note travelled with every batch so the colourist never had to guess.

Two scripted splash pages were re-thumbnailed as quiet nine-panel pages, with the writer's agreement, because the arc's tension lived in waiting, not impact.

The Outcome

Four issues, four dates met

All four issues were delivered on the publisher's dates. The credits below are printed exactly as they appear in the books — this studio claims the drawing, and only the drawing:

Story: D. Mensah · Pencils & inks: Lena Moreau · Colours: R. Tanaka · Letters: publisher's studio · Editor: Meridian Comics.

CASE FILE 03 · COVER COMMISSION · FULL ART

A Field Guide to Vanishing Birds

Cover + 3 thumbnails · Novel by H. Abernathy · Cover art by Lena Moreau · Cover design & type by the publisher's designer · Field & Furrow Books, 2026 · Status: published

Thumbnail A

The wing, only the wing

Dramatic, and wrong: at thumbnail size on a bookshop page it read as an abstract shape, not a bird. The client loved it; the sales range would never have seen it.

Rejected
Thumbnail B · Chosen

One bird, one field, one sun

The whole novel in one frame: a single swift, still flying, over an emptied field. It survived reduction to a phone-screen thumbnail, which is where covers now earn their keep.

Chosen
Thumbnail C

The honest one

The truest to the book — the birds are gone — and the bleakest. Kept for the paperback, where the readers already know why the field is empty. Rejected for the hardback, kept in the file, used in the end.

Rejected · Kept
The Final Cover

Cover art by Moreau; the title lettering and author line here are set in the template's display face to show placement — on the printed book, cover type is the publisher's designer's work, and is credited to them there.

The Brief → The Outcome

A cover that survives a phone screen

The brief: a literary novel about a bird recorder watching her species list shorten, year by year. The publisher's one condition: the cover had to read at 200 pixels wide, because most of its readers would meet it on a phone.

The decisions: the three-thumbnail round above. Every cover commission from this studio includes one — the client chooses with the rejected options in view, and the rejected stay in the file. Thumbnail A was the favourite in the room and failed the 200-pixel test in the corridor outside it.

The outcome: Thumbnail B, drawn up to the final above. The book was published in spring 2026; the paperback, due next year, uses thumbnail C — the empty field, once the readers know.

Fee, for the record: one cover at the printed line — £650, thumbnail round included, exactly as the estimator on the home page computes it.

The Pattern

What the three files have in common

A schedule printed before the work, credits printed beside the work, and the failures filed in the same drawer as the successes. That is the whole method; the drawing is the easy part to show and the hard part is this.
