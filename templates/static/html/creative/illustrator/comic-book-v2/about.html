About & Terms

The hand, the credits, the rights

Who draws here, how a book moves through the studio, and the terms it moves under — printed before you write, not after you sign.

Panel A

The illustrator

Origin Issue

Lena Moreau, Bristol

Moreau came to comics from theatre carpentry — building sets that had to stand up by Friday — and it shows in the work: pages are built, not decorated, and a schedule is a structure, not a hope. A first self-published book in 2016 (96 pages, printed at a copy shop, sold at two conventions) led to series work, then to the full-art graphic novels the studio now specialises in.

Nine years and 4,214 pages later, the practice is deliberately small: one artist, one book at a time, no studio assistants and no ghost hands. When a book needs hands this studio doesn't have — a colourist, a letterer — those hands are hired openly and credited by name, as the case files show.

The studio's working opinion, for the record: most late books are not drawn slowly, they are promised dishonestly. The pace table exists so this one never has to be.

Panel B

How a book runs

Six stages, in order, each with an approval gate. A stage is never started on an unapproved previous stage — that single rule is where most of the schedule is saved.

1 · Breakdown

The script is read whole, then broken into pages and page-turns. Problems of length are found here, where they cost an afternoon — a 148-page script that wants to be 160 is a conversation now, not a crisis in month nine.

2 · Thumbnails

The whole book, drawn small. The writer and editor approve composition and reading order. Changes at this stage are free and encouraged; this is the last cheap moment.

3 · Pencils

Finished drawing, in batches of ten pages, approved per batch. Casting, acting and architecture are settled here. The 137 redrawn pages on the record were redrawn at this pass, at the artist's call.

4 · Inks

Brush and pen over approved pencils. No structural changes past this gate — a change at ink stage is a new page, priced and scheduled as one, whoever asks for it.

5 · Colour

On full-art books, colour follows the book's fixed palette, set in the first ten pages and held. On art-only books this stage belongs to the colourist — credited, always.

6 · Letters & Delivery

Lettering is placed by the publisher's letterer over the finished art — lettering is its own craft and is never bundled into the drawing fee. Delivery is print-ready files plus the original art inventory, below.

Panel C

The honesty page

Drawn by Hand — the Evidence Is the Process

Every page here was drawn by a hand

Stated plainly, as a working fact: the art on this site and in the studio's books is hand-drawn — brush, pen and pencil first, colour laid digitally over hand ink — and no generative AI is used at any stage of the drawing. That is not offered as a slogan. The evidence is on the home page: the same panel shown at thumbnail, pencil, ink and colour, because a hand leaves a trail a machine doesn't.

Where digital tools do work here, they are named: pages are scanned, colour is laid in software over the inked line, and files are prepared for print digitally. The drawing — the decisions — is the hand's.

The Credits Rule

Every hand on the book is named on the book

Comics are made by more hands than the one on the cover. On every project in the case files, the credits are itemised — writer, pencils, inks, colours, letters — and this studio claims the drawing and only the drawing.

If you commission pencils only, the inker and colourist you place will be credited the same way, and the studio will say so in its own register. Credit is not a courtesy in this trade; it is how the next book gets its team.

Panel D

Terms, in the open

The practice-level terms every engagement starts from. The contract for a specific book is drawn from these, with the publisher's own terms read alongside — where they conflict, the conflict is settled in writing before the first thumbnail.

Table T1 — engagement terms. Plain language, as they will appear in the studio's covering letter.

Rights, art, money, lateness
Reproduction rightsThe fee buys the agreed edition and territory: first print edition and its ebook twin, in the contracted language. Reprints, new editions, translations and merchandise are licensed separately, at rates agreed when they arise — the drawing fee is not a buyout.
CopyrightCopyright in the artwork stays with the artist; the publisher licenses what the contract lists. The writer's copyright in the script is theirs, untouched by anything here.
Original artInked pages remain the artist's property and are sold separately after publication — buyers of originals acquire the object, not reproduction rights. A full inventory of the book's pages is delivered with the final files so nothing is ever in dispute.
Deposit & stages50% of the estimated fee books the slot and is the point the schedule is promised from. The balance is invoiced per approved stage batch on long books, or on delivery for covers and short work.
The late clauseIf the studio delivers late against the contracted schedule, the fee falls by 5% per completed week of lateness, capped at 15% — the clause The Salt Meridian was delivered under. If the client side delivers late (script, approvals, assets), the schedule moves by the same count of days, in writing, at the time.
Kill feeA book cancelled by the client is paid for every approved stage completed, plus 25% of the next stage. Work already drawn may not be finished by another hand without a written release — the credits rule cuts both ways.
