Wedding vows and certificates, book plates, illuminated initials and whole manuscript leaves — made to be kept for centuries, and priced in the open below.

The order of work Five stages, always in this order. The gilding comes before the painting — the reason is in stage three, and it is practical, not traditional for its own sake.

How a leaf is made

Stage I

Design & ruling

The page exists on paper before it exists on vellum. Text block, margins and miniature space are planned on a layout sheet; then the skin is pricked down its fore-edge and ruled with a lead point — the faint plummet lines still visible on medieval pages today.

Stage II

The script

Quills are cut by hand for each job — goose, from the left wing for a right-handed writer, the nib shaped to the hand being written. The ink is made in the studio: iron-gall for permanence, carbon black where a support demands it. Writing happens at a sloped desk, a few lines at a sitting.

Stage III

Gilding — before painting

Where gold will sit, raised gesso — slaked plaster, a little lead white, fish glue, a breath of honey — is laid with a quill and scraped level. Breathed on to wake the glue, the leaf is pressed home and burnished with a dog-tooth agate. Lines too fine for leaf take shell gold: powdered gold in gum arabic, painted like a pigment. Gilding comes first because burnishing presses hard enough to crack fresh paint, loose leaf drifts and sticks to wet colour — and a mistake in gold can still be scraped clean away before any painting is down.

Stage IV

Painting

Pigments are ground by hand and bound in gum arabic, some in egg glair. Ultramarine — ground lapis lazuli — is the costly blue, laid sparingly and kept for the best initials. Vermilion serves the rubrication. Verdigris gives the greens, used with respect: over the centuries it can bite through paper, so it is always isolated from its neighbours.

Stage V

Finishing

Corrections are scraped out with a knife point, the rubrication is checked against the exemplar, and the leaf rests flat under light weights. Every piece leaves the studio with framing and hanging guidance in writing — light is the slow enemy of every pigment named above.

The terms A written proof before any making. A 40% deposit once the proof is approved. The balance on delivery, never before. Two slots open for spring 2027.

Commissions, in the open

It runs in four steps. Enquiry — one email with your text, your occasion and your date. Proof — you receive a sample line in the chosen hand on the chosen support, charged at £40 and deducted from the deposit if the work proceeds; nothing is made in full before you have held the proof. Making — the 40% deposit books the slot, and the five stages above run their course. Delivery — balance on delivery, the piece flat-packed as described below.

Starting prices. What moves them: the support (vellum costs more and forgives less), the amount of raised gold, the complexity of any miniature, and the length of the text.

Sending fragile work. Illuminated pieces travel flat — never rolled — interleaved with acid-free tissue, between boards, double-boxed and insured. Work is sent unframed unless it is collected in person: glass and raised gesso do not survive couriers together, and a good local framer, given the guidance sheet that travels with the piece, will do the rest.
