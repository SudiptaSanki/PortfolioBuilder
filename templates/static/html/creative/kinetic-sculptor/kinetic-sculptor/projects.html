Case 01 · The Quiet Brief

Ward Round — Leeds, 2024

A motorised mobile for a children's hospital atrium, specified first by a noise limit and only afterwards by how it should look. Forty-one elements, one motor, and a maintenance regime a hospital could actually run.

The brief

The atrium sits above a paediatric ward entrance. The trust's conditions came before any aesthetics: no more than 25 dB(A) at 3 m — the level of a quiet whisper — nothing small enough to fall and be swallowed, every surface wipeable with hospital disinfectant, and the whole piece removable by two people with a tower scaffold for ceiling maintenance. A beautiful mobile that failed any one of those would be removed within a year.

The decisions

A stepper motor on a belt drive, chosen over a geared motor because gear mesh was the dominant noise in every prototype; the motor sits in a sealed, acoustically lined housing on the roof steel, driving the arm through a magnetic coupling so there is no shaft penetration to leak sound or dust. Bearings are sealed-for-life. Elements are anodised aluminium over 90 mm in every dimension, tethered with secondary safety wires, and the sway is limited by stops to a 9° envelope that can never reach the glazing.

The outcome

Installed over two nights in March 2024. Measured noise at commissioning: 23 dB(A) at 3 m in the empty atrium — inside the brief with margin, and the figure is engraved on the piece's plate. Two annual services completed; the year-two report records zero unplanned stoppages, one re-balance after the building's HVAC was re-commissioned, and a waiting room that has adopted the mobile as its clock. The client's testimonial is on the home page.

Case 02 · The Weather Brief

Nine Breezes — Cornwall, 2022

A wind-driven garden piece for an exposed headland garden, designed backwards from a survival figure: first decide what the worst night of the decade does to it, then decide what it looks like on a calm one.

The brief

The garden faces the Atlantic with nothing between it and the weather. The clients wanted movement on ordinary days — visible from the kitchen window, alive in a light breeze — and a piece that would still be there, unbent, after the storms that take their fence panels most winters. They also wanted no concrete: the garden is old, and the ground was not to be dug beyond hand tools.

The decisions

The vanes feather: each blade is mounted off-centre on its own bearing, so as wind speed rises the blades progressively turn edge-on and spill the gust instead of fighting it — the piece turns slower in a storm than in a fresh breeze, which is the opposite of most wind sculpture and the whole point. A calibrated shear pin is the deliberate weak link: if a freak load ever exceeds the design case, a £4 pin fails, the head parks itself, and nothing tears. The mast stands on ground screws, hand-driven, no concrete anywhere.

The outcome

Installed June 2022. It has since weathered two named storms with recorded gusts above 120 km/h at the nearest station: no structural damage, the shear pin intact (the feathering did its job first), and one vane repainted under warranty after wind-borne grit stripped its finish — recorded as a materials lesson and the reason outdoor pieces now get a harder coating as standard. The clients report it is the first thing visitors ask about, and the last thing they check before a storm.

Corten steel, stainless, bronze bearings
Span / height
3.6 m / 4.4 m mast

Wind — feathering vanes, shear-pin fuse

130 km/h gusts, feathered

Case 03 · The Honest Brief

Tide Table — Sheffield, 2021

A wave-motion piece over a leisure-centre pool — and the workshop's one public failure. A gearbox failed at month fourteen. This case file exists because how a maker behaves in month fourteen is most of what a commission buys.

The brief

A leisure trust wanted slow wave motion above a teaching pool: 316 stainless and polymer vanes for the chlorinated air, a drive quiet enough not to fight the lifeguards' whistles, and — because it hangs over water — a failure mode that could never drop anything into the pool. The piece was installed in 2021 and ran, at first, exactly as drawn.

What happened

At month fourteen the gearbox — a bought-in unit from a supplier whose batch records later showed a heat-treatment fault — shed a tooth. The piece did the thing it was designed to do: the brake engaged, the wave settled level, nothing fell, nobody was under it. The centre's manager stilled it with the manual procedure from the handover pack and phoned the workshop. The replacement drive was fitted in nine days, under warranty, at the workshop's cost — and the investigation, not the invoice, is the point of this file: the fault was in a bought part, and the design's job was to make that somebody else's bad day instead of a swimmer's.

The outcome

Three changes are now standard on every motorised piece, and they date from this failure: drivetrain parts only from named makers with batch traceability; a service bulletin in the handover pack that lists the failure modes the workshop has actually seen, not generic ones; and the manual stilling procedure rehearsed with the client's staff at handover, not just written down. Tide Table itself has run since the refit with two clean annual services. The trust renewed its service plan for a second five years — which the workshop counts as the real outcome of the case.
