Restoration

What releading does

Before After

Lead is a structural material with a working life of roughly seventy-five to a hundred years. Past that, the came stretches, the cement falls out, and a panel begins to bow under its own weight — the glass itself is usually fine.

Releading takes the window down, records every piece, cleans the glass, and resets it in new lead of the original profile. The colour was never lost. It was waiting behind a century of grime and tired lead.

An honest note on the photographs: the two frames show heraldic windows of the same period — the left as such glass usually reaches the studio, the right after releading — rather than one window twice. Client windows are photographed for their owners' records, not for this page. Figures below are from the St. Brigid's chancel window, releaded in 2023.

The craft

From cartoon to cement

A stained glass panel is drawn twice — once on paper, once in lead — and the second drawing is also its structure. These are the six stages every panel on this page went through.

  1. 1

    Cartoon & cutline

    The design is drawn full-size — the cartoon — and a second copy, the cutline, is taken from it. Every piece of glass is numbered on the cutline before a single sheet is touched.

  2. 2

    Choosing the glass

    Mouth-blown antique glass, full of seeds and striations, for colour that moves; machine-rolled cathedral glass where an even field is wanted. Sheets are chosen against the window light, because that is the light the panel will live in.

  3. 3

    Cutting

    Each piece is scored with a steel-wheel cutter, broken out along the score, grozed with pliers and ground to the line. A piece cut a millimetre fat will crack its neighbour in the leading.

  4. 4

    Painting & silver stain

    Detail is painted in vitreous paint — ground glass and iron oxide — traced, matted and fired at about 1,150°F until it fuses to the surface for good. Silver stain, painted on the back and fired, turns the glass itself yellow to amber: it is the original stain in stained glass.

  5. 5

    Leading & soldering

    Pieces are built up on the cutline inside H-profile lead came, held with horseshoe nails as the panel grows. Every joint is then soldered, on both faces, so the panel holds as one sheet.

  6. 6

    Cementing & hanging

    A stiff cement of linseed oil and whiting is brushed under every lead flange and the panel is scrubbed clean as it sets — this is what makes it weatherproof and rigid. Tie wires are soldered on for the saddle bars, and the panel is installed and sealed.

Commissions

How a window gets made

  1. Site visit & light assessment

    Every commission starts where the glass will hang. I measure the opening, note which hours the sun reaches it, and talk through what the room actually needs — privacy, colour, or simply more light. For restoration, this visit is the condition assessment.

  2. Design & cartoon approval

    You see a scale sketch first, then the full-size cartoon, with the actual glass samples laid over it in your light. Nothing is cut until the cartoon is approved — changes are free on paper and expensive in glass.

  3. Booking & deposit

    A deposit of 40% books the bench slot and buys the glass. The balance is split: 40% when the panel is leaded, 20% on installation. Restoration work is quoted fixed, from the written assessment, before anything is taken down.

  4. Making & installation

    Most commissions take ten to sixteen weeks from cartoon approval. The panel is installed and sealed by the studio, and the cartoon, glass list and photographs are archived — so a repair in fifty years starts from records, not guesswork.
