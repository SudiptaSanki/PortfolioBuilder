Case files

Three pieces, told whole

A case file is the piece's paperwork made public: what was asked, what was decided, what happened — including the cast that failed and the piece that broke. The numbers are the studio's real schedule, the one printed on the Objects page.

Case File 1 — The Long Anneal

Kiln-cast form · Obsidian, Glass Buttes · 48 × 34 × 15 cm · 2025–26 · Private collection, Seattle

Reference photograph — the studio's kiln room, in spirit.

The brief

A collector in Seattle had a stairwell niche, 60 cm tall, that had stood empty for four years because everything she tried in it looked like decoration. She asked for the opposite: one form, dark, that would hold the wall the way a stone holds a field. “A piece of the mountain, indoors.”

The decisions

Kiln-cast obsidian, from a single cleaned block of Glass Buttes rough, so the flow lines in the finished form would run unbroken. At 48 cm the piece sits in Table S1's large row: 16 making days, four anneal cycles of three days each. The mould was built in two parts to let the form come out without forcing it — a forced release is where large casts pick up the stresses that kill them later, quietly, in someone else's house.

The outcome

The first cast cracked on the third day of its first anneal — 19 days in (16 making, 3 annealing) — along a line that led back to a stone inclusion no surface inspection had caught. The cast was written off, the collector was told the same day, with the photograph of the crack, and the clock restarted under the terms printed on the About page: breakage in the making is the studio's loss.

The second cast ran the full schedule — 16 making days, 12 annealing days — and came out whole. Total bench time: 47 days, of which 19 bought nothing but knowledge. The piece was delivered in March 2026. The cracked first cast was not thrown away; it stands in the studio, and it is the first thing a visitor is shown who asks why the annealing column of Table S1 is not shorter.

Case File 2 — Nine Rooms

Series of nine vessels · Borosilicate, torch-worked · Hotel Anselm, Bend · Installed October 2026

Reference photograph — the studio's study for the series stands on the Objects page as Three Vessels.

The brief

A small hotel in Bend was refitting nine rooms, each with a lit niche by the door. The owners wanted one vessel in each niche — “the same, but not identical”, made by one hand, and they wanted to be able to say honestly where the vessels came from: the studio is eleven minutes from the hotel.

The decisions

The work was taken as a series of nine — numbered 1/9 to 9/9 on their bases, as a work order. It was not called a limited edition, because nothing about it is limited except the number of niches; if the hotel builds a tenth room, a tenth vessel can be made, and it will be numbered 10/10 and the record will say so. Each vessel was torch-worked to the same drawing and then allowed its own small deviation in the shoulder — the deviation is the handwriting, and it is why the series was commissioned from a person instead of ordered from a catalogue.

The outcome

Nine vessels were delivered over six weeks. Each is a small vessel by Table S1 — three bench days apiece, thirty days of bench time across the six weeks. One was lost at the torch to thermal shock in the fourth week and remade; the count stayed nine, and the loss went into the studio's scoreboard, where it remains. Installation took two days: the niches were lit by the hotel's own electrician, the vessels set on steel pins by the studio, and two vessels were turned 180 degrees at install because the light read better from the door — a decision recorded on the install sheet, so that if a vessel is ever re-set after cleaning, it goes back the way the light wants it.

Case File 3 — The Break

Ember Vessel · Borosilicate, torch-worked · Sold 2026 · Broken after delivery · Replaced under the printed terms

Reference photograph — broken glass, which is what this file is about.

The brief

There was no brief. There was a purchase: Ember Vessel, from the register, sold to a collector in Portland in the spring of 2026, collected from the studio in its crate and placed on a shelf in a room with north light.

What happened

Five months later a shelf bracket failed in the night. The vessel fell about a metre onto a wood floor and broke at the neck. The collector wrote to the studio with photographs, expecting — her word — an argument. The break was not the studio's fault: not the glass, not the crate, not the anneal. It was a shelf.

The outcome

The terms printed on the About page were written for exactly this: after delivery, repair is quoted first; if a piece cannot be repaired, a replacement is made at half the original price within two years, from the studio's records of the piece. A neck break in borosilicate cannot be repaired to strength, so the vessel was remade — 8 days in all, per Table S1's medium row for a torch vessel: 4 making days and two anneal cycles of two days — and the collector paid half, because the terms said half, not because anyone negotiated. The broken vessel came back to the studio at her insistence. It sits by the bench, and it is the reason the terms are printed where anyone can read them before they buy, instead of explained afterwards.

“She could have called it my shelf's fault, and it was. She split it anyway, because the terms said she would.”

— The collector, Portland, on the replacement of Ember Vessel
