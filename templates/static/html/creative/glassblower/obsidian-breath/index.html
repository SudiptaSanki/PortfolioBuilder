Gather

A gather is a decision made in four seconds. The pipe goes into the furnace door, turns a quarter, and comes out with 1.8 kilograms of glass at the end of it. Too little and the vessel is thin at the base; too much and no breath will move it.

Our black is a reduced soda-lime coloured with iron from crushed basalt. It reads black in the shop and green-black in daylight, which is the point: the colour only exists once the light is behind it.

Furnace 1 · 90 kg continuous tank · 1,150°C working temperature

Marver

The marver is a steel plate and the first shape the glass is given. Rolling the gather on it chills the surface, stiffens the wall, and lets the block push a shoulder into the bubble before the breath arrives.

We work the black hotter than a clear glass, because the iron makes it stiff. The block is soaked in water, the jacks are warm, and the whole shape is decided before the second gather goes on.

Steel marver · cherrywood block · wall held at 3.2–4.5 mm

Blow

Breath is pressure, not volume. A single long breath through the pipe opens the bubble; short pulses steer it. The bubble wants to be round, and every tool we use afterwards is an argument against that.

Vessels are blown into beech moulds turned in the workshop, then finished at the bench with jacks and shears. The lip is widened while the glass still moves, and the foot is applied from a second gather.

Beech moulds, turned in-house · lip opened at 780°C

Anneal

The kiln is the part of the process nobody photographs. A vessel goes in at 560°C, holds while the whole wall equalises, and then cools slowly enough that no part of it is in tension. Cool it too fast and it will not break in the shop. It will break in a kitchen, in two years.

Every piece leaves with its firing card: the schedule it followed, the kiln it was in, and the day it came out. If a vessel ever fails, the card tells us why.

Annealing point 542°C · strain point 510°C · 40°C per hour to 510°C

Every vessel is argued with the kiln

Thick glass cannot be cooled quickly. The outside sets before the inside, and the difference is stored as stress that shows up much later. This is the schedule we run for a wall between three and five millimetres; change the wall and the schedule has to change with it.
