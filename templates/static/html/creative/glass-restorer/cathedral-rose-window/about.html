The window decides how much treatment it needs

We are a two-bench conservation studio in Rouen. We take on four or five windows a year, which is as many as can be documented properly and still be in the studio by the time the scaffold comes down.

Why a conservation studio, not a glazing firm

Most of the glass we treat was made between 1200 and 1260, when a rose was assembled from hundreds of small pieces held in lead. It has survived eight hundred years of weather, revolution, and at least one earlier restoration. Very little of it needs replacing. Almost all of it needs holding.

Came & Cobalt was founded in 2003 by Marguerite Vasseur after eleven years in the ateliers of the DRAC Normandie. The studio works only on historic glass: no new windows, no domestic commissions, no reproduction panels. That restriction is deliberate. A bench that also sells new work is tempted to see a medieval panel as raw material.

Every intervention is reversible. Adhesives are chosen so a future conservator can undo them. New lead is matched to the original width and profile rather than to a modern standard section. Nothing is re-fired, re-painted or toned to blend.

“A window that has lasted eight centuries does not need to be improved. It needs to be understood, and then left alone as far as possible.”

How a window is read

Before anything is lifted, the window is drawn in elevation at 1:10 and every panel is numbered to the Corpus Vitrearum convention. Those numbers follow the panel through the workshop, the crate, the report and the archive, so a fragment can be found again in fifty years.

Raking light shows the surface; transmitted light shows the glass. Both are photographed, rectified to the drawing, and kept as the baseline against which any future change is measured. We photograph the whole window, then each panel, then each loss.

Condition is recorded in four separate registers, because the failures are not the same failure: glass (fractures, losses, corrosion, paint loss), lead (fatigue, open joints, loss of section), ferramenta (corrosion, deflection, missing saddle bars), and fabric (mortar, putty, ventilation, water paths). A report that merges them into one “poor condition” grade tells a fabric committee nothing it can act on.

What we will not do

We will not re-lead a window that only needs its joints repaired. We will not replace medieval glass with new glass to make a composition read more clearly, and we will not move a panel to a museum case when it is stable in its opening.

We will not accept a commission that requires the window to be treated inside a fixed programme with no room for what the survey finds. Rose windows are not predictable; a schedule that assumes they are puts pressure on the person holding the glass.

We will not sign off a treatment without the monitoring data. Isothermal glazing changes the environment around the glass, and the only honest way to know whether it worked is to leave a datalogger behind and read it a year later.
