Every commission has a melt window

These are five pieces with the numbers that decided them: the weight, the room, the hours the detail had to hold, and how we got them in the door.

Saint Paul, Minnesota

Winter Carnival arch

An outdoor gateway for the carnival's east entrance: 4.6 m wide, 3.4 m high, assembled from 38 blocks and welded on site in −14 °C. The arch had to survive fourteen days of public traffic and two warm afternoons.

We built it in three sections in the shop, trucked them over at −18 °C, and welded the joints outdoors with slush. The feet were set into a snow base to spread the load, and the whole thing was re-squared each morning.

Minneapolis, Minnesota

Bar wall, Cedar Lake

A 6.1 m run behind a bar, carved with three niches for bottles and a lit channel at the base. The room ran at 21 °C with a door to a patio that opened all evening, so the wall was built 220 mm thick at the face.

The niches were carved with a die grinder and finished with a torch to make them clear. We left a melt watch on site until midnight and cut back one niche that had started to sag at the top edge.

Chicago, Illinois

Launch piece, Wicker Park

A product launch in a warm gallery: one 900 kg piece, spotlit from below, standing where guests would walk within a metre of it. The carving had to read as a brand mark from across the room and as a facet study up close.

We cut the silhouette with a chainsaw, then finished every plane with a flat chisel so the light broke consistently. Warm LEDs were kept 1.2 m from the surface and the piece was on display for five hours.

Duluth, Minnesota

Wedding centrepiece

A 180 kg piece for a December wedding, set on a pedestal in the middle of a dance floor. The couple wanted the shape to stay readable in photographs taken over six hours, which meant a thick profile and a shallow engraving rather than deep carving.

Delivered at 15:00 for a 17:30 service, on a drip tray hidden inside the pedestal, with the room at 20 °C. It was still sharp in the last photograph.

Madison, Wisconsin

Frozen table for a tasting

A 3 m table poured and frozen in one piece, with a channel down the centre for oysters and a carved lip so plates would not slide. The kitchen needed the surface to stay below freezing for a two-hour service.

We froze the table in two halves and welded them in the truck, then laid a chilled glycol line under the tray to hold the surface. It was the only commission that year where the ice was not the hardest part of the logistics.
