Silas Merritt

Cabinet maker first, repairman second, guitar maker third — in that order, and the order matters.

Silas Merritt came to guitar making through furniture. He spent ten years building cabinets and tables in western North Carolina, learning the two lessons that govern everything since: wood moves, and joinery is either honest or it is decoration.

From 2009 to 2011 he apprenticed with a guitar maker in Santa Cruz, California, building steel-string acoustics in the California style — light, responsive, and voiced by tapping rather than by template. Then came six years on the repair bench of a Nashville vintage shop, where the work is old Martins and Gibsons with their tops off: neck resets, cracked sides, bridges lifting, frets worn to the board. Repair teaches what building alone cannot — exactly which decisions a guitar forgives, and which it collects on thirty years later.

He opened his own workshop in Asheville in 2016. No. 1 was a Sitka and mahogany OM, and the numbering has run unbroken since: No. 48 is on the bench now. The workshop builds eight to ten guitars a year and keeps a repair bench open to local players, because a maker who never sees his old work come back is a maker guessing.

The approach has not changed since the apprenticeship: choose the wood for the player, voice the top by hand, keep the finish thin, and write everything down. A guitar should still be explainable — and repairable — by whoever owns it in fifty years.

Wood, kept properly

Half the workshop is the wood store, and it is run like a cellar. Top and back sets are racked with stickers between them for airflow, dated in pencil on the end grain, and left for at least two years before they are considered. The room is held at 45–50% relative humidity year-round — the same air the finished guitars will live in at the bench.

The racks hold the usual spruces and cedar, Indian rosewood and Honduran mahogany, black walnut from North Carolina sawmills, and big-leaf maple. In one locked cabinet sits the small documented stock of Brazilian rosewood, bought from a retired maker’s estate with its paperwork; it is spent rarely, and only where a brief genuinely calls for it.

Nothing in the store is bought on colour. Sets are chosen by tap tone and stiffness at the merchant’s warehouse, and rejected far more often than they are bought.

Keeping a guitar healthy

Most of the damage seen on the repair bench is not bad luck — it is dry air, heat, and small problems left alone. Five habits prevent nearly all of it.

  1. Hold humidity at 45–55%

    Wood shrinks as it dries. Below 40% relative humidity a top can sink, frets sprout from the board edges, and cracks follow. Use a case humidifier through the heating season and a small hygrometer in the case — they cost little and answer honestly.

  2. Keep it out of the car

    A guitar in a summer trunk or against a winter radiator is being cooked or frozen in glue-softening, finish-checking air. If a guitar arrives cold, let it warm up inside its closed case for a few hours before opening it.

  3. Change strings before they give up

    Phosphor bronze strings last roughly six to ten weeks of regular playing. When a set stops intonating cleanly at the 12th fret, it is done, however bright it still sounds. Wipe strings down after playing; hands are the other half of string life.

  4. Expect the action to breathe

    A guitar’s action rises a little in humid summers and falls in dry winters. That is normal movement, not a fault. Many players keep a summer and a winter saddle; the workshop cuts the second one free with any setup.

  5. Bring small problems in early

    Buzzing that was not there last month, action creeping up, a bridge corner lifting, a crack along the grain — each is a small job in its first month and a large one in its second year. A crack repaired early is cleated and nearly invisible; left alone, it becomes a story.

  6. When to seek a setup

    Once a year is a good rhythm for a played guitar, and immediately if the guitar has moved climate, changed string gauge, or started fighting you. A setup is cheap. A neck reset, postponed five years too long, is not.
