Restorations, told in full

A polish is a chain of decisions, most of them about what not to do. These three are written the way the work is recorded for owners: what arrived, what was decided and why, and what the blade went home with. Client blades are photographed for their owners only — the photographs here are details of blades and bench work, captioned for what they show.

Case 01 · Private commission

The Farmhouse Katana

A family's blade, kept in a farmhouse store-room for forty years, and a hamon nobody living had ever seen.

Blade
Katana, 69.8 cm
Attribution
Unsigned (mumei); family attribution unverified
Work
Full shitaji & shiage, hadori finish
Duration
11 weeks on the bench, 2025

What arrived

The blade came wrapped in a blanket, inside its shirasaya, inside a tea chest. The scabbard had done its work — the wood was sound — but the steel carried blooming surface rust across both faces, old oil varnished to a brown film, and three small nicks in the edge. The tang was untouched and honestly aged, which is exactly what a polisher hopes to find: nobody had been at this blade with a wheel or a stone.

Decisions

The assessment was blunt: the rust was shallow, the geometry underneath was intact, and a full foundation polish was justified — one of the minority of store-room blades of which that is true. The nicks came out within the kaisei stage without touching the blade's lines. For the finish, the owner chose hadori over sashikomi: the point of the work was a hamon his grandfather had described, and he wanted it legible to his own grandchildren.

Outcome

Under the uchigumori the hamon appeared in the second week of foundation work — a bright, drifting line that matched the family description closely enough that the owner asked for the stage photographs to be printed. The blade returned with its full photographic record, a care kit, and one instruction repeated twice: hands never touch the steel. It is now the centre of the family's New Year display.

Case 02 · Private commission

The Returned Wakizashi

Carried overseas by one generation, kept unopened by the next, and brought to the bench with a brief for restraint.

Blade
Wakizashi, 44.2 cm
Attribution
Signed; reading supplied with the assessment
Work
Edge-chip correction, conservation shiage
Duration
7 weeks on the bench, 2025

What arrived

A signed wakizashi in tired mountings, the blade dulled by decades of still air and one careless drawer. Three chips along the edge — ha-kobore — the largest a millimetre deep. The polish beneath was old but skilful, and most of the blade's original surface survived under it.

Decisions

A full foundation polish would have erased that surviving surface to cure a local problem, so it was refused — by the polisher, in the assessment, before the owner had committed to anything. Instead: geometry correction limited to the chipped section, blended by hand until the repair disappears into the line, then a conservation shiage that kept the blade's age visible in the ji. The rotted shirasaya was replaced through a sayashi I work with; scabbard-making is its own craft, and I do not pretend otherwise.

Outcome

The chips are gone to any eye that is not looking for them with a loupe and the stage photographs side by side. The blade went home in its new shirasaya with a written handling protocol for the family — who may hold it, how, and what to do in the first hour if it is ever dropped. The owner writes that it has been shown, carefully, twice since its return.

Case 03 · Museum commission

The Museum Tantō

A regional history museum, a corroding tantō, and a brief with one word underlined: stabilise.

Blade
Tantō, 28.6 cm
Attribution
Unsigned; museum catalogue entry unchanged
Work
Rust stabilisation, minimal conservation polish
Duration
5 weeks including documentation, 2024

What arrived

The tantō arrived by courier in its museum mount, with the accession file: found in a store-room clear-out, catalogued, and then left for a decade in a cabinet whose humidity nobody had been logging. Active red rust was spreading along one face — the kind that keeps eating steel while a committee decides.

Decisions

The museum's brief was conservation, not restoration, and the work followed it strictly. Active corrosion was reduced mechanically and stabilised; sound old surface was left exactly as found. No hadori, no beautifying — nothing that would make the blade read as younger or finer than the record says it is. Every stage was photographed and written up to the museum's own documentation standard, so a future conservator can see precisely what was done, and reverse the reasoning if not the work.

Outcome

The rust is inactive and the blade is stable in store at a specified humidity, with a one-page handling protocol for curators — gloves, support, no bare-hand contact, inspection every twelve months. The tantō has since been displayed for one exhibition season, the first time it has been shown in the museum's history.
