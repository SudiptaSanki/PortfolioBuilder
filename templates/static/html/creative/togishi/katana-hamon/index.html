The craft

Two stages, ten stones, no shortcuts

A polish is two crafts in sequence. Shitaji, the foundation, rebuilds the blade's geometry on graded waterstones. Shiage, the finish, is done with fingertip stones and powders, and brings out what the foundation made possible — the grain of the steel and the hamon along the edge.

  1. Kaisei 修正砥

    The correction stone. Rust, shallow damage and distorted geometry are worked out here. A blade that skips this stage carries its problems into every later one.

  2. Binsui 備水砥

    Removes the kaisei scratch pattern and settles the surface, until the blade reads as one continuous plane again.

  3. Chu-nagura 中名倉

    The middle nagura. The blade's lines sharpen; the shinogi ridge is re-established, crisp and straight.

  4. Koma-nagura 細名倉

    The fine nagura. The last of the foundation scratch is chased out, section by section, working toward the uchigumori.

  5. Uchigumori 内曇

    Ha-to across the edge, ji-to across the body. Under this stone the hamon shows itself for the first time — and tells the polisher how much finish the blade can honestly take.

  1. Hazuya 葉ずや

    Flakes of uchigumori fixed to paper and worked with the fingertip. The edge is refined in small sections — the slowest work on the bench, and the least forgiving.

  2. Jizuya 地ずや

    Harder fingertip stones draw the hada — the folded grain of the steel — out of the body of the blade.

  3. Nugui 拭い

    Kanahada powder in clove oil, carried with ibota wax, wiped over the ji. It darkens and evens the body of the blade, so the hamon's brightness has something quiet to stand against.

  4. Hadori 羽取り

    Selective hazuya work whitens the hamon itself, until its activities — nie, nioi, the drifting pattern of the hardening line — can be read. Where a blade asks for quiet, I finish in sashikomi instead, without the whitening.

  5. Migaki 磨き

    The shinogi-ji and the spine are burnished with a steel needle to a still mirror — the only shine on the blade that comes from pressure rather than stone.

Commissions

How a blade comes to the bench

Nothing is polished on a first impression. Assessment comes first, in writing, and a third of all enquiries end there — with advice, a referral to a smith, or the honest news that a blade should be left alone.

  1. Photographs first

    Before anything travels, send photographs: the whole blade on both sides, the tang on both sides, close-ups of any damage, in plain daylight. Most questions can be answered from these.

  2. Inspection & written assessment

    If the photographs justify it, the blade comes to Kamakura — in person, or by insured post. You receive a written assessment: condition, what a polish can and cannot do, a fixed price, and your place in the queue. The fee is credited in full against the work.

  3. The work

    A deposit of one third confirms the booking. The blade is photographed at every stage, and the full record returns with it.

  4. Return

    Balance on completion, before return. Blades travel in their shirasaya inside a padded crate, fully insured, signature on delivery. A blade leaving Japan needs its registration and export permission in order — I prepare the paperwork with your agent.

Taken on

  • Antique blades in honest condition — rust and tired polish included
  • Family blades, with their stories; provenance notes are kept with the record
  • Museum and school collections, documentation agreed in advance
  • Blades being prepared for shinsa appraisal — polished to be read, never flattered

Not taken on

  • Blades that need a smith first — bent, cracked through the edge, or flawed beyond what polish can hold
  • Modern machine-made reproductions; the stones cannot give them what they never had
  • Any request to remove, deepen or alter a signature (mei)
  • Authentication or valuation — that is the work of shinsa panels, not of a polisher
  • Any polish asked to make a blade look other than it is

Stated plainly

What a polish cannot do

Trust in this trade is built on limits. These are mine.

It cannot authenticate

A polisher reads a blade's surface, not its paperwork. Attribution and papers belong to shinsa appraisal panels such as the NBTHK. I will say when a blade is ready for one — I will not predict its result.

It cannot add value

A good polish can reveal worth that was hidden; it cannot create worth. Any polisher who promises a return on investment is selling something other than polishing.

It cannot undo damage

Rust that has eaten through the hamon, a crack in the edge, steel polished away by earlier hands — these can be softened, sometimes stabilised, never erased. The assessment says which, before you pay for the work.

Begin with photographs.

No blade travels until it has been read. Send photographs and the story of the blade, and expect a straight answer.
