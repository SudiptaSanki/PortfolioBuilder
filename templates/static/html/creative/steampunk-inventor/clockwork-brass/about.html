How the workshop came about

The Sheaf Works was a cutlery rolling mill before it was a workshop, and the floor still slopes toward the old drain. I took two rooms here in 1997 with a Boley lathe, a box of files and a longcase movement in pieces on a tea tray. The first thing I made was a replacement escape wheel for that clock; the second was a filing jig, because the first wheel had taken eleven hours and I could not face doing it again badly.

The wheel engine came in 2001 from a closing workshop in Rotherham — an 1893 machine with a dividing plate of 96 holes and a cutter spindle that runs true enough to cut a wheel you cannot see the joins in. It is still the most used machine here. Most of what the workshop does is not cutting new wheels, though. It is reading a movement that someone else built in 1830 and working out where the last three repairs went wrong.

The restorations pay for the automata. That is the honest arrangement, and it means I can say no to a commission that would be a piece of scenery rather than a machine.

Why reversibility matters

A clock is a document as well as an object. Every file stroke removes evidence of how it was made. So the workshop works to three rules: nothing original is cut away, every new part is stamped with a small CB and dated, and every intervention is written into a service book that stays with the piece.

Where a part is missing, I make it to the same principle as the original — same tooth form, same material, same finish — rather than to a modern shortcut that will outlast the rest of the movement and make the next repair harder.

Four principles

  1. Nothing original is removed. If a part is broken, it is conserved and the repair is made beside it.
  2. Every new part is marked. A CB stamp and a date under the arbor, small enough to need a loupe.
  3. No modern materials in old movements. No epoxy, no superglue, no aluminium where brass belongs.
  4. The piece must run as designed. A clock that keeps time by accident is not restored.

Teaching and the trade

Two apprentices come in on Fridays: one from the local college, one who came to have a bracket clock repaired and stayed. They start on bushes and screws, and they are not allowed near a customer’s movement for the first year.

The trade is small and getting smaller. There are perhaps forty people in Britain who can cut a wheel by hand to a good standard, and most of them are over sixty. Taking on apprentices is not charity; it is the only way the bench is still here in twenty years.

I also teach a two-day depthing course twice a year at the Kelham Island museum, which is mostly attended by engineers who want to understand why a clock is not a gearbox.
