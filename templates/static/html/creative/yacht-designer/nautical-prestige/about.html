§ 01

The office

One naval architect.
No sales department.

Aoife Byrne designs yachts the way surveyors read them: from the numbers outward.

She took her MEng in Ship Science at the University of Southampton, then spent six years in the design office of a Solent production builder — the last two signing off stability books for boats of 15 to 24 metres. It was good training in how a design office works, and better training in how its numbers get bent: a range quoted at the speed nobody cruises at, a displacement that quietly excludes fuel, water and the options list, a stability category presented as a promise about weather.

She set up on her own in Cowes in 2016 with one rule, which is now the office’s whole method: no figure leaves this office without its conditions attached — load case, sea state, reserve, and the date it was measured. Fourteen designs are in the water. Their predicted-versus-measured tables are the practice’s real portfolio, and any client can ask for all of them.

The office takes on three to four projects a year: new designs of 12–40 metres, weight audits and refit engineering on older boats, and owner’s representation at build. It does not do production-boat styling, and it does not take commission from anyone it specifies equipment from. Current state: one design in build (Halcyon, Sheet 01), one design slot open from spring 2027.

What a year actually costs

The table design sites

Ownership cost is the figure prestige marketing hides hardest, because it is the one that decides whether the second season happens. These are worked annual figures for three sizes of yacht, South Coast UK berthing, at the stated use. Your marina, insurer and cruising ground will move them — the structure of the table won’t change.

The old rule of thumb — budget 10% of the boat’s value per year — survives because it is roughly right once depreciation and the occasional five-figure surprise are included. If a salesperson shows you a running-cost figure under 4% of value with no assumptions attached, you are reading the same kind of number as a range quoted with an empty reserve.

§ 03

What the brochure figures hide

A buyer’s guide, free of charge —
use it on our competitors.

  1. Range, quoted at the speed nobody uses

    Range rises steeply as speed falls, so the printed figure is worked at displacement speed with the tank run dry. Ask: at what speed, with what reserve? On Meridian the honest spread is 1,371 nm at 5.5 knots against 350 at 9.5 — both true, neither sufficient alone. Try it yourself on Sheet 01.

  2. Displacement, quoted lightship

    “Lightship” means no fuel, no water, no stores, no crew, no tender, and none of the options list. A cruising boat in service commonly displaces 8–15% more, and every performance figure moves with it. Ask: which load case, and was it inclined or calculated?

  3. Top speed, measured on a boat that doesn’t exist

    Trials are run light, clean-bottomed, in flat water, sometimes with the builder’s smallest propeller choice aboard. Your boat, loaded for a season with a month’s growth on the hull, will not do it. Ask: for the trial report, not the brochure — the load case is printed on it.

  4. “Sleeps ten”

    Count the berths that are usable at sea, in a seaway, by an adult: not the saloon table conversion, not the pilot berth behind the chart table. Then count again for the boat as you will actually crew her. Ask: for the general arrangement drawing, and count on that.

  5. The design category, presented as weather

    CE Category A certifies a design standard for construction and stability. It is not a promise that a given crew, on a given day, in a given ocean, will be comfortable or safe — seamanship is not certified. Ask: for the stability book, and read the AVS and the downflooding points yourself.
