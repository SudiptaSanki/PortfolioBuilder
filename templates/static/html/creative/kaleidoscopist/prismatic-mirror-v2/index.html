The Mirror Bench · Frome, Somerset · Est. 2009

Heirloom kaleidoscopes, built like instruments.

A kaleidoscope is an argument that symmetry is a kind of honesty. Two mirrors, set at a true angle, cannot invent anything — they can only repeat what is put in front of them, exactly.

Plate 2 · The Optics, Printed

Two mirrors, and one division sum

Every scope on this bench is specified the same way: a mirror angle, a sector count, a point count. The relation is printed in full below and the planner runs on nothing else. If a figure in a case file ever disagrees with this page, the case file is wrong and the bench wants to be told.

The printed relation. For a two-mirror system with the mirrors set at angle θ, the sectors are m = 360 ÷ θ. The pattern closes only when m is a whole number. When m is even, the field has m points. When m is odd, the object and its mirror image fall out of step at the join and the eye reads them in pairs — the field has 2 × m points. When m is not whole, there is no field: the last image lands out of register and the join shows as a doubled point.

A three-mirror system is a different instrument: three mirrors in a triangle — 60° / 60° / 60° on this bench — reflect without limit and tile the whole field in a hexagonal repeat. There is no point count, and the planner says so rather than inventing one.

The Mirror System Planner

Mirror system
From the printed relation

360° ÷ 30° = 12 sectors exactly — the pattern closes.

Field: 12 points.

12 is even, so the object and its mirror image alternate evenly around the field and no point is doubled at the join. This is the system in the viewer above.

Cell character — dry cell: loose lampworked glass, beads and wire tumble as the cell turns. Shapes stay separate, points stay sharp, and no two turns give the same arrangement.

Plate 5 · Fees

What moves the price

Base prices, from the printed list: collector scopes from £1,450; one-off commissions from £2,600; museum pieces from £4,800. Four things move a price away from its base, in this order.

  • 1 · The mirror systemA three-mirror set takes roughly twice the alignment time of a two-mirror set, and alignment is the bench's scarcest hour.
  • 2 · The cell workLampworked contents are made, or commissioned from named glassworkers, piece by piece. An oil fill adds a day and a seal that must be perfect.
  • 3 · The barrel and its engravingFigured timber or brass, turned on the bench; engraving is cut by hand and charged at the bench rate, by the hour.
  • 4 · The formatLength and aperture set the mirror size. Museum format is cut from plate glass stock, not from the bench's standard blanks.
