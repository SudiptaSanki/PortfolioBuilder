About · The Maker, the Method, the Terms

An instrument maker who chose the smallest instrument

Ansel Whitaker trained in optical instrument repair — telescopes, binoculars, theodolite glass — before building his first kaleidoscope in 2009. The bench has made them, and only them, since.

The Story

Repair first, making second

Eleven years of repair work teaches a person one thing above all: how instruments fail, and how rarely they fail where the brochure said they were strong. Coatings delaminate. Cheap mirror stock — the foil-faced board sold to the toy trade — clouds from the edges inward. Cells sealed with the wrong compound sweat, then bloom.

The first scope Ansel built, in the winter of 2009, was built out of that repair bench knowledge: first-surface mirrors or nothing, glass in the cell or the cell's contents printed as what they are, and a barrel a future repairer could open without destroying. It was bought by the optician who had trained him, which the bench still counts as its most demanding sale.

His position on the toy end of the trade is a matter of record on this bench: a foil-mirror scope with acrylic “gems” is a pattern that dies within the year, sold at the exact age when a child is learning whether beautiful things last. This bench makes about forty scopes a year, of glass and brass, and signs every one inside the barrel — where a repairer, decades from now, will find the name and know who to blame.

The bench also repairs other makers' scopes — 47 to date — under the ethics set out in Case File 3: measure first, replace only what has failed, never open a sealed cell, never write on the instrument.

The Working Page

How a scope runs, from glass to signature

Five stages, in the order the bench performs them. A scope does not move to the next stage until the current one is right; the fourth stage is where most of the time goes, and the fifth is where scopes are most often sent back.

  1. Mirror cutting and alignment

    First-surface mirror stock is cut to length and angle, then set in a jig and aligned against a divided circle until the test field closes — the join invisible at the specified angle, whether that is the bench's 30° or a client's chosen geometry from the planner.

  2. Body turning

    The barrel is turned — brass on the lathe, or figured timber bored and lined — and the eyepiece and cell housing are fitted. Engraving, where commissioned, is cut now, while the metal can still be re-turned if a letter goes wrong.

  3. Cell making

    The object cell is assembled: lampworked glass made at the bench or commissioned from named glassworkers and credited, dichroic glass named as coated glass, filings weighed. Oil cells are filled and sealed at this stage and rested for a week before the scope is closed, so a failing seal fails on the bench and not in a collector's cabinet.

  4. Assembly and closing

    Mirrors, barrel and cell come together. The field is checked at arm's length and at the eye, in daylight and under a lamp, because a scope lives in both. Alignment is adjusted in minutes of arc until the join cannot be found by someone who knows where to look for it.

  5. Final inspection and the signature

    The finished scope is turned slowly through a full circle of the cell, twice. If any point doubles, anywhere, it goes back to stage four. If it passes, the bench signs inside the barrel, enters the piece in the bench book with its full plate — system, angle, points, cell, materials — and the register entry on the Home page is written from that line of the book.

The Honesty Page

What this site cannot show you, and what the plates will not say

A screen is not an eyepiece. The viewer on the Home page is drawn geometry — the bench's own SVG, built from the same 30° relation as the planner. It turns when you turn it and its twelve points agree with the printed table. What it cannot do is be glass. Through a real scope the elements have depth and weight; dichroic glass changes colour as it tilts; an oil cell keeps drifting after your hand stops. The screen version is an approximation and is labelled as one wherever it appears, because a bench that prints its mirror angles to the minute has no business pretending a drawing is a view.

Materials are named as they are. Brass is brass. Glass is glass. Dichroic glass is glass with a coated surface, and the plates say “coated glass” because that is what it is. Nothing acrylic has ever been sold from this bench as crystal, and nothing foil-faced has ever been sold as a mirror — the scoreboard on the Home page keeps that count at zero, in public, where it can be checked against every plate the bench has published.

Photographs are reference photographs. The pictures on these pages were taken by the photographers credited beneath them, of their own subjects, and are never presented as this bench's scopes, clients or premises. Where a piece may not be pictured — a family's inheritance, a museum's gallery — the file says so and prints its plate instead.

Engagement Terms

The terms, before you write

Printed here so that the first conversation can be about the scope, not about the small print.

BookingBench slots are booked by the month. The book is currently open for spring commissions; repair work is taken all year and quoted within a week of the piece arriving.
Deposit50% of the agreed figure on booking, against the printed list or the written quote. The balance falls due when the scope is collected or posted.
Making time10–14 weeks from the booked slot for commissions and collector scopes; museum pieces by agreement. The slot is a promise: if the bench runs late, the client hears it from the bench first, with the reason.
PricingThe estimator on the Home page adds up the printed list. A firm quote follows the cell conversation and has never yet exceeded the estimate for the same specification.
DeliveryCollection from the bench by appointment, or insured courier at cost, packed as the bench packs mirrors: cell removed, wrapped separately, the plate card in the lid.
CareFirst-surface mirrors are cleaned by the maker only — the first clean, at year one, is included in every commission's price. Cells are sealed for the life of the piece; if a seal fails, the bench re-seals its own work free, for ever. Other makers' scopes are serviced under the repair ethics in Case File 3.
