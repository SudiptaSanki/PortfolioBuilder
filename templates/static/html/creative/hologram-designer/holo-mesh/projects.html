Case files · 01–03

Three builds, three different physics.

A light-field panel, an additive headset and a retail display walk into the same studio. Each one broke a different assumption — here is what the brief asked, what the hardware allowed, and what shipped.

Fig. 02 · Case file 01

Meridian Light-Field Interface

A touchless interface for a 27″ light-field display presenting a sculpture collection — visitors browse pieces in volume, without touching anything, from a step away.

The brief

The gallery wanted visitors to rotate and inspect twelve sculptures on a Looking Glass 27″ light-field display. Conservation ruled out touching the screen; hygiene ruled out shared controllers. Visitors stand 0.6–1.2 m away, sessions last under two minutes, and admissions staff — not technicians — reset the exhibit each morning.

The decisions

The spike measured a usable viewing cone of 42°; content at the edges ghosts for off-axis viewers, so every control was kept inside the central 30° and nothing important ever sits at the frame's edge. Pinch-to-select failed for one in five testers — and for nearly everyone wearing gloves or with limited hand mobility — so selection is a dwell: hold a hand steady for 800 ms, with a visible progress ring. Text was set at a 28 px minimum at one metre, and scenes were capped at 84k triangles to hold frame rate on the display's own PC.

The outcome

The exhibit ran eleven weeks and roughly 41,000 visitors. Mis-selections stayed under 2% of sessions; the morning reset takes staff under a minute, on one laminated card. The gallery has commissioned the same interface pattern for a second room — the strongest verdict a case file can carry.

Regional art gallery
Platform
Looking Glass 27″ light-field
Deliverable
Unity build + glTF scenes
Budget held

Fig. 03 · Case file 02

Northline Maintenance Overlay

A hands-free HoloLens 2 overlay that walks pump technicians through a fourteen-step seal replacement — in a dim, steel, signal-dead plant room, wearing gloves.

The brief

An equipment OEM's technicians service pumps from paper manuals and memory. The overlay had to work at roughly 80 lux, with nitrile gloves, with zero network — the plant room is effectively a steel box — and it had to survive a full shift on one battery strategy or a hot-swap routine.

The decisions

Everything ships on the device: no cloud, no streaming, no account sign-in at the pump. World-locked steps drift — measured at up to 4 cm over a shift — so each pump carries a printed anchor card the technician re-registers in five seconds at the start of a job; drift stops being a failure mode and becomes a habit. Because the HoloLens display is additive, it cannot show black: the palette was rebuilt light-first and tested at 80 lux, and CAD models were decimated with the OEM to a 100k-triangle scene budget.

The outcome

In the client's own trial, the fourteen-step seal replacement fell from 26 minutes to 17, with two fewer call-backs to senior technicians per week. The build has since rolled out to three sites — each one onboarded with the same anchor cards and the same five-second habit.

Client
Industrial equipment OEM
Platform
HoloLens 2, offline
Deliverable
Unity / MRTK build + anchor kit
Budget held
≤100k tris · 60 fps · 0 network

Fig. 04 · Case file 03

Velora Volumetric Configurator

A furniture retailer's sofas, viewable in volume on an in-store light-field display — and, honestly, in 2D and AR at home, where the display isn't.

The brief

The retailer wanted customers to configure sofas — model, fabric, feet — on a Looking Glass Go in six stores, then continue at home. The unspoken assumption was that the home experience would be "the same, but smaller." It couldn't be: almost no home has a light-field display.

The decisions

Two honest formats instead of one fake one: volumetric in-store, and at home a USDZ Quick Look AR view on phones that support it, with a 2D turntable everywhere else — each labelled as what it is. Then the colour problem: the display shifts saturated fabrics by about ΔE 6, enough to sell a rust sofa and deliver a brown one. The configurator was limited to the 24 fabrics that held their colour, and physical swatch cards, matched to the display, sit beside it in every store.

The outcome

In-store dwell ran 3.2 times the old print-catalogue corner. In the pilot quarter, returns on configured sofas fell 11% on the client's own figures — the swatch cards doing quietly what no display could. The format split has become the retailer's standard brief for spatial work.

Furniture retailer, 6 stores
Platform
Looking Glass Go · USDZ · web
Deliverable
glTF / USDZ package + web viewer
Budget held
46 MB package · 24 fabrics passed
