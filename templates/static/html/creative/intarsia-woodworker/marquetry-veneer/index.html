The veneer palette

The palette is the picture

A marquetry picture has no paint. Light, shade and distance all come from choosing the right leaf — and from which way its grain is laid. These are the woods I reach for first.

How a piece is made

From a drawing to a picture in wood

  1. The cartoon

    Every piece starts as a full-size drawing. Each shape is numbered, and the direction its grain must run is marked in pencil — grain is the shading, so this decision cannot be left to the bench.

  2. Choosing the palette

    Leaves are pulled from the flitch and held against the cartoon in daylight. A landscape may need forty leaves from nine species; a box lid, six leaves and patience.

  3. Cutting

    Repeating patterns are packet-cut — several leaves pinned in a stack and sawn together. One-off pictures are cut piece by piece, each shape sawn into its neighbour's place (the window method), on a fretsaw with 2/0 blades or with a knife for the finest work.

  4. Hot-sand shading

    Edges that need shadow are dipped for a few seconds in hot silver sand. The scorch gives a soft, permanent shade line that no stain can imitate — and cannot be undone, which concentrates the mind.

  5. Assembly and laying

    The picture is assembled face-up and held with gummed paper tape, then laid onto its ground with hot hide glue and a veneer hammer, and pressed flat under cauls until the glue cools.

  6. Scraping and finish

    The surface is levelled with a cabinet scraper — sandpaper would tear the finest joints — then finished with French polish for furniture or hard-wax oil for panels that will be handled.

Commissions

How a commission runs

No surprises is the whole system: you approve the drawing, then a sample of the actual veneers, before the piece itself is cut.

  1. Stage 1 — Conversation

    We talk through the subject, the room it will live in, and the size. Photographs of the space help more than measurements at this stage. No charge.

  2. Stage 2 — Design

    I draw the cartoon at full size and we settle the composition. Design fee £180, credited in full against the finished piece.

  3. Stage 3 — Veneer proof

    You receive a sample panel — a corner of the picture, cut in the actual veneers — to live with for a week before deciding. Colours in wood are a commitment.

  4. Stage 4 — Making

    A 40% deposit confirms the booking. Making takes most of the quoted time; I send bench photographs at cutting, assembly and finishing.

  5. Stage 5 — Delivery

    Panels are crated and insured for courier delivery, or delivered and hung in person within 60 miles of Bath. Furniture inlay is fitted on site.

Restoration

What can be saved, and what cannot

Antique marquetry fails in predictable ways: veneer lifts and blisters, stringing drops out, sunlight bleaches one side of a top, and someone, at some point, varnishes over all of it. Most of that is repairable. Re-laying, re-cutting losses in matched period veneer and reviving an original finish are the everyday work of the bench.

Two honest limits. Veneer that has powdered under water damage cannot be re-laid — it must be replaced, and the replacement is recorded. And new work is never artificially aged to deceive: it is toned to sit quietly beside the old, and every replaced piece is listed in the treatment report that stays with the furniture.
