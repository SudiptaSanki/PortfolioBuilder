The first headset

In 2014 I put on a borrowed DK2 in a friend’s flat in Berlin and immediately fell over. Not from nausea — from the floor. The demo had placed the ground plane twelve centimetres below where my body insisted it was, and my knees had already decided to trust my body over my eyes. I spent the rest of that evening not looking at the graphics but at the gap.

I have been chasing that gap for ten years. Most VR problems that get called “immersion” are really calibration problems: the floor, the IPD, the latency, the scale of a doorframe, the fact that a hand is rendered where the tracking system thinks it is rather than where it is.

A player will forgive a low-poly tree. They will not forgive a floor that moves.

What I actually do on a team

I am a gameplay engineer who takes ownership of the parts that touch the player’s body: locomotion, grabbing, hands, comfort systems, camera rigs, and the frame budget those things live inside. I am not the person to ask for a beautiful vegetation shader. I am the person to ask why the beautiful vegetation shader costs 6 ms of your 11.

On small teams I also do build engineering, device provisioning and the unglamorous work of getting a Quest to boot into the right scene without a laptop attached. On larger teams I have led gameplay teams of four and worked as the bridge between design and rendering.

The uncomfortable parts

I will tell you when a mechanic does not belong in VR. Some ideas are wonderful on a screen and hostile in a headset — camera shake, forced turns, anything that takes the horizon away from the player without a physical reason. I have talked two clients out of motion-sickness-heavy designs and one out of a project entirely. It cost me the second contract and was still correct.

I also keep a small log of every comfort complaint from playtests, with the build number and the head pose at the time. It is currently 214 entries long, and about a third of them turned out to be the same bug wearing different clothes.

Teaching and writing

I run a two-day workshop on VR comfort at a game development school in Ljubljana twice a year. The exercise that lands hardest: build a five-minute experience with no camera movement at all. Students hate the constraint for the first hour and then discover how much they were using the camera as a crutch.

I write occasionally about latency budgets and hand tracking for a small newsletter that goes to about 900 people. There is no paywall and no growth plan.

Line

Ten years, abbreviated

  1. 2014
    The floor problem

    First DK2 demo, first fall. Started building small room-scale experiments in Unity 4.

  2. 2016
    Studio job, Zagreb

    Two years on a VR training platform for industrial equipment. Learned to ship to hardware I could not take home.

  3. 2019
    Freelance

    First contract: a museum installation with a two-week deadline. It shipped, mostly because the scope was honest.

  4. 2021
    Paper Planets

    First title under my own name. Passthrough calibration taught me more about colour than any shader course.

  5. 2023
    Riftline, Vienna

    Eight headsets in one room. Everything I know about build engineering came from this project.

  6. 2025
    Low Orbit Salvage

    The tether, finally. 90 Hz on Quest 3 with headroom, and no forced reprojection in the release build.
