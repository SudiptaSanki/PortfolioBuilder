Training

I grew up in Lyon, in a flat with two stained glass windows that my grandmother had rescued from a demolished chapel and installed in the stairwell because, she said, “the light was wasted in a museum”. I spent a childhood watching the stair change colour at different hours. It is a cliché to say that decided it. It probably did.

I trained as an apprentice at a restoration workshop in Chartres, where the first year was mostly cleaning and the second was mostly lead. The third year I was allowed to cut glass for a 19th-century window and made every mistake available. From there I studied at the Institut national du patrimoine for two years, learning the survey and documentation side, which is the part that keeps the craft honest.

I have worked in Chicago since 2013, mostly on American windows from the 1880s to the 1930s, which are made differently from French glass: thicker, more opalescent, often with painted faces rather than pure colour. Learning to work in both traditions has made me a better designer, because it taught me that a palette is a set of decisions about the building, not a matter of taste.

How a commission runs

The opening. I visit the building, measure the opening and photograph it through the day. A north window and a south window in the same room are different jobs. If the building is far away, I ask for a photograph every hour from nine to five on a clear day and a section drawing of the wall.

The cartoon. A full-size drawing in charcoal and gouache, made in the studio and pinned up in the actual opening, or a mock-up of the same size if the building is remote. We adjust it for a week before any glass is cut. Clients often find this stage uncomfortable; it is much cheaper than the alternatives.

Glass selection. The palette is chosen from sheets at the studio, in daylight, with the drawing beside them. I buy mouth-blown glass from three suppliers and keep a stock of old glass for restoration, because a 1900 ruby is not a 2026 ruby.

Cutting and leading. Each piece is cut to the cartoon, the lead came is stretched and formed, and the panel is assembled dry. The dry assembly is where the drawing usually gets one final adjustment. Then soldering, puttying and bracing.

Installation. We install with the stonemason or the glazier, not instead of them. The panel is set in a rebate with a ventilated putty bed, and the saddle bars are fixed to the stone, never to the lead.

Restoration principles

Original glass is kept wherever it is structurally sound. The default is repair, not replacement: a cracked quarry can be mended with a copper foil on the inside face, invisible from the floor. Where glass must be replaced, it is matched from the sheet stock and recorded.

The lead net is a different matter. Lead has a working life of roughly eighty to a hundred years before it becomes brittle and the panel starts to bow. A window with failing lead needs re-leading even if every piece of glass is original, and that is a full dismantling, drawing and rebuilding job. I document every piece before it comes apart.

I do not repaint, “improve” or re-colour a historic window. If a panel was altered in 1950, that alteration is now part of its history; I record it and recommend keeping it unless it is causing damage.

The studio

Four people in Lyon and a small bench in Chicago, which exists because too much of the work is on this side of the Atlantic to keep flying glass back and forth. The Lyon studio has a proper light table and a sandblasting cabinet; the Chicago bench is smaller and mostly does surveys and installation.

The apprentice joins for two years, and the deal is the old one: they learn the craft, and they make a window of their own in the second year. Three of the last five have stayed in the trade.
