Furniture that outlives its maker.

I build dining tables, chairs, cabinets and benches to commission in a one-bench workshop in Copenhagen — solid oak, ash, birch and cherry, joined with mortise, tenon and dovetail, finished with soap or oil so the surface can be renewed at your own kitchen table. A small number of workshop pieces are also made each year between commissions and sold from the bench.

Four stages, in order, every time

A piece takes between six and fourteen weeks at the bench. Most of that time is not drama — it is wood resting, glue curing, and a maker checking a surface against the light for the third time.

Bench hours: Monday to Thursday at the bench. Fridays are for deliveries, repairs, and teaching the apprentice — the trade is passed on deliberately, not by osmosis.
  1. 01

    Timber, selected and seasoned

    Boards are bought whole — a table's top comes from consecutive boards of one tree, so the grain runs through it like a sentence. Timber is stacked with stickers in the workshop loft and rested until a moisture meter reads 8–10%; furniture built wetter than the room it will live in will move, crack, and blame the maker.

  2. 02

    Joinery, cut to hold for a century

    Mortise and tenon for frames, dovetails for drawers and boxes, wedges and drawbore pegs where a joint must never creep. A joint in this workshop is wood holding wood; glue assists, metal does not. Any joint here can also be knocked apart by a future repairer — that is part of the design.

  3. 03

    Shaping, by hand where the hand touches

    Seats are hollowed with an inshave, edges are broken with a spokeshave, and flat surfaces are finished with a cabinet scraper rather than sanded into submission. The parts a hand or a back will meet are shaped by hand, because a hand can feel what a machine cannot.

  4. 04

    Finishing, thin enough to repair

    Soap flakes or hardwax oil, two or three thin coats, never a plastic film. A soap finish can be renewed in an afternoon with a kettle and a cloth; a ring mark is a ten-minute repair, not a reason to refinish the whole table. The finish is chosen for the second owner as much as the first.

How a commission runs

Five stages, agreed in writing before any timber is cut. You can stop after the drawings and owe nothing but the drawing fee.

  1. 1

    Conversation and measurements

    We talk about the room, the people, and what the piece must survive. For tables and fitted pieces I measure the room myself within Zealand; further away, I work from your measurements and photographs.

  2. 2

    Drawings and timber, approved by you

    You receive measured drawings and photographs of the actual boards proposed for your piece. Nothing is cut until you have approved both. Drawing fee 1,500 kr, deducted from the price if we go ahead.

  3. 3

    Deposit and a place in the queue

    A deposit of 40% reserves your timber and your place in the queue. The queue is honest: when I say April, the bench is booked to April.

  4. 4

    The build, photographed as it goes

    You receive photographs at each stage — timber, joinery, shaping, finishing — and you are welcome at the workshop to see the piece half-made. Most clients come once. Most say it is the best part.

  5. 5

    Delivery and fitting

    I deliver and fit the piece myself in Denmark — a table is levelled in its own room, not left on a doorstep. Elsewhere in Europe the piece travels crated and insured, with a fitter's notes written for whoever unwraps it. The balance is due on delivery, not before.
