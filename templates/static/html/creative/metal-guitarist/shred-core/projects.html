Engagements — case files

Three jobs, told properly

Not a poster wall. Each engagement is written up the way it was worked: the brief as it arrived, the decisions taken under it, and the outcome — with the tunings, tempos and notice periods printed, because those are the facts a booker actually judges.

CASE 01 — LIVE DEP

Fourteen songs in forty-eight hours

Played as booked

Client Kestrel NineRole Lead guitar — depSet 14 songs · 75 minTunings D std · Drop C · E♭ stdNotice 48 hoursDates 3 festival shows

The brief

On the Tuesday, Kestrel Nine's guitarist broke his wrist falling off a drum riser. The band had a Friday headline festival set, two more shows that weekend, and a set built on three tunings and a click track. The call was simple: can you play it, exactly as recorded, on Friday?

The decisions

The MD sent the stems and the live click maps that night. I charted all fourteen songs from the stems — notation, not memory — and grouped the running order by tuning so guitar changes fell in the two songs with long intros. Both solos were learned note-for-note from the record; a dep's job is the band's show, not an audition. One rehearsal, Thursday, full set at show volume.

The outcome

All three shows played, no songs dropped, no tempos changed. The band rebooked me for the autumn leg before the Friday encore, and the arrangement is now standing: I hold the charts and the click maps, so a future dep call is a rehearsal, not a rebuild. The MD's account of it is quoted on the Setlist page.

CASE 02 — STUDIO SESSION

The solo that fought the vocal

Single mix kept

Client Ashen VerdictRole Session lead + solo writingTrack “Glass Cathedral”Key E minorTuning Drop CTempo 142 BPMSolo 16 bars

The brief

A finished album track with one problem: the composed guitar solo sat directly on top of the chorus vocal melody, and the two lines fought for the same bars. The producer did not want the vocal touched and did not want the solo to feel like a backing part. One day in the studio to fix it.

The decisions

Instead of decorating around the vocal, I rebuilt the solo in the gaps the vocal leaves — answering phrases rather than parallel ones. The harmony run moved down to the low string that Drop C gives you, so the solo's peak sits an octave under the singer instead of on top of her. We tracked two takes of the rebuild; the producer kept the first.

The outcome

The track went to the single mix with the rebuilt solo, no re-record of the vocal, drums or bass, and the fix cost one session day instead of a remix. The solo is now part of the live arrangement — it is the version in the setlist register on the Setlist page, at its real tempo of 142.

CASE 03 — REMOTE RECORD

Seven strings, written for six

Two versions, one session

Client Sister AnvilRole Remote lead + rhythmTrack “Low Mass”Written B std · 7-stringDelivered B std + E std radio editTempo 96 BPM

The brief

Sister Anvil's album was written and tracked on a seven-string in B standard. Then the label asked for a radio edit with the cleaner top end of a six-string in E standard — without re-cutting the drums and bass, which were already mixed. The low parts were the problem: half the riffs live below a six-string's range.

The decisions

We mapped every low figure against the bass line first. Where the bass already owned the root, the guitar part could move up an octave and gain clarity instead of losing weight. The open-string pedal figures were kept with a partial capo, and the two riffs that genuinely needed the low B stayed on the seven-string for the album master only.

The outcome

Both versions were delivered from the one remote session: the album master on the seven-string, untouched, and a radio edit on the six that the label serviced without booking a second day. The revoicing map — which riff moved, and why — went to the band as a document, so the live arrangement matches the edit.
