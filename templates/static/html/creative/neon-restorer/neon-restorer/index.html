Size the tube before you size the transformer

A neon circuit is a series of voltage drops: every foot of tube, every electrode, every splice. Get the total wrong and the transformer runs hot, the tube strikes poorly on a cold morning, or both. These figures are bench estimates for a single 30 mA circuit — always check the core against its nameplate.

The part nobody photographs

Restoration is mostly cleaning and bookkeeping. A sign arrives dirty, often in pieces, with a story that does not match its own hardware. Before anything is bent, I photograph every joint, trace the circuit, and write down what is original and what some previous owner invented. That record decides what happens next: a sympathetic repair that keeps the 1958 tube layout, or an honest rebuild that admits the original is gone.

The tubes come off the sign and go into the pump-out manifold, where the old gas is drawn out and the glass is flushed. Then bombarding: high current through the electrodes to burn off impurities while the tube is held under vacuum. This is the step that decides whether a tube will light evenly or have a dark patch at one end for the next forty years.

Bending is done by eye over a ribbon burner, with a chalk drawing pinned to the bench. A 12 mm tube wants a gentle heat and a slow bend; an 8 mm tube for lettering is far less forgiving. Every bend introduces stress, and every stress point is where a tube will crack in a cold snap, so the fewer the better — which is why a good repair sometimes changes a corner rather than reproducing a bad one.

Gas, colour and the lies of old catalogues

Neon gives the red-orange, argon with a mercury jacket gives the blue, helium the warm gold, krypton the pale white. Manufacturers in the 1950s mixed and filtered to get colours the gas alone cannot produce: yellow from helium through a uranium-glass sleeve, green from a phosphor coating inside the tube. If a sign was originally green and the coating is gone, matching it means matching the coating, not choosing a greener gas.

I keep a shelf of period glass and a spectrometer on the bench. When the original colour cannot be recovered, the report says so, with the closest reversible option and what it will look like at night.

What I keep original

Porcelain faces, brackets, cans and transformers are preserved wherever they are safe. Tubes are consumables and are replaced. A restoration that keeps a dangerous 1930s core is not a restoration; it is a fire.

Safety, plainly

Neon cores run at lethal voltage and current limited only by the tube. Nothing in this shop is powered without a cover and a ground. If you are repairing a sign yourself, the transformer is not the place to learn.

History work

Sign surveys for Main Street programmes, museum labels, and a small archive of North Carolina sign makers. Enquiries welcome even when there is no restoration in it.

The sound of a 60 Hz core
