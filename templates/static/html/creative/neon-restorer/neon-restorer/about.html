A bench, a burner, and thirty-four years of other people’s signs

The shop is a former garage with a good roof and bad heating. There is a pump-out manifold along one wall, a ribbon burner by the window, and a shelf of period glass that I will not sell.

June Halloran

I started in a sign shop in 1992 because it was the only job in Asheville that would teach me to bend glass. The owner, Walter Pike, had been at it since 1954 and did not believe in written instructions; he believed in watching him do it forty times and then trying it on scrap. I broke a lot of scrap.

When Walter retired in 2004 I bought the equipment and the phone number. For the first decade the work was almost entirely commercial: new faces for restaurants, repairs after storms, the occasional marquee. The restoration work started by accident when a motel owner asked whether the old sign in his storeroom could be made to light again. It could. Word travelled through the kind of people who keep old signs in storerooms.

Now roughly half the work is restoration and half is new hand-bent work for people who want a sign that will outlive them. I still do commercial repair, because a shop that only does museum pieces forgets how to work fast.

What actually happens to a dead sign

Survey. Photograph everything, trace the circuit, note what is original. The transformer nameplate is often the only date on the whole object. I write a condition report before touching a tube, because the client needs to know what they have before they decide what to spend.

Pump-out. The tubes are opened at the electrodes and connected to the manifold. Old gas comes out, the glass is flushed with an inert gas, and the tube is pumped down. Fifty years of air and moisture is the main reason an old tube glows patchily: the gas is contaminated and the electrodes are sputtered.

Bombarding. High current through the electrodes while the tube is under vacuum burns impurities off the glass and the metal. It is the least glamorous step and the one that determines whether the sign looks right at night. A tube that is not bombarded properly will have a dark band at one end within a year.

Bending. A chalk drawing on the bench, a ribbon burner, and a lot of small movements. Every bend adds stress, so a repair sometimes changes a corner rather than reproducing a bad original. On an 8 mm lettering tube the margin for error is about a second of heat.

Gas and pressure. Neon for red, argon with a mercury jacket for blue, helium for gold, krypton for pale white. The pressure is set by the tube diameter and the length; too low and the tube is dim at the far end, too high and it strikes hard and runs hot.

Aging. Every tube is run on the bench for at least twelve hours before it goes back on the sign. Most failures happen in the first hour. Finding them on the bench rather than on a roof is the whole point.

Restoration ethics

The question is never “how do we make it look new”. It is “what does this object need, and what would be lost by changing it”. A 1949 porcelain face with a replaced tube is still a 1949 sign. The same face repainted in modern acrylic is a reproduction with an old bracket.

I keep original porcelain, brackets, cans and period transformers wherever they are safe. I replace tubes, wiring and cores, because those are consumables and because a 1930s core is a fire waiting for a dry summer. Where the original colour cannot be recovered — a green phosphor that has flaked away, a filter glass that is no longer made — the report says so and gives the closest reversible option.

Nothing is repainted without the owner’s written agreement, and nothing is “restored” to a condition the sign never had. If a sign was repainted in 1971, that repaint is part of its history and may be worth keeping.

The archive

I keep a small archive of North Carolina sign makers: shop drawings, catalogues, glass samples and about 1,400 photographs. It is not a museum, but it is available to researchers, Main Street programmes and anyone trying to work out what their sign originally looked like. Enquiries are welcome even when there is no restoration in it.
