Sixteen colours is not a limitation. It is the art direction.

The artist

Marisol Vega — ten years at small canvases.

I learned pixel art the way most of us did: editing sprites I wasn’t supposed to edit, on a family computer, one pixel at a time. I spent 2016–2019 drawing for mobile games in a Valencia studio — hundreds of icons, all of them competent, none of them mine — and left to find out what the form could do at the size it was invented for.

Since 2019 the practice has been this: small games of my own (Tidepool Post, 2021; Kelp, 2023; Lanternfall, 2024 — and two shelved, kept on the record) and commissions for other indie developers, taken one at a time. The constraint is the point. A 32 × 32 character has nowhere to hide: every pixel is a decision somebody made, and I would rather make 1,024 good ones than ten thousand vague ones.

My opinion, stated once so clients know what they are hiring: a walk cycle is eight frames or it is a shrug — and if your sprite needs a gradient and a drop shadow to read, it doesn’t need more colours, it needs a better silhouette.

Tile 05

How a commission runs — five stages, no surprises

  1. The brief You send the game, the canvas, the deadline and three games whose art you admire. I reply within two working days with questions, not a quote — a quote before questions is a guess wearing a tie.
  2. The palette lock We agree the palette — twelve colours or fewer per sprite — and the fixed price, in writing, before any production drawing. The palette is the contract’s load-bearing wall: changing it later is a new commission, and both of us know it in advance.
  3. First-frame approval You approve one finished frame — the character, at final size, in the locked palette — before the remaining frames exist. Every expensive mistake in sprite work is visible in frame one; this stage exists so we pay for it once, at frame-one prices.
  4. Full production The set is drawn, timed on paper, and checked in your engine at final resolution. Revision rounds are counted against the contract as we go — you always know how many you have left.
  5. Handover Sprite sheets (PNG), the .aseprite sources, the palette file, and a one-page note on how the set is organised. Sources are handed over at final payment — not held hostage, not drip-fed.
Tile 06

The honesty page

A mockup is not a sprite sheet

A pretty keyframe proves one frame can be pretty. It says nothing about the other fifteen, the timing, or whether the character reads at 32 × 32 in motion, in daylight, on a handheld. That is why this site shows sheets and counts frames instead of pinning up single hero images — and why the first frame you approve is a production frame, not a painting of one.

On AI “pixel art”

Generated “pixel art” has pixels of five different sizes in the same sprite, because nobody drew it — a photograph of a rumour. It cannot be animated consistently, cannot hold a palette, and cannot be fixed one pixel at a time, which is the entire craft. This practice does not sell it, does not tidy it up for clients, and does not use it for “concepts”. If a sprite on this site has a flaw, it is a human flaw, and I can point at the pixel.

Shelved means shelved

Two of my projects are shelved, and they are labelled shelved everywhere they appear — including the home page, where it would be easier to leave them out. A client is trusting me with their game’s face; the least the record can do is be complete.

Figures carry their basis

Download counts on this site name their stores and their month. Rates name what moves them. Frame counts are counted. Where a number can’t be backed, it isn’t printed — you will find no “millions of players” here, because there aren’t.

Tile 07

Terms, printed before you ask

  • Revisions, counted: two rounds are included with a sprite set, three with a combined set-plus-animation contract. A round is one consolidated list of changes at one stage. Further rounds are €60 each, agreed before they start — never invoiced as a surprise.
  • Payment: 40% at palette lock, 60% at handover. The fixed price is the fixed price; if the work grows, we re-price it in writing before it grows.
  • Sources & rights: .aseprite sources and the palette file are handed over at final payment. Full commercial use of the artwork in your game is included in the fee. Reselling the assets as stock, or minting them, is not — that is a separate conversation with a separate price.
  • Credit: I am credited as pixel artist in the game’s credits; you are credited as the client everywhere the work appears here. Either of us may show the work in a portfolio once the game is public.
  • Turnaround: a standard character set runs two to three weeks from palette lock; the diary is honest — if the slot is January, the enquiry page says January.
  • Cancellation: either side may stop at a stage boundary. Work done is paid for; sources for paid stages are handed over; the palette lock fee is the only non-refundable part, because the palette work is real work.
