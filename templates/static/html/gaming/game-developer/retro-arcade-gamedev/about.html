How a TV repair shop became a game studio

My father repaired televisions in the same room where I now repair monitors. I spent six years doing service calls with him — carrying a tube up three flights, learning which capacitors fail hot, learning that a customer will always say “it was working yesterday”. When CRT televisions stopped being worth repairing, the skills did not stop being worth having. Arcade cabinets are the last place where that knowledge still pays.

I built my first cabinet in 2016 out of a dead generic upright and a Raspberry Pi, which I am not proud of, and then replaced the Pi with a real board within a year because the timing was wrong. The difference between emulation and a board at 60 Hz is about four frames of input lag, and four frames is the difference between a game that feels fair and one that does not.

The player is not measuring milliseconds. They are deciding whether to trust the machine.

What I do on a project

Two kinds of work. The first is a new cabinet: I design the game, build the control panel and harness, source and service the monitor, and install it in the venue. The second is restoration: a dead machine gets a full service — power supply, caps, edge connector, tube, art — and goes back into circulation rather than to a collector’s spare room.

I write my own game code in C against a small framework I maintain, which is why I can promise a ROM size and a frame budget. On bigger builds I work with a programmer and an artist; I keep the frame data, the hardware and the cabinet.

Why venues

A cabinet in a bar or a market is a different design problem from a cabinet in an arcade. It has to explain itself in ten seconds to somebody holding a drink, survive being leaned on, and make money without feeling like a slot machine. Most of my design decisions come from standing behind the machine on a Saturday night and watching where people give up.

I keep a notebook per machine: coin counts, session lengths, the exact moment someone walks away. Cabinets are the only game platform where you can watch the quit happen.

What I will not do

I do not put a modern console in a vintage cabinet and call it a restoration. I do not sell a machine without servicing the monitor. And I will not design a game around a paywall timer — if the only reason to insert another coin is artificial difficulty, the design has failed and the machine is lying.
