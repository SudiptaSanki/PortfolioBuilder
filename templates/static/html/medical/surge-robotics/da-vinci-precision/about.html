METHOD & MEASURE

A surgeon who trained as an engineer, in that order of stubbornness.

Colin Avery qualified in medicine in Edinburgh, trained in general and colorectal surgery, and completed a fellowship in robotic surgery — after a first degree in biomedical engineering that he has never quite stopped using. He spent a decade as a consultant robotic surgeon and clinical lead for robotics at a Scottish teaching hospital (institution anonymised here, named in any formal proposal), building its programme from first case to three specialties. Since 2019 he has done the same work for other people's programmes: independently, which is the point.

His logbook stands at over 1,400 console cases. He publishes his assessment figures with their workings, takes no money from any platform manufacturer, and has advised two boards — in writing — not to buy the system they had budgeted for. Both reports are summarised in the case record.

THE METHOD

How an engagement runs

Five stages, in order, none skippable. The order is the method: data before opinion, assessment before advice, and a written report a board can argue with.

STEP 1

Data first

Theatre logs, case mix, calibration records, list timings. If the data doesn't exist yet, the engagement starts by creating it.

STEP 2

Assessment

On site: lists observed, team interviewed, the machine's actual day reconstructed hour by hour — not the timetable's version.

STEP 3

Report, with working

Findings and figures in writing, sources named, arithmetic shown. A report you cannot check is an opinion with a letterhead.

STEP 4

Training & proctoring

Where the report calls for it: curriculum, simulation rubric, dual-console cases until the standard holds without him.

STEP 5

Audit at six months

The same measures, re-run. If the numbers haven't moved, the report says that too — including when the reason is his advice.

THE WORKING PAGE

Measure, calibration, honesty

MEASUREMENT
Every figure this practice quotes a client comes from a log, a rubric or a model whose inputs are printed with it. Console time, utilisation, scores and costs are defined before they are measured — "utilisation" alone has at least three honest definitions, and an assessment states which one it used.
CALIBRATION
A surgical robot is verified, not trusted: calibration and system checks before lists, instrument counts against their rated lives, error logs actually read. The same discipline applies to the assessor — his logbook is audited annually and his rubric scores are sampled by a peer.
UNCERTAINTY
Error budgets are quoted as ranges with the combination method named (worst case, root-sum-square), never as a single heroic decimal. Where a number in a report is an estimate, the report labels it one and says what would settle it.
INDEPENDENCE
No vendor commissions, no speaker fees from manufacturers, no per-case incentives, no equity. Training has been delivered on more than one platform. If a conflict ever arose, the engagement terms require it to be declared in the report itself.
CASE SELECTION
Not every operation should be robotic, and a programme measured on volume will eventually do cases that laparoscopy or open surgery would serve better. Helping a department write — and keep — its selection criteria is part of every assessment.
THE LIMITS, IN PRINT

What the robot does not do

Stated plainly

  • No sense of touch. Current mainstream platforms give the surgeon no force feedback. Tissue tension is judged by eye, from deformation — a skill that has to be taught, and a limit no software update has yet removed.
  • No judgement. The robot scales and filters movement. It does not choose the plane, spot the bleeder or decide to convert to open surgery. Training the human remains the entire game.
  • No shorter learning curve. It standardises the curve — measurable, rubric-scored, credentialed — which is different, and better, and slower than the brochure implies.
  • No automatic benefit. For several common procedures the outcome advantage over good laparoscopy is contested in the literature. A programme should know which of its cases the robot genuinely serves.

Engagement terms

  • Fixed fees as printed in §04 of the Practice page; anything outside them quoted in writing before work starts.
  • Reports are the client's property, figures and all — including the ones that argue against the purchase.
  • Two assessments run at a time, no more; proctoring is booked around existing clinical commitments.
  • Travel outside Scotland charged at cost and itemised; no expenses are marked up.
  • This practice advises hospitals and surgical teams. It does not accept patient referrals, and it cannot comment on any individual's treatment.
