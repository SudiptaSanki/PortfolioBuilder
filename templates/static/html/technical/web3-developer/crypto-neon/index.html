§01 · The signature instrument

One engagement, five phases

Follow one real-shaped engagement — a vesting contract for a small DAO tooling team — from first call to handover. The client’s name is withheld; the phases, and the failure classes each phase exists to catch, are the ones I see every quarter. Step through it.

Phase 01Days 1–4 · before any code

Specification

The deliverable

A written specification, agreed before code: the state the contract holds, every function and who may call it, the invariants it must never break, and the threats we are designing against.

The failure it exists to catch

The unwritten assumption. On this engagement the client assumed only the treasurer could pause distributions; nothing in their draft said so. Writing it down turned an argument into a line in a table.

The artefact

The SPEC document — versioned, signed off by both sides — with a threat list: one line per threat, and the design answer to each.

Phase 1 of 5

Every phase produces a document. If a phase of your current project produced nothing you can read, it did not happen — whatever the invoice says.

§02 · The arithmetic habit

What a transaction costs, worked in the open

Gas is how the network meters computation: every operation carries a price in gas, and you pay gas used × gas price. Pick an operation class and a gas-price band, and do the multiplication the way I make clients do it before we discuss optimisation.

Operation class — illustrative gas used

Gas price band — per unit of gas

Illustration only. The gas figures are representative classes, not measurements of your contract; real costs depend on storage state and calldata. Gas prices move block by block, and layer-2 networks meter differently — usually for far less. This panel teaches the multiplication. It is not a quote, and it says nothing whatsoever about what any token is worth.

1 ETH = 1,000,000,000 gwei. If the formula above ever disagrees with the big number, the formula is the one to trust — and you should tell me.

What moves the price

  • Scope: number of contracts and lines of code in scope
  • Complexity: external calls, cross-contract interaction, callbacks
  • Admin design: upgradeability and key management multiply review surface
  • The spec: a written spec that already exists is worth money off
  • Timeline: a launch date I did not choose costs more than one I did
  • Chain count: every additional deployment target is reviewed separately
