The lab

Nineteen years of glass, and the eleven that did not seal.

A vapor cell programme is a fabrication problem with a physics paper attached. I run a small lab in Jena: one coating oven, two laser benches, a three-layer shield and a shelf of cells that failed.

Career

From atomic clocks to brain fields

I did a doctorate on buffer-gas collisions in rubidium at TU Graz, then six years in an atomic-clock group where the cells had to run for a decade without drifting. That decade taught me the thing the magnetometry literature tends to skip: a cell that is 20% worse but hermetic is worth more than a perfect cell with a leak.

In 2015 I moved to Jena to build SERF magnetometers for a magnetoencephalography project, where the constraint was not sensitivity but packaging: 48 sensors in a helmet, each with its own heater, laser and shielding can, all of them close enough to a human head to pick up the brain and far enough not to burn the scalp.

Since then the work has split between academic instruments and three field programmes: a gradiometer for ordnance survey, a borehole sensor for geomagnetic monitoring, and a compact three-axis unit for a satellite payload study that never flew. The last one produced the best cell I have made and no flight hardware, which is the usual ratio.

What I cannot do in-house is thin-film deposition, sapphire windows or a cleanroom. Those go to two suppliers in Germany and the Czech Republic, and their lead time sets the programme's schedule.

One development programme at a time

I take a single new development programme each year alongside the academic work. If you are at the paper stage, the useful conversation is about the environment the sensor will live in, not the sensitivity figure.
