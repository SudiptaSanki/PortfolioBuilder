The referee is the product.

Anyone can dress an exercise up as a break-in. What a client can actually use is a measurement they trust — and trust comes from the control around the exercise, not from the operators’ war stories. This page is about that control, and about the person who signs for it.

§01Clara Whitfield, exercise lead

PERSONNEL FILE · ABRIDGED

Clara Whitfield spent the first half of her career on the other side of the table. Fourteen years in defence: incident response at a managed detection provider, then leading a detection-engineering team for a financial-services group — the person who got the 3 a.m. call, and later, the person who built the tooling that decided whether the call happened at all.

She moved into adversary emulation in 2019 for one reason: every post-incident review she had ever written contained the sentence “this was visible in the logs”, and nobody could say whether it would have been visible at the time. Emulation, run honestly, is how you find out before it counts. She founded Red Shell in 2022 to run exercises where the measurement is the deliverable — and where being caught early is recorded as the client’s success, in the client’s report, in those words.

Her standing opinion, printed because clients keep asking: “We got in” is the first line of a report. It is never the report. A team that sells the thrill of the entry and skips the detection arithmetic is selling theatre — and theatre does not survive contact with a regulator, an insurer, or a board that has seen a real incident.

The team is four: the lead, two operators, and a white-cell controller whose only job is control — the log, the clock, the stop conditions. Team members hold recognised offensive-security and incident-response certifications; the details are supplied during scoping, under NDA, because a certificate list on a public page proves nothing about the last exercise anyone ran.

§02How an engagement runs

FOUR STAGES · NO SHORTCUTS BETWEEN THEM
STAGE 1

Authorisation & scope

Named authorisers on both sides sign the rules of engagement: scope, window, exclusions, stop conditions, data handling. This stage produces the scope document — billable, and yours to keep even if the exercise never runs.

STAGE 2

Threat profile, at class level

The exercise is shaped against a named class of opponent — financially motivated, state-aligned, insider-assisted — chosen with the client. The profile sets which tactic classes are attempted and in what proportion. It is agreed in the scope, not improvised in the window.

STAGE 3

Controlled execution

The window opens. Operators act inside the scope; the white cell logs every action against one shared clock, watches the stop conditions, and holds halt authority jointly with the client’s authoriser. Purple-team days, where booked, pause the clock for joint review.

STAGE 4

After-action measurement

The client’s detection records are reconciled against the white cell’s log, action by action. The report is the reconciliation: scorecard, class-level gaps, first-alert timeline, and a remediation order the client’s own team helped sequence. Re-test days validate the fixes later.

§03What an exercise cannot tell you

THE HONESTY PAGE · READ BEFORE SCOPING
LIMIT 01

A gap is not a prophecy

An undetected exercise proves your detection missed this exercise, in this window, run by four people who stop at boundaries a real opponent would cross. It does not prove a real attack would succeed — and a measured exercise does not prove one would fail.

LIMIT 02

The sample is the window

Detection is measured on the days the exercise ran, against the staffing and tooling of those days. A rate is a measurement with a date on it, not a property of your organisation. That is why programmes re-run.

LIMIT 03

We are not your opponent

The team emulates a threat profile at class level; it does not replicate a specific group’s patience, budget or willingness to burn a year on one target. Anyone who claims otherwise is describing a different, larger engagement than the one on this page.

LIMIT 04

The report is not a shield

A good scorecard is evidence for a board, an insurer or a regulator that detection was tested on a date, by a method. It is not a certification, a guarantee, or a substitute for the remediation it recommends.

§04Engagement terms, in plain language

THE FULL TERMS ARE SCHEDULED TO THE SCOPE
Authorisation

No exercise begins without the rules of engagement signed by a named authoriser with authority over every system in scope. If authority over part of the scope is unclear, that part comes out of the scope.

Stop conditions

Printed in the scope: safety boundaries, excluded systems, live-service impact thresholds, and the accidental-data rule. A stop can be called by the white cell or the client’s authoriser, unilaterally, without explanation at the time. The debrief explains it.

Data handling

Objectives are synthetic tokens planted for the exercise. Real customer or patient data is never an objective, is never copied, and if it is encountered accidentally the action stops and the white cell is informed before anything else happens. Exercise logs are delivered to the client and Red Shell’s copies are destroyed on the schedule in the scope.

Confidentiality

Engagements run under NDA from the first scoping call. Findings are the client’s property. Red Shell does not publish engagement details; the files on this site exist because three clients chose, in writing, to let abridged versions stand as references.

Fees

As printed on the brief page: the planning phase is fixed and billable, the scope fixes the exercise price before the window is booked, and the result never changes the bill. Cancellation before the window opens is charged at the planning phase only.

Availability

Two exercise slots per quarter; the team does not run parallel windows. Scoping for Q3 2026 is open. A practice that sells measurement should not overbook the measurers.
