Approach

The model is the last thing I change.

A perception stack fails for boring reasons: a lens model that was assumed, a rolling shutter that was ignored, an uncertainty estimate nobody calibrated. I start there, because those fixes are cheaper and they keep working after the next model release.

Six years of cameras with consequences

I did a PhD on depth from defocus, which is a slightly unfashionable corner of vision: no learned prior, just the physics of a lens and the way a known aperture blurs a scene at different distances. It taught me to measure the camera before trusting the network, and it is the reason I still reach for a calibration rig before a new architecture.

Since 2021 I have worked on surgical robotics and mobile manipulation. Both fields share a constraint that academic benchmarks are kind enough to omit: a wrong depth estimate does not lower a score, it moves a metal instrument towards something it should not touch. That changes what a reasonable error metric looks like, and it makes calibrated uncertainty a requirement rather than a nice figure in a paper.

What I actually do in the first two weeks

Measure the optics. Focal length, distortion, principal point, rolling-shutter line delay, and the real aperture curve — measured, not read from the datasheet. On three of the last five projects, the single largest accuracy gain came from replacing an assumed lens model with a measured one.

Look at the failures. Not the metrics: the frames. I collect two hundred failures with a reason attached and sort them into optics, data, label and model. Usually the split is nothing like what the team expected, and the optics bucket is bigger than anyone wants.

Set the budget. The controller runs at a fixed rate; the perception stack has to fit inside it, on the computer that ships. I write the latency and memory budget down before choosing anything, because a model that misses the loop is not a model that ships.

On uncertainty

A depth map without confidence is a pretty picture. For a robot, the useful output is a distribution or at least a calibrated interval, so the planner can decide whether to move slowly, look again, or stop. I train with a heteroscedastic loss, then check calibration on held-out scenes: does the 90 % interval actually contain the truth 90 % of the time? If not, the uncertainty is decoration and I say so.

If the system cannot tell you when it is unsure, it is not ready to move.

Things I have changed my mind about

I used to think synthetic data was a shortcut. It is not: it is a way to buy coverage of conditions you cannot film, and it works when the renderer is calibrated to the real sensor. Smoking out a synthetic-to-real gap with five hundred real frames is now the first experiment, not the last.

I also used to dismiss classical geometry as a fallback. In practice a well-calibrated homography beats a learned pose head on a planar target, every time, and it is auditable. The modern stack is geometry where geometry is exact, and learning where the world is ambiguous.

Collaborations

I work with one external group at a time for six to twelve weeks. The best fit is a team that already has data, a specific failure and a deadline; the worst is a team that wants a demo for a funding review, because the honest version of that job is a slide, and I am not the right person for it.
