§2 — Case files

Three engagements, at class level.

Case files in this trade are written the way the reports are: the client anonymised, the findings described by class — an over-privileged account, a forgotten host, a logic flaw — never by the technical detail that would teach a stranger how to repeat them. What is not anonymised is the judgement: what was decided, what it cost, and what changed afterwards.

CASE 01

The supplier's door

REF RT-26-014
Engagement
Red team
Client
UK logistics group
Elapsed
6 weeks
Outcome
Objective reached; process fixed

The brief

The board's question was specific: could a determined attacker reach the data store behind the warehouse management system — the system that knows where every pallet in the country is? The engagement was authorised by an executive sponsor, with a named “white cell” contact who knew testing was live and a stop letter carried by the testers throughout.

The decisions

Two scope decisions shaped everything. First, the supplier's own systems were out of scope — they are a third party, and nobody can authorise testing someone else's network. Second, the supplier's access path into the client was in scope, because that path is the client's risk to test. The route to the objective ran through that path: a standing remote-access account for the supplier that was over-privileged — able to reach far more than the maintenance task it existed for — with no expiry date and no second factor. It was not a clever hole. It was a process failure wearing a technical costume: accounts created for people who had long since left the supplier were never reviewed, because nobody owned the list.

The outcome

The report led with the process, not the hole. The client renegotiated its supplier terms: expiring accounts, access through a single monitored jump host, and a named owner for the access list on both sides. The closing detection review was the other half of the value — the client's own monitoring team had in fact seen two of the test actions and triaged them as noise, so the engagement ended by improving the defenders' rules as well as the client's contracts. The CISO's verdict on the engagement is quoted in §1.5.

CASE 02

The flaw no scanner flags

REF WA-26-031
Engagement
Web application test
Client
Fintech — payments platform
Elapsed
8 days + report
Outcome
1 critical, fixed & retested

The brief

A payments platform that had passed automated scanning every quarter for two years wanted a manual test before a funding round — partly for assurance, partly because their enterprise customers had started asking for one by name. Scope: the application and its API, with test accounts at each privilege level, on a staging mirror carrying realistic but synthetic data.

The decisions

The automated scanner's clean bill of health was accurate, on its own terms: the application's defences against the known, pattern-shaped weaknesses were genuinely good. The critical finding was a business-logic flaw — a money-movement flow that behaved exactly as coded, in a sequence its designers had never walked through end to end as an attacker would. Scanners check software against catalogues of known mistakes; a logic flaw is a mistake unique to one application's design, so there is no catalogue entry for it. It was found the slow way: by reading the flow, asking what it assumed, and testing the assumption. The finding was evidenced with the minimum demonstration the scope allowed, rated critical on a simple argument — it moved money, and anyone with an ordinary account could reach it — and reported the same day rather than held for the final report, because a live critical is not a deliverable to sit on.

The outcome

The client fixed the flow, added the case to their own automated test suite so the regression would be caught forever, and the retest — included in the fee — confirmed the closure a fortnight later. The wider lesson went into their engineering standards: every money-movement flow now gets a short “how would I misuse this?” review at design time, which costs an hour and is the cheapest security control they own.

CASE 03

The test we stopped

REF IN-26-008
Engagement
Internal network assessment
Client
Regional healthcare provider
Elapsed
Stopped day 2; completed next quarter
Outcome
Fragility fixed first, then tested

The brief

A first internal assessment for a healthcare provider: start from a standard staff account and a network point, and establish how far an attacker who got that foothold — a phished laptop, say — could realistically travel. The rules of engagement named the clinical systems that were out of bounds entirely, and the stop conditions were agreed before day one, as they always are.

The decisions

On the second day, ordinary assessment traffic — nothing exotic, the routine enumeration any assessment begins with — was answered badly by a legacy system in the estate: it slowed, then began failing requests it should have shrugged off. The stop conditions existed for exactly this moment. Testing stopped, the named contact was called within the hour, and the decision was put in writing the same day: this estate would not be assessed by active testing until the fragile system was understood, because the first duty of an authorised test is to do no harm to the thing it was hired to protect. The engagement re-scoped on paper: the fragile estate moved to a review-based assessment — configuration, accounts and architecture read and reasoned about, nothing probed — and the active testing of the remaining estate was postponed a quarter.

The outcome

The client treated the fragility itself as the engagement's most valuable finding, which it was: a system that falls over under a polite scan would not survive a rude one, or a busy Tuesday. It was remediated over the following quarter, the postponed testing then ran in full, and the final report covered both estates. This case is in the register and on this page because a practice that will stop — visibly, in writing, at its own cost in delay — is one whose “all clear” you can also believe.
