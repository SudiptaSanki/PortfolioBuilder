The pilot · 1,340 hours on the sticks

I fly the fastest line I can still explain.

Six years of racing, one broken wrist, 219 props and a bench notebook that has every build I have flown since 2021. The notebook is the part that made me faster; the sticks were already fine.

From a whoop in a car park to the European circuit

I started flying in 2019 with a 65 mm whoop in an underground car park in Plagwitz, because it was February and the alternative was the simulator. The simulator taught me the gates. The car park taught me that a quad with a cracked frame flies like a different aircraft, which is the lesson that eventually turned into a build spreadsheet.

My first five-inch build weighed 704 g and hovered at 46% throttle. It was, by the standards of the people I was flying with, a brick. The fix was not a better motor: it was 90 grams of wire, a lighter camera mount and a pack that was honest about its capacity. That build got to 618 g and took three seconds off my lap times without touching the tune.

Since then the work has been methodical. One change per session, three laps to judge it, and a written note on every crash. In 2024 I started coaching, which improved my own flying more than any component I have bought: explaining a line to somebody forces you to know why it works.

01 — The bench

What a build actually costs in grams

These are the numbers from the notebook, not from a marketing page. Every one of them was measured on the same scale, with the same pack, on the same day.

612 g

Race build, 2026

Frame 78 g, motors 4 × 31 g, stack 12 g, camera 9 g, props 4 × 4 g, pack 218 g, wiring and hardware 155 g. The wiring is always heavier than the drawing says.

704 g

First five-inch, 2021

Same class, 92 g heavier, and it hovered at 46% throttle. Racing it felt like flying with the handbrake on, which is exactly what an extra 92 g is.

3.2 s

Lap time bought by mass

The 92 g came off over four sessions and bought 3.2 seconds on a 28-second lap. No tune change, no new motors. Mass is the cheapest speed in the sport and the hardest to accept.

14

Builds that never raced

Fourteen of the 23 airframes were experiments that went in the bin or became practice quads. A build sheet is a hypothesis; most hypotheses are wrong, and the notebook is where they go to die.

02 — Method

How a session is run

  1. 01

    Warm up on the same three gates

    Every session starts with three laps of the same sequence, at 80%, to establish what the aircraft is doing today. Air density, pack temperature and a slightly bent prop all show up here before they show up in a heat.

  2. 02

    Change one thing

    Prop, pack, mass or tune. Three laps, then decide. If the difference is inside half a second, it is not a difference; it is the wind.

  3. 03

    Review the DVR before the next pack

    Two minutes of footage, watched at half speed, with the crash noted by gate and stick input. Most of my gains came from this, not from the bench.

  4. 04

    Land at 3.5 V per cell

    A pack that lands lower than that was too small for the session, and a pack that is routinely over-discharged is a pack that will sag in a final. The numbers on the charger are part of the tuning.

Coaching, two pilots a season

Two sessions on your own circuit, with your own aircraft, and a build sheet review before the first one. I do not teach stick wiggling; I teach the bench and the line.
