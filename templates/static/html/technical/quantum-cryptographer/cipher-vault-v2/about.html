About & method

An engineer who reads
the budget before the brochure.

Dr Sofia Marek trained as a physicist — a doctorate on single-photon detectors, the unglamorous end of quantum optics where a percentage point of dark-count noise is a career — and spent six years in a national telecoms research laboratory, working on fibre QKD field trials across real carrier routes: real splices, real weather, real repair margins.

She left in 2019 to practise independently, from Delft, for a reason this page exists to state: the laboratory numbers were honest and the sales numbers were not the same numbers. Cipher Vault is the practice that resulted — link engineering and crypto-agility for organisations whose auditors ask the second question.

The practice is deliberately small: Marek, one associate engineer, and a bench of measurement kit. It designs links, audits estates and measures acceptances. It does not sell hardware, and it takes no commission from anyone who does.

Her standing line, printed because clients keep asking for the other version: “‘Quantum-proof’ is a phrase I will not put in your board paper — because your auditors will ask me to prove it, and I would have to say no.”

Engagement terms — the short version

  • Fixed-fee studies, quoted in writing once scoped; day rates are the ones printed on the Log page.
  • Deliverables are documents — the budget, the key-rate sheet, the register — and they belong to the client, including when the conclusion is “do not build”.
  • No vendor commissions, referral fees or hospitality, from QKD, HSM or network vendors, ever.
  • Defensive engineering only. No offensive testing, no engagements against third-party systems.
  • Case files are anonymised and published only with the client's written consent.
METHOD

How an engagement runs

Three steps, in this order, for every client — including the ones who arrive asking for step two.
01

Inventory first

Before anything is designed, the estate is written down: every key exchange, certificate, protocol and library, classified by data lifetime and harvest-now-decrypt-later exposure. Half the time, this register is the deliverable the client needed — and it decides, with evidence, whether a quantum link belongs anywhere in the answer.

02

Budget before boxes

If a link is warranted, it starts as arithmetic: a route survey, a loss budget in dB, a predicted QBER and key rate from the printed rule — Sheet K-1, with the client's own fibre numbers in it. Hardware is then chosen to fit the budget. Any proposal that starts with a product name is sent back.

03

Assurance in writing

Acceptance is measured over a stated window and reported next to the prediction — brochure figure included, difference explained in plain dB. The report belongs to the client and is written to survive an auditor reading it without the author in the room.

THE HONESTY PAGE

The caveats, in the main text

Most QKD marketing puts these in footnotes, or nowhere. They are the product here.

Trusted nodes are trusted

Beyond metropolitan distances, today's QKD networks relay keys through trusted nodes. A trusted node can, in principle, read the keys it relays — so each one must be housed and guarded like a vault, because that is what it is. End-to-end quantum repeaters would remove the caveat; they are research, not product. Any diagram this practice draws shows the trusted nodes on it. Ask any vendor to show you theirs.

The deadline is a recording, not a machine

No cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists today. The risk that matters is harvest now, decrypt later: an adversary records your ciphertext today and decrypts it the year such a machine arrives. Data that must stay secret for twenty years is already late to migrate — which is why the register puts migration (S.03) ahead of links, and why “we'll wait and see” is a decision with a date on it.

QKD secures a link — nothing else

It does not patch your VPN concentrator, un-phish your operators, or encrypt data at rest. It is one line in a security budget. A practice that lets a client believe otherwise has sold them a very expensive line.

The rule of thumb is a rule of thumb

Sheet K-1's r = 1 − 2·h(q) is the asymptotic Shor–Preskill-style bound the practice uses for first-pass budgets. Real systems do worse — finite-key effects, imperfect error correction, detector quirks — and the gap is exactly what an acceptance measurement (S.05) exists to catch. The sheet says so on its face; so does this page.
