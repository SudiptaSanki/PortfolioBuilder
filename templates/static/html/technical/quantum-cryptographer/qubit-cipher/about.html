I am Clara Behrens. I have spent fifteen years in cryptographic engineering — first in smart cards, then in network security, and since 2017 in quantum key distribution and the migration to post-quantum algorithms.

The work is mostly measurement and documentation. The interesting part is deciding what to trust when a vendor says the link is secure.

01 · Career

From a card that could be power-analysed to a link that can be watched.

I started in 2009 at a smart-card company in Munich, on a payment application that was eventually broken by differential power analysis. The break was not clever; it was patient. Someone sat with an oscilloscope for three weeks and read the key out of the current trace, and the fix took eighteen months and a new mask.

That experience set the pattern of my work. I stopped asking whether a design was secure in principle and started asking what an attacker with time and a bench could actually measure. In 2014 I moved to Berlin and into network security, and in 2017 a fibre-QKD deployment needed someone who could read both the physics paper and the protocol specification. I have been doing that since.

  • 2009–2014Smart-card security, MunichPayment applications, side-channel countermeasures, Common Criteria evaluations.

02 · Standards

The unglamorous work that decides whether a link is allowed to exist.

I sit on the ETSI Industry Specification Group for QKD, where the documents that matter are the ones nobody cites: the characterisation of a module, the definition of a trusted node, the test procedure for a detector after maintenance. A QKD link without a characterisation standard is a physics demonstration with a purchase order.

I also contribute to ISO/IEC 23837 on quantum-safe security requirements, and I have been a technical reviewer for two national cybersecurity agencies. That work is confidential, which is why it appears in this file as a count and not as a list.

03 · Method

Four rules an audit runs by.

  1. A
    Measure the QBER yourself

    Not the vendor’s dashboard. A second, independent measurement over at least an hour, with the source and the detectors logged.

  2. B
    Draw the trust boundary before the topology

    Every diagram in the report has a dotted line around the parts that must be trusted absolutely. If a node sits inside, the report says so in the first paragraph.

  3. C
    Find the fallback

    Every system has one, and it is usually RSA. The most common finding in my audits is a quantum-safe link in front of a classical key server.

  4. D
    Write down what would change the conclusion

    A QBER budget is only useful if it lists the numbers that would move it. That list is the last page of every report I write.
