§3 · Method & terms

The researcher, the method, and the refusals.

I am Dr Leonie Fischer. I spent a decade in applied cryptography research — protocol analysis, parameter selection, the unglamorous arithmetic of key sizes — and I have audited post-quantum readiness independently since the standards were finalised in August 2024. This page is the working record: how an audit runs, what this practice refuses to do, and the terms it works under.

The researcher

Trained to count

My doctorate was in mathematics; my postdoctoral years were spent on lattice-based constructions — the family that now carries most of the standardised schemes. That background is why this practice sits on the research and audit side of the field: I do not sell links, appliances or licences, and I do not implement migrations. I measure estates, classify lifetimes, check parameters, and write the plan. Other people — often excellent people — sell the shovels.

The practice is deliberately small: one researcher, a network of inventory engineers engaged per audit, and a hard limit on concurrent engagements, because an audit written from a template is worth the paper it replaces. The case files in §2 are the shape of the work: three audits, three different answers, each one derived from the client’s own numbers rather than from the year’s mood.

Berlin is the base; the work travels. Archives, health suppliers, law firms and the companies that build for governments share one property that matters here: their data will still need protecting when today’s product cycles are landfill. That property — a long X — is the entire reason this practice exists.

The method

How an audit runs

Four stages · No stage is skippable
  1. ST. 1

    Inventory

    Every system, certificate, library and key-exchange path is listed — a CBOM-grade register. Most estates cannot produce this list on day one; producing it is the first deliverable, and it is valuable even if the audit stopped there.

  2. ST. 2

    Lifetime classification

    Data classes are assigned their X from retention law, contract and sensitivity — not from policy slogans. “Everything is confidential forever” is not a classification; it is the absence of one, and the audit says so.

  3. ST. 3

    Scenario analysis

    The Mosca check is run per data class against the three stated scenarios of Table 1, and parameter choices are reviewed against the lifetimes they must serve — at class level, with the basis of every judgement written down.

  4. ST. 4

    The phased plan

    What moves first, what can wait, what the new sizes will break, and what it costs — sequenced by exposure, costed from the published fee shape, and written so that a board can act on it without a cryptography degree.

The honesty page

What this practice will not tell you

Printed before you enquire, not after
  1. H. 1

    Scenarios, never predictions

    Nobody knows when a cryptographically relevant quantum computer arrives — not this practice, not the vendors, not the keynote speakers. Anyone who gives you a year for it is selling you the year. This page works with three stated scenarios and shows its arithmetic; if a scenario’s basis moves, the briefing in QC-S05 says so.

  2. H. 2

    The standards are young

    FIPS 203, 204 and 205 were finalised in August 2024. The schemes are the best-reviewed constructions the field has — and they are young, their implementations younger. Parameter choices inside them are judgement calls. This practice states the basis of every judgement it makes, so a future auditor can disagree with it precisely.

  3. H. 3

    Harvest-now is a long-lived-data risk

    Recording traffic today to decrypt it later is worth an adversary’s storage only when the data stays valuable for decades. For most operational data it is not a sensible fear, and this practice will not use it as one. The fear is scoped to the data that earns it — archives, records, deeds — and the scoping is printed, not implied.

  4. H. 4

    The audit bills the same either way

    “Move now” and “you have time” leave this office at the same day rate, and seven of the thirty-one audits on the scoreboard concluded the calmer thing. A practice that earns more from alarm will find alarm. This one has arranged its fees so that it cannot.

  5. H. 5

    Defensive work only

    Reviews are conducted and reported at class level — parameters against lifetimes. No exploit detail is produced, published or sold, and no engagement is accepted whose purpose is to weaken someone else’s cryptography.

Terms

Engagement terms, in short

  1. T. 1

    Fees

    Day rates and per-system rates as printed in Table 3 on the Audit page; migration planning fixed per phase, quoted after the audit and before the phase begins. No vendor commissions, no referral fees, no success fees in either direction. Invoices monthly; reports are the client’s property on delivery.

  2. T. 2

    Confidentiality

    An audit learns where a client’s cryptography is weakest; that knowledge is the client’s. Inventories and reports are delivered over the client’s chosen channel, retained by this practice only as long as the engagement requires, and never used — even anonymised — without written permission. The case files in §2 were published under that rule.

  3. T. 3

    Limits

    An audit is an assessment at a date, against stated scenarios — not a guarantee about a machine nobody has built. If the scenarios move, the honest response is a revised briefing, and past clients receive it under QC-S05.
