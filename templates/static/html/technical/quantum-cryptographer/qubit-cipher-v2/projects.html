§2 · Case files

Three audits, three different answers.

An audit that always concludes “move now” is a sales funnel. These files are published the way they were delivered — brief, decisions, outcome — with the X, Y and scenario figures left in, because the numbers are the work. Clients are described by sector and shape, never named: their data is the point of the exercise.

CASE FILE QC-F01

The firm that had time

Client
40-partner law firm
Sector
Legal
Estate
212 systems inventoried
X — bulk of client data
7–10 years (planned at 8)
X — long tail
25+ years (wills, deeds, trusts)
Verdict
Within Central — spend the margin on inventory

The brief

The firm’s board had read the headlines and expected a five-year emergency programme. Its data, however, had never been classified by secrecy lifetime — “everything is confidential” was the policy, which is another way of saying nothing had been measured. The audit was commissioned to produce a verdict per data class, not a mood.

The decisions

Inventory first: 212 systems, every certificate, library and key-exchange path listed before any product was discussed. Then classification: matter files and correspondence at X = 7–10 years under the firm’s retention rules; a thin long tail — wills, deeds, trust instruments — at X = 25+. The Mosca check was run per class against the Central scenario, not once for the whole firm.

The outcome

For the bulk of the estate, at the planning figures (X = 8, Y = 3 for a standardised, well-documented stack): X + Y = 11 against Z = 12 under the Central scenario — within it, with one year of margin. That is what “you have time” means in this workbook: a measured year, not a mood — and the report says plainly that under the Fast scenario the same estate is late, which is why the margin is being spent on the inventory rather than on relief. For the long tail (X = 25+), beyond every scenario, the plan is different and already running: hybrid key establishment on the archive’s ingest paths first. The written conclusion — “you have time; spend it on inventory” — was billed at the standard day rate, under the rule printed on the Audit page. It is counted among the seven “you have time” audits on the scoreboard, and it is the outcome the firm’s testimonial describes.

“We budgeted for a frightening report and a product shortlist. We got a calm one and a spreadsheet — our own cryptography, listed properly for the first time. The long tail is now on a plan; the rest of the firm stopped losing sleep it never needed to lose.”
Head of Risk & Compliance, the client firm — printed with permission; the firm is not named.
CASE FILE QC-F02

The fifty-year archive

Client
Digital-preservation supplier to public archives
Sector
Archives & records
Estate
Ingest, storage and access tiers across 3 sites
X
50 years, by statute
Y — planned migration
6 years, phased
Verdict
Beyond every scenario — begin now, in phases

The brief

Some archives do not get to choose their X. Retention statute set this client’s secrecy and integrity horizon at 50 years, which makes the Mosca arithmetic brutal under any scenario: X + Y = 56 overshoots even the Slow scenario’s Z = 24 by three decades. The question was never whether to start — it was how to start without pretending the whole backlog could move at once.

The decisions

The plan was phased by exposure, not by convenience. Phase one protected the future: hybrid key establishment (classical + ML-KEM-768) on all ingest and inter-site transfer paths, so that material recorded from now on stops accumulating harvest-now risk. Phase two scheduled the backlog by lifetime class — the longest-lived collections re-wrapped first. Phase three addressed the integrity side: a signature-migration path for the archive’s attestations, using the standardised schemes in Table 2, with SLH-DSA reserved for the small set of artefacts whose signatures must verify for the full 50 years. Costs were planned in bytes as well as euros: the archive’s access tier needed its certificate store rebuilt for 1,184-byte public keys, and that was in the plan, not discovered by it.

The outcome

Phase one is complete; the ingest paths no longer add to the harvestable backlog. Phases two and three are running on the published schedule. The client’s own report to its archive customers states the position plainly — including that historical recordings cannot be un-recorded, and that the plan’s job is to stop the exposure growing while the backlog is worked through. That sentence, unsoftened, is the deliverable this practice is proudest of.

CASE FILE QC-F03

The vendor’s parameter set

Client
Health-records software supplier
Sector
Health
Finding
ML-KEM-512 default against a 30-year retention class
X — affected class
30 years
Review basis
Class level — parameters vs stated lifetime
Verdict
Undersized for the stated lifetime — re-parameterise

The brief

The client’s platform vendor had shipped its post-quantum update with ML-KEM-512 as the single default for every data class — chosen, the documentation said, for performance. The client’s longest retention class, however, is 30 years. The review was commissioned to answer a narrow question: does the chosen parameter set match the stated lifetime?

The decisions

The review stayed at class level, deliberately: no exploit detail, no attack narrative — a comparison of parameter categories against lifetimes and scenarios. ML-KEM-512 is a Category 1 parameter set; for a 30-year class, under the Central scenario, the margin argument does not hold — X alone is two and a half times Z. The recommendation was specific and boring: ML-KEM-1024, in hybrid construction, for the 30-year class; ML-KEM-768 hybrid for the platform’s ordinary classes; the 512 default retired. The size cost was tabled from Table 2 — 1,568-byte ciphertexts against 768 — and accepted by the client’s engineers once it was printed next to the retention figure it protects.

The outcome

The vendor changed its default in the following release and published its parameter rationale — the first time, its engineers said, a customer had asked for the reasoning rather than the badge. The client’s 30-year class now runs the recommended parameters. The finding is reported here at the same class level at which it was delivered: parameter sets are judgement calls, and this one was undersized for the job it was given. Nothing in this file would help anyone attack anything; that is by design.
