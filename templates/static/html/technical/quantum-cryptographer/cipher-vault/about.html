Cryptography is a maintenance discipline, not a choice of algorithm

I have spent ten years on the unglamorous side of the field: the implementation, the deployment, the inventory nobody wants to build, and the migration that has to survive a Tuesday release.

From lattice proofs to a payment network

My doctorate was on the concrete cost of lattice reduction against module-LWE with small parameters — the kind of question that is interesting for a year and then becomes a product decision a decade later. I finished in 2019, when Kyber was a candidate and “post-quantum” was still a phrase that made product managers change the subject.

I spent four years after that at a payment network, on a team of nine people responsible for every key in the scheme. We had 12,000 endpoints terminating TLS, an HSM estate from three vendors, and a firmware team that shipped on a five-year cycle. That is where I learned that a cryptographic migration is 20% mathematics and 80% finding out which certificate is pinned inside a mobile app that nobody has the source for.

Since 2022 I have worked independently, mostly with teams that have already decided to migrate and need a plan that survives contact with their own release process. The work splits into three kinds: inventories, implementations, and the argument that gets a budget approved.

What I actually do on an engagement

Cryptographic inventory. A machine-readable map of every key, certificate, protocol and library in scope, with an owner for each. I read certificate transparency, load-balancer configuration, mobile binaries, firmware images and the procurement list, because the vendor you forgot is usually the one that matters.

Hybrid handshake work. Measurement first: what the new key share does to packet counts, latency and middlebox behaviour on your actual paths. Then the rollout order — canary, internal, then customers — and the rollback that does not silently renegotiate to classical-only.

Implementation review. Lattice code has different failure modes from RSA: constant-time discipline matters more, stack use is larger, and a single early return in a decapsulation routine can leak the key. I review the code and the side-channel tests, and I run the decapsulation on hardware you actually ship.

Standards translation. Someone has to read FIPS 203 and turn §7.3 into a ticket. I do that, in your tracker, with acceptance criteria that a test can check.

Positions I will defend

QKD is not a replacement for public-key cryptography. It solves key distribution over a dedicated link and nothing else. Authentication still needs signatures; a QKD link without an authenticated channel is a very expensive way to exchange a key with an attacker. I deploy it where the link justifies it and say plainly where it does not.

A hybrid is a safety net only if it fails closed. If a middlebox strips the ML-KEM share and the client quietly continues with X25519 alone, you have deployed a downgrade path, not a migration. The downgrade needs its own test.

“Quantum-safe” is a claim with a date on it. Algorithms age. The durable property is crypto-agility: algorithm selection in configuration, a versioned key format, and a test suite that runs against two schemes at once.

Don’t migrate what you can delete. The cheapest quantum-safe certificate is the one retired last quarter. Inventories usually find 10–15% of keys that no live service needs.
