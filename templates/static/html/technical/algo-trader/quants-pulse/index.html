The trade is the easy part. The fill is the job.

Microstructure

The half-spread is not the cost. It is the entry fee.

Every execution conversation starts with the spread, because it is the one cost you can see in the book. On ES it is usually one tick, a quarter of a point, about half a basis point at 5,000. The expensive part arrives later, when your own order starts moving the queue.

For a 500-lot order the visible spread costs roughly $3,100 in total across the fill. The impact of working the order over 45 minutes costs about the same again, and the timing risk — the market drifting while you wait — costs more than both if the alpha is fast.

Capacity

A strategy is only as large as its worst fill.

Backtests assume you can trade at the close. Real books cannot: at 2,000 contracts in a quiet hour, the last hundred fills are visibly worse than the first, and the difference is not noise you can average away.

I size every strategy to the point where impact eats a third of gross alpha, then run it at half that. The capacity number in the tape above is the first figure; the book sits comfortably inside it.

Cost model

Square-root impact, with the exponent earned.

The model on this page is deliberately simple: impact grows with volatility and with the square root of size relative to volume. It is fitted to my own fills, not borrowed from a paper, and it is wrong in exactly the regime that hurts — the first ten minutes after a data release.

That is why the bench below has a timing term. If the model were honest everywhere, execution would be an optimisation problem rather than a risk problem.

03 / Notes

Three things the backtest does not tell you.

Your fills are a sample of your own behaviour

If the algorithm always sends after the open, the impact model is fitted to the open. That is fine until someone changes the schedule. I refit quarterly on the previous quarter's logs and report the out-of-sample error, which is usually twice the in-sample one.

Refit error 0.8 bpsIn-sample 0.4 bps

Correlation breaks the capacity number

Three strategies that are uncorrelated in a backtest tend to become one trade at the worst moment. Capacity is computed per strategy and then halved for the portfolio, because the market does not care that the models disagree theoretically.

Portfolio cap one thirdSum of parts three thirds

The cheap order is usually the patient one

Lowering participation from 15 % to 5 % cuts impact by a third and doubles the time the order is exposed to alpha decay. Whether that is a good trade depends on the half-life of the signal, which is the number most execution research leaves out.
