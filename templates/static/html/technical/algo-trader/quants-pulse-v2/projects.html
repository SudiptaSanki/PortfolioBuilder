File 01 · Backtest audit

The two-year look-ahead

Outcome — strategy shelved

The brief

A proprietary trading firm had a futures strategy with a two-year backtest showing 6.2 bps gross edge per trade and a smooth, enviable curve. Before committing capital, the firm commissioned an independent audit of the backtest itself — the data, the joins, the fill assumptions.

The decisions

The data was frozen and versioned on day one, so every restatement could be traced. The audit found the cause in the fundamentals feed: company results joined to trading days by period end rather than publication date. The backtest had been trading on figures the market would not see for weeks. The pipeline was rebuilt point-in-time — every record carrying the moment it became knowable — and the strategy rerun untouched on honest data.

The outcome

Restated, the same strategy showed 1.8 bps gross per trade — real, perhaps, but under the firm's own 3.1 bps cost line. The firm shelved it before capital followed the chart. Nineteen of my forty-one audits have ended this way. It is the outcome the fee is for.

File 02 · Execution build

Model said 1.6. The market said 1.1.

Outcome — live, reconciled monthly

The brief

A trading company was replacing a broker-supplied execution stack with its own engine and wanted the thing most builds skip: a cost model written before the code, so the finished system could be judged against a prediction instead of a feeling.

The decisions

The model was fixed in writing at the start: fees 0.4, half-spread 1.1, slippage modelled at 1.6 bps median adverse fill. The build captured every fill against its decision price from the first test trade, so measurement was a property of the system, not a project bolted on afterwards. Routing changes were made only where the fill data — not the demo — argued for them.

The outcome

Over the first ninety live days, measured median slippage came in at 1.1 bps against the 1.6 modelled. Both figures stay printed in the file: the model was conservative, the market was kinder than the model in normal hours — and the same sheet shows the three stressed sessions where slippage ran past 4 bps, because a median is a summary, not a promise. The client now reconciles fills against the model monthly, in-house.

File 03 · Risk controls

The incident that did not happen

Outcome — controls in production

The brief

A firm preparing to take a strategy live had the strategy, the infrastructure and the capital — and a risk layer consisting of a person watching a screen. They commissioned the control layer before launch, not after the first incident: kill switch, hard position limits, and daily reconciliation that did not depend on anyone remembering.

The decisions

Limits were set in the matching layer, not the strategy, so no code path could argue with them. The kill switch was tested in anger — deliberately tripped in staging until the team trusted it. Reconciliation ran three ways every day: positions, fills, cash, each from an independent source, with any break halting the next session's start until a human signed the sheet.

The outcome

Six months after launch, a faulty deployment began doubling order sizes. The position limit — not a phone call — stopped it inside the session, flat and small. I state the outcome carefully: the control worked once, on the day it was needed. That is the whole success metric for this kind of work, and it does not generalise into a promise about the next fault.

“The limit caught what our monitoring didn't, and the reconciliation sheet told us exactly what had happened before the market opened next day. Boring, documented, decisive — what you want from a control layer.”
