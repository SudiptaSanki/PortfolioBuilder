Eleven chips, four fridges, one question: where does the energy go?

I am a cryogenic quantum hardware engineer. I take a chip from a junction model to a cooled package and then argue with the measurement until the three agree. Most of the work is loss, thermal anchoring and the unglamorous arithmetic of how a millikelvin is actually reached.

Oslo · since 2019 · 4 dilution systems booked, 2 of them mine

How I got here

I started in radio astronomy, building cryogenic low-noise amplifiers for a 20 m dish in the Atacama. The lesson there was that a receiver is not the amplifier; it is the whole chain from the feed horn to the digitizer, and the weakest link sets the noise. When I moved to superconducting qubits in 2016, the same arithmetic applied with a different vocabulary: instead of noise temperature, T1; instead of gain drift, dephasing; instead of a warm connector, a 4 K attenuator with a bad ground.

After three years inside a national lab’s fabrication line, I opened an independent practice in 2019. The premise was narrow on purpose: I design chips and their immediate cryogenic surroundings, and I do not sell algorithms, software stacks or a roadmap. Clients are university groups, small quantum computing companies and two national metrology institutes. Some engagements are a full 24-qubit tile; others are four weeks of finding out why a chip that measured 300 µs on the dicing frame measures 90 µs in the package.

I keep a fabrication log in the same handwriting I used at the telescope. Every process change gets a date, a wafer map and a measurement, and nothing is called an improvement until it survives a second cooldown in a different fridge. That rule has saved more designs than any simulation.

Working rules

Measure the package first

A chip that is good but packaged badly reads as a bad chip. Half of the hardware problems I am paid to find are in the indium bumps, the copper pocket, or a coax shield that touches the cold plate at one point instead of four.

One variable per cooldown

Fridge time is the scarcest resource in the field. A cooldown with two changes and a good result teaches nothing. I would rather run four 12-qubit diagnostic tiles with one change each than one 24-qubit tile with a story.

Report the floor, not the best pair

Every chip has one pair with 99.6% CZ and one pair that is unusable. The median and the worst pair go in the report next to the best, because that is what a compiler will have to route through.
