CASE 01

The copilot that failed its own launch gate

Gate held · 14 Feb 2026 Shipped · 2 Apr 2026

The ask

Halden Mobility, a B2B SaaS company, had a support copilot that demoed beautifully and a customer launch six weeks out. I was hired for "final integration." There was no eval set — only a founder's conviction and forty cherry-picked example questions, all of which the system answered correctly, because they had been chosen for that.

What I did

Built eval set v1.3: 412 real resolved tickets, sampled from actual traffic, labelled by two of Halden's own support leads with disagreements adjudicated. Froze it. Set the gate in writing before the first run: groundedness ≥ 92% (every claim in the answer supported by retrieved policy text), correct escalation ≥ 95%. First full run, 14 February 2026: 84.6% and 91.2%. The gate held and the launch moved. The failure taxonomy: retrieval misses 41% of failures, stale or duplicated policy documents 27%, over-long contexts truncating the relevant passage 18%, everything else 14%. The model was the smallest part of the problem.

The outcome

Six weeks of unglamorous work: policy corpus re-chunked by section (1,140 documents deduplicated to 763), citation-required answers, an escalation rule rewritten with the support leads, context budgets enforced. Same frozen set, re-measured 27 March 2026: 93.8% grounded, 96.4% escalation. Shipped 2 April. Production cost: $0.0074 per drafted answer at frontier prices (1,900 in / 260 out, Table 1 arithmetic) — $2,278 in March at 310k requests. The gate still runs monthly; it has caught two regressions since, both before customers did.

CASE 02

The corpus that ate the budget

Internal launch · Sep 2026

The ask

An occupational-pensions administrator wanted an internal Q&A tool over fourteen years of scheme documents. A vendor had already demoed "94% accuracy" — on the vendor's own forty questions, written by the vendor. The board had nearly signed.

What I did

Built a 300-question eval from real adviser queries, with answers verified against the scheme rules. The vendor's system scored precision@5 of 0.52 — on half the questions, the right document wasn't even in the top five retrieved. Then the corpus audit, which is where the project actually lived: 38,400 documents; 31% duplicates or superseded versions; 62% carrying no usable metadata; three different "current" versions of the same regulation in three folders. No model retrieves its way out of that. We deduplicated to 26,500 documents, rebuilt the metadata, and created a version-of-record register the client's own team now maintains.

The outcome

Re-measured on the same 300 questions: precision@5 0.87, answer groundedness 91%. Launched internally in September 2026 — and deliberately not to scheme members, because the printed threshold for public answers is 95% groundedness sustained over two quarterly re-runs. It may get there; it hasn't yet, and the file says so. The honest line of this case: 58% of the budget went to corpus work, 42% to the model layer. Any quote that reverses that ratio for a corpus like this is selling you the demo.

CASE 03

The token bill that fell 78%

In production · savings reconciled to invoice

The ask

A returns platform had two LLM features in production — returns-reason classification and draft replies to customers — both running on the frontier class because "that's what the prototype used." Finance's question was new: what does one request cost? Nobody knew. The February 2026 invoice said $18,254 and climbing with volume.

What I did

Metered first: 1.71M requests in February, averaging 2,350 input and 480 output tokens — the arithmetic of the old bill is reproducible from Table 1 on the index page. Then three levers, in order of effect. Routing: classification is 78% of traffic and needs a label, not prose — moved to the small class with a 40-token output cap. Caching: every draft request re-sent the same returns-policy prefix; prompt caching reached a 64% cache-hit rate on draft input tokens. Output discipline: draft length capped by template, 480 → 240 average output tokens, with agent-edit rate watched so brevity didn't just move the cost to humans.

The outcome

May 2026 invoice: $4,096 — a 78% reduction, reconciled line by line against the provider's billing export, not estimated. The quality ledger is printed because a saving bought with silent quality loss is a loan, not a saving: classification accuracy on the frozen 2,000-item set moved 96.1% → 95.8%; agent acceptance of drafts 88% → 87%. Three-tenths of a point, stated in the report, accepted by the client in writing. If your cost engineer won't show you that column, ask why.
