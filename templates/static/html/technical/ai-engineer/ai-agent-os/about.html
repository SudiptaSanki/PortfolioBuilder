§02 — Method

The engineer, and how the work runs

From platform engineering to other people's agents

I spent eight years in platform and ML infrastructure — search ranking pipelines first, then the LLM platform team at a fintech, where my job was the unglamorous layer between "the model works" and "the service survives Monday". When agent frameworks arrived, the same teams discovered the same lesson distributed systems taught a decade earlier: the demo is the easy 10%, and operability is the product.

The independent practice, running since 2023, exists because the failure modes turned out to be wonderfully consistent. Different vendors, different frameworks, different industries — the same four classes from §03 of the index, wearing different clothes. I do not sell a framework, a platform or a model, and I hold no vendor partnerships: the review can therefore afford to conclude "don't build this", and sometimes does.

What I sell is a discipline: evidence from your own traces, an evaluation that can fail, guards that halt instead of warn, and numbers recomputed in front of you — the way every figure on this site can be recomputed from the counts printed beside it.

How an engagement runs

Four steps, in this order, for a reason

01

Traces first

Every engagement opens with twenty real production runs, read line by line. Not the architecture diagram, not the demo — the traces. Most findings in most reports are visible in those twenty runs to anyone who reads them in sequence; the skill is knowing which of the four failure classes you are looking at, and what it costs per day while it stays unfixed.

02

Evaluation before changes

Before a prompt, model or tool is touched, we build the instrument that can tell a good change from a bad one: a held-out set of real tasks, graded by humans against a written rubric, frozen against tuning. Human review is then sampled continuously in production — a small, steady sample, graded the same way, so drift shows up as a number before it shows up as a complaint.

03

Guards before scale

Step caps, per-run budget caps, repeated-call detection, strict tool schemas, least-privilege permissions and approval gates on irreversible actions. Guards are installed while traffic is small and forgiving — retrofitting them during an incident, as Case File 01's client did the first part without me, is the expensive order.

04

Ship behind flags, gate on the suite

Changes roll out behind flags to a slice of traffic, with the regression suite from step 02 wired into the release process as a gate, and budget alarms set at the forecast rather than at multiples of it. The engagement ends when your team can run the whole loop — read, evaluate, guard, gate — without me in the room.

The honesty page — what agents are bad at

Long chains. Reliability compounds multiplicatively, and the arithmetic is brutal:

0.95 per step, chained 30 deep → 0.95³⁰ ≈ 0.21 — about one run in five completes cleanly.

A step that is right 95% of the time is a good step. Thirty of them in a trench coat are a coin flip weighted against you. Long-horizon work needs checkpoints, state outside the prompt, and humans at the irreversible moments — or it needs not to be an agent.

Knowing when to stop. Models are trained to produce the next step, not to conclude that no step should be taken. Every "the agent should have escalated" post-mortem is this gap, and it is closed by guards and routing rules, not by asking the model to be humble.

Telling you it failed. The default failure is a fluent, confident, wrong answer (Class 04). Nothing in the standard logs alarms. If your monitoring would not catch a wrong-but-plausible output, you do not have monitoring — you have accounting.

And demos are not evidence. A demo is a trace the presenter chose. Bring me the traces nobody chose — the 2 a.m. ones, the expensive ones, the ones support apologised for. That is the system you actually own.

Engagement terms

  • Scope in writing first. Fixed shapes with fixed deliverables; no open-ended "AI transformation".
  • Two engagements at a time. The waitlist on the index is real; trace reviews are the fast lane in.
  • Your traces stay yours. Reviewed under NDA, never used in another client's work or in any training set, deleted at engagement close on request.
  • No vendor commissions. No platform, model or tooling partnerships — recommendations are unbought.
  • The declined engagement. If a form and three rules would do the job, the review says so and charges for the review. One client this year was told exactly that; it remains the cheapest correct answer I have sold.
