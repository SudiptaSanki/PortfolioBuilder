CASE FILE 01 · READINESS ASSESSMENT
Client
European logistics group
Engagement
Readiness assessment, 3 weeks
Fee
€11,000 fixed
Verdict
Not yet — in writing

THE BRIEF

The board had been shown two vendor proposals promising quantum route optimisation “within two years”, and a pilot budget in seven figures to match. Before signing, the Head of Innovation asked a narrower question: at our actual problem sizes, is there anything here?

WHAT WAS DONE

We measured the problem before discussing the technology. The group optimises around 280 delivery stops a day across fixed depot windows — a problem their classical solver already handles to within a fraction of a percent of the best known solutions. We then reformulated their routing as a quantum optimisation problem on paper and costed it honestly: the qubit counts were attainable, but the error rates and shot counts needed for a trustworthy answer were not — not on any hardware shipping today, and not on any roadmap a vendor would put in writing.

Both vendor proposals were reviewed line by line against those three numbers — problem size, error rate, shots. Neither survived the exercise; one quoted a speedup for a problem the group does not have.

OutcomeThe pilot was closed and the budget redirected — most of it to delivery-data quality, the unglamorous input their classical solver had been starving on, which the client reports was worth roughly an 11% route-cost saving on its own. The report stays on file with three measurable triggers — a published error rate, a problem class, a shot budget — that would reopen the quantum question. When one fires, they will know before the vendors do.
“Elena’s report was the only document in our quantum file that told us what not to do. It closed a seven-figure pilot, redirected the money to a data problem we could actually solve, and left us three measurable triggers for reopening the question. That is the most valuable consulting we bought this year.”

The formulation was the hard part

CASE FILE 02 · SIMULATOR-FIRST PROTOTYPE
Client
Materials research consortium
Engagement
Prototyping, 2 sprints
Fee
€29,500 fixed
Hardware
1 confirmatory run campaign

THE BRIEF

A computational chemistry group wanted a working prototype of a variational ground-state estimation for a small test molecule — not a publication claim, a pipeline their own researchers could rerun as hardware improves.

WHAT WAS DONE

The engagement’s first surprise was where the effort went. Roughly 80% of the work was formulation, not hardware: choosing the basis set, mapping the molecular problem onto qubits, and picking a circuit shape shallow enough to survive real noise. The algorithm itself was the easy part — a week of the eight. Everything ran on a simulator first, where mistakes are free and exact answers exist to check against.

Only then did we spend the client’s cloud hardware budget: a short run campaign whose results matched the simulator only after error mitigation, and then within the spread the shot counts predicted. That agreement — not the energy value — was the deliverable’s real content.

OutcomeThe consortium keeps a reusable formulation pipeline and a written record of exactly which numbers must improve, and by how much, before a bigger molecule is worth attempting. Their researchers rerun it each hardware generation. Nobody claimed a quantum advantage, because there wasn’t one to claim — there was a method, ready for the day the machines catch up.

Teaching a team to grade the claims

CASE FILE 03 · TEAM EDUCATION PROGRAMME
Client
Public-sector innovation team
Engagement
2-day programme, 3 cohorts
Fee
€8,500 per cohort
People
24 engineers & analysts

THE BRIEF

An innovation team inside a public body was fielding a quantum vendor pitch roughly every month and had no internal way to grade them. They did not want a lecture tour; they wanted a skill the team would still have in a year.

WHAT WAS DONE

Day one built the vocabulary with the notebook method: one qubit exactly (Sheet 01 of this site is the same exercise), then circuits, noise and shots, each idea introduced only when a claim needed it. Day two was grading practice on real, anonymised vendor documents — the red-pencil rule from the honesty panel: problem size, error rate, number of shots, or it is not yet a claim.

The final session ran the team’s own candidate problem — a pattern-search over case records — through the analysis themselves. They found the catch unaided: loading their classical data into a quantum state would cost more than the search could save. Data loading is the hidden bill in a large share of quantum proposals, and they now spot it in minutes.

OutcomeThe team now runs a standing claim-review checklist before any quantum meeting is accepted, and writes its own one-page verdicts. Two vendor meetings since have ended early — politely, and with the three numbers requested in writing. The programme’s success metric was never satisfaction scores; it was whether the team still grades claims without me in the room. They do.
