Sheet 01 · The signature instrument

One qubit, exactly.

This is the notebook page I open in almost every first meeting. A qubit is a pair of numbers — amplitudes α and β — and everything a quantum computer does is arithmetic on pairs like these. Apply gates and watch the numbers, the probabilities and the phase. Nothing here is simulated hype: the arithmetic is the real arithmetic.

Every gate on this sheet is exact and reversible, and the state always obeys |α|² + |β|² = 1. On hardware, each gate also adds a little error and the state leaks away on its own. Read §02 before quoting any of this.

Sheet 02 · The honesty panel

Shots, noise, and the limits.

Three entries from the front of the notebook. They are printed here, unchanged, in every proposal I send — because a client who has read them cannot be sold a fantasy by anyone, including me.

ENTRY 1 — MEASUREMENT

One shot tells you almost nothing

A measurement returns a single bit and destroys the state that produced it. A quantum result is a distribution built from thousands of repeated runs — shots. Worked example, printed so you can check it: a true 50% outcome measured over 100 shots has a sampling spread of σ = √(100 × 0.5 × 0.5) = 5 counts, so tallies between 40 and 60 cover about 95% of runs. Try it in Sheet 01 — the scatter is the lesson.

ENTRY 2 — NOISE

Noise is the current state of the art

On today’s leading superconducting hardware, two-qubit gate error rates are typically around 0.1–1% per gate, and a qubit holds its state for tens to hundreds of microseconds before the environment scrambles it. Those are ranges, deliberately: platforms differ, and the figures improve month by month. Anyone who quotes you a single exact number for “quantum computers” is quoting a brochure.

ENTRY 3 — ERROR CORRECTION

The open problem, stated plainly

A dependable logical qubit has to be woven from many noisy physical ones, with most of the machine’s effort spent catching its own errors. The demonstrations progress every year and they are genuinely impressive. But a fault-tolerant quantum computer is not a capability anyone can sell you today — and most of the field’s near-term value is research, learning and preparation, not production answers.

Red-pencil ruleIf a quantum claim cannot be restated as a problem size, an error rate and a number of shots, it is not yet a claim. Ask for those three numbers. The reaction will tell you the rest.
