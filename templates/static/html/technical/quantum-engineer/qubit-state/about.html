Entry · The engineer

Dr Elena Varga

My doctorate was in quantum information, working on superconducting qubits — the kind that live at the bottom of a dilution refrigerator, colder than deep space, and forget everything they know in less than a millisecond. Six years followed as a research engineer in an industrial quantum hardware lab, where my job was the unglamorous seam between the physics and the software: calibration data, error budgets, and the weekly meeting where someone had to say which numbers were real.

Since 2021 I have worked independently from Delft, and the work changed sides of the table. The questions stopped being “can we make it run?” and became “should we buy it, build it, or wait?” — asked by logistics boards, chemistry consortia and public innovation teams who were being sold certainty by people with quotas. Those questions deserve an engineer with no quota.

So the practice is built to stay unbought: no vendor commissions, no hardware resale, no equity in anything I assess, and two engagements at a time so the verdict never depends on keeping a client happy. When the honest answer is “not yet”, the practice still gets paid — that is the entire business model, and it is why the assessments in the case files read the way they do.

Entry · Methods, in plain words

Four things, explained without fog.

M.1

What a circuit is

A circuit is a recipe written in gates: a sequence of exact operations on amplitudes, read left to right, like the strip at the top of Sheet 01. It is not a programme that “runs everywhere at once” — it is a careful arrangement for making wrong answers cancel and right answers reinforce, through interference. When a proposal cannot show you its circuit, or an honest sketch of one, it has not yet reached the stage of being a plan.

M.2

What noise does

Qubits leak. The environment — heat, stray fields, the control electronics themselves — continuously measures them in small ways, and each leak scrambles the phase that the whole computation depends on. Gates add their own small errors on top. The practical consequence is a budget: today’s circuits must be short, because depth is spent against decoherence. Every assessment I write states the client’s problem in exactly those terms: how deep, how wide, at what error rate.

M.3

Why simulation comes first

A simulator is a classical computer doing the amplitude arithmetic exactly — exponentially expensive as qubits grow, but flawless where it can reach. That makes it the perfect first courtroom: formulation errors, unrealistic assumptions and bad problem choices all show up there, for free, before a single euro of hardware time is spent. An engagement that starts on hardware has skipped the step where most projects quietly die. Mine never skip it.

M.4

What a shot is

One run of a circuit, ending in one measurement: a shot. Because measurement reports probabilities, a result is a histogram over thousands of shots, and its trustworthiness is arithmetic — the spread shrinks like 1/√n, which is printed, with the worked example, in the honesty panel on the notebook page. When a vendor shows a single beautiful run, ask how many shots it took to get it. That question ends more pitches than any other I know.

Entry · The hype page

Claims this practice will not make.

Printed here so you can hold me to them. If you hear any of these from a quantum vendor — including, one day, from me — ask for the problem size, the error rate and the number of shots.

“Quantum advantage for your workload, this year.”

Advantage is real research and, for a handful of contrived problems, real results. For an ordinary business workload it is almost certainly not available this year, and it cannot be promised in advance — only measured, afterwards, against your best classical method.

“Encryption is broken — migrate in a panic.”

Shor’s algorithm is correct mathematics and a genuine long-term planning issue for cryptography; planning a calm migration is sensible. But no machine today can run it at a scale that threatens real keys. Panic is a sales instrument, not a risk assessment.

“Fault-tolerant machines arrive in [year].”

Nobody knows the year. The honest statement is a list of measurable milestones — logical-qubit error rates falling below threshold, at scale — and those are worth tracking. A date, on its own, is marketing.

“Exponential speedup for your data.”

Speedups belong to specific problem structures, not to data in general. And loading classical data into a quantum state has its own cost, which quietly eats the speedup in a large share of proposals — as the team in Case File 03 discovered for themselves.

“This is an investment opportunity.”

This practice sells engineering judgement, not financial advice, and takes no position — in either sense — on any quantum company’s prospects. Anyone blending the two conversations should be asked which one is paying them.

Entry · Engagement terms

How an engagement runs.

WRITTEN SCOPE FIRST

Every engagement starts with a fixed written scope and a fixed fee shape — the ones printed in §04 of the notebook. No open-ended “quantum exploration” billing.

SIMULATOR FIRST

Formulation and prototyping happen on simulators. Hardware runs, when justified, go through your cloud account and are passed through at cost, with the shot budget agreed in advance.

INDEPENDENCE

No vendor commissions, no resale margins, no equity in assessed companies. If a conflict ever appeared, it would be declared in the report’s first paragraph.

VERDICTS IN WRITING

Every engagement ends in a written verdict with its arithmetic attached — including, where the evidence leads there, “not yet”, with the measurable triggers that would change the answer.

TWO AT A TIME

The practice runs at most two engagements concurrently. If both slots are full, the honest options are a start date or a referral — never a diluted third.

YOUR DATA STAYS YOURS

Problem formulations, data and reports belong to the client. Case files here are anonymised and published only in the illustrative form you see.
