About the binder

The bench, and whose it is.

Hester Marlowe learned the trade the slow way: a two-year foundation in bookbinding and paper conservation, then an apprenticeship at a trade bindery in London, where the work was library binding by the hundred and the lesson was that structure is invisible only when it is right. Seven years followed in a university library conservation studio — board reattachment, box-making, and the discipline of writing down everything you do to someone else's book.

The Marlowe Bindery opened in Oxford in 2016 and has stayed deliberately small: one bench, one binder, never more than three books in progress. Small is not a limitation here. It is how a book's treatment record can be written by the person who did the work, and how the answer to "what exactly did you do to my Bible?" is never a guess.

The bench takes three kinds of work: restoration and repair of valued books, new fine bindings to commission, and boxes and slipcases. It declines a fourth kind — rebinding sound old books to make them look new — and says so in the assessment, with the reasons, whenever it is asked.

Materials

What the bench works in

Materials are chosen for the book, not from a sample card's order of price. These are the ones in regular use, with the honest notes a sample card leaves off.

MaterialUsed forBench notes
Goatskin Fine bindings, rebacking better books Vegetable-tanned, fine-grained and strong for its weight; pares cleanly and takes blind and gold tooling well. The default leather for work that must last.
Calf Rebacking period bindings Smooth and quiet under the hand; tones well to sit against old leather. Less hard-wearing than goatskin at the joints, so it is matched to books that will be shelved, not carried.
Sheep Repairs to sheepskin bindings only Weak and short-fibred; old sheepskin splits at the joints and there is no honest way to make it strong. Used to match an original binding, with the weakness stated in the assessment.
Buckram Library bindings, boxes, slipcases Starch-filled cloth that shrugs off shelf wear and damp handling. The working material for boxes, where the book inside is the precious part.
Linen cloth Cloth bindings, box linings Unfilled and softer than buckram; ages gracefully and tools blind with a clear impression. Chosen when a box or binding will be handled often and looked at closely.
Handmade paper Endpapers, guarding, leaf repair Cotton and linen papers for strength; Japanese tissue for repairs that must be strong, thin and reversible. Machine-made paper is not used where a repair has to outlive its maker.
Hand-marbled paper Endpapers, box linings Marbled in small batches and bought from two independent marblers; every sheet is unique, so the client chooses the actual sheet, not a pattern name.
Gold leaf, 23.5 ct Titles and tooling Loose leaf laid over glaire for spine titles and fine tools. Nothing on this bench is gold foil or gold paint; where gold is not warranted, the tooling is blind and says so.
Wheat starch paste Repairs, guarding, covering Cooked on the bench, reversible with moisture, and the adhesive of record for conservation work — a repair a future binder can undo without damage.
PVA & hide glue Casing, box-making, spine linings Used where their strength is the point and reversibility is not the issue — boxes and new work. Never used to attach an old book's leaves; that is paste work, and the record says which was used where.

Aftercare

Keeping a bound book sound

Most damage to old books happens at home, slowly, and with good intentions. This is the guidance that goes home with every book from this bench.

Light

Leather and cloth fade and embrittle in sunlight. Keep bound books out of direct sun; a book displayed open should rest at low light and have its opening changed every few weeks.

Air & damp

Aim for the air of a lived-in room: roughly 40–55% relative humidity, steady rather than exact. Attics bake, basements damp, and both grow mould. Never store books in plastic bags or sealed plastic boxes.

Handling

Take a book from the shelf by pushing its neighbours back and gripping the middle of the spine — never by pulling the headcap, which is how headcaps tear. Support both boards of a large book when reading, and let a heavy book rest on a cushion or table, not on its own joints.

Shelving

Upright, supported at both ends, neither leaning nor packed so tight that removal drags the covers. Oversize and heavy books are better flat, in a stack of no more than three.

Opening a new binding

A new hand binding is stiff by design. Open it gently, a few leaves at a time from front and back toward the middle, over its first weeks — never force it flat on the first day.

Cleaning

Dust with a soft brush, working away from the spine, with the book held shut. That is all. Anything beyond dust belongs to a bench, not a kitchen table.

Never do these

  • Sticky tape on a torn page or a split joint — it yellows, hardens, and takes the surface with it when it fails.
  • Leather dressing, feed or oil on an old binding — it darkens leather, can stain endpapers, and does nothing for red rot, which is the leather itself decaying.
  • Laminating or wrapping a title page "to protect it" — irreversible, and it destroys value and evidence together.
  • Rubber bands around a fragile book — they perish into the covers and cut into the edges.
  • Forcing a book open flat for a photograph or a scan — the spine you hear crack is the sewing failing.

If a book is damaged, the kindest first aid is the plainest: keep the pieces together, in order, in a box, and change nothing. Then ask a binder.
