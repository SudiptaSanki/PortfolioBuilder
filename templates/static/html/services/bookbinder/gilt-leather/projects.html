Case 01 — Restoration

The Hartley Bible, 1846

A family Bible whose binding had failed in the usual order — joints first, then the spine, then the boards — while the pages the family actually cared about were coming loose at the front.

The brief

The Bible had been read at table for five generations. Both boards were detached, the spine lining had disintegrated, and fourteen leaves of the family register — births, marriages and deaths in iron-gall ink, 1846 to 1962 — were loose, two of them torn. The family asked for the book to be made usable again, and said the register mattered more than the binding.

Decisions

Keep the original boards and as much original leather as survives: the book was rebacked in new calf, toned to sit quietly against the old, and the original spine piece was lifted and laid down over the new spine. The register leaves were repaired dry with Japanese tissue and wheat starch paste and guarded back in. Washing the foxed pages was refused: water can move iron-gall ink, and the foxing harms nothing but looks.

Outcome

The book opens flat and shuts square; 92% of the original covering leather is still on it, by area. The register is legible, complete and safe to turn. A clamshell box was made to the finished size, because the book will be stored upright on a shelf that leans. Six weeks on the bench, charged as assessed.

Case 02 — New fine binding

Field Notes, 2019–2024

A working book, not a trophy: a landscape architect's printed field journal, bound to be opened flat on a desk every working day for years.

The brief

Five years of site notes, sketches and planting records, printed and gathered into sections by the client. The brief was a book that opens completely flat, survives a desk, a site cabin and a car boot, and still looks like a considered object at a client's table.

Decisions

Sewn on tapes rather than raised cords — cords give a handsome spine but a book that resists lying flat. Goatskin over thin, flexible boards; blind tooling only, because gilt on a book handled daily wears bright at the edges and then looks neglected. The title alone is gilt, on the spine, where hands do not rest. Endpapers in a hand-marbled paper the client chose from six sheets.

Outcome

The book opens to a full 180 degrees and stays there unheld. After its first year the client reported the spine unmarked and the boards' corners — the usual first casualty — intact, because the leather was pared and turned with the corners mitred square. It is the case this bench shows clients who ask whether a fine binding can also be a tool.

Case 03 — The box instead

A Victorian photograph album, c. 1880

The right job was the one that left the book alone: a sound original binding, brittle pages, and a family that wanted grandchildren to go on turning them.

The brief

A cabinet-card album, its cloth binding original and sound, its leaves brittle at the folds. The family had been quoted a full rebinding elsewhere and asked for a second opinion before agreeing to it.

Decisions

No rebinding. Rebinding would have meant cutting the leaves from their original sewing to make them lie in a new structure — destroying the very thing that makes the album itself a document. Instead: a clamshell box in buckram, lined with marbled paper, with a fitted cradle that carries the album's weight open or shut, and a handling note card in the lid — support both boards, turn leaves from the top corner, never force a fold.

Outcome

The album stays in family use, housed so that lifting it out does no harm and putting it back does less. The box cost less than half the rebinding quoted, and nothing original was cut. This bench declines more rebinding work than it accepts, and this case is why it says so on the website.
