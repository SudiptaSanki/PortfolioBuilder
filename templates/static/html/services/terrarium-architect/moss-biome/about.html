Hollow Glass / Studio

The studio

One bench, a north window, and a lot of patience.

Hollow Glass is Wren Ashby, a former conservation technician, and a shelf of jars in a former glassworks on Ducie Street. Every vessel is built here, settled here for four weeks, and only then handed over.

01Wren

From conserving moss to growing it

I spent six years as a conservation technician, mostly cleaning lichen off stonework that the wrong approach had damaged. The work taught me two things that shaped this studio: moss is far tougher than it looks, and the failures are almost always about water and air rather than about the plant.

The first jar was a demijohn from a skip, filled with moss from a garden wall and sealed with a cork. It sat on a windowsill for three years and grew a fern from a spore I did not know was there. I have been trying to build that accident on purpose ever since.

The studio opened on Ducie Street in 2019, in a building that used to make laboratory glass. That is not a coincidence: the offcuts and seconds from the glassworks next door are still the best vessels I can buy, and a jar with a visible seam is a jar with a history.

A good vessel should look like it has been left alone for a year, because it has. Wren Ashby, maker

What we will not do

  • No moss lifted from a wild wall or a Site of Special Scientific Interest.
  • No sealed vessel sold before it has settled for four weeks on the shelf.
  • No tropical species sold for a cold north-facing room without saying so.
  • No fertiliser, no drainage "trick" that hides a compacted substrate.
  • No jar that needs opening every fortnight and calling that self-sufficient.

02Method

Why the settle period matters

A jar that fails usually fails in the first month: mould blooms on dead material, moss lifts because the substrate moved, or the waterline climbs because the drainage is too small. We keep every vessel for four weeks under the light it will live in, and we open it, observe it and correct it before it is sold. That is the difference between a terrarium and a temporary arrangement of plants.

The other half of the method is restraint. A jar with three species and a piece of cork bark will still read well in five years. A jar packed with eleven species and four stones will collapse under its own ambition. We plant for the year after next, not for the photograph on the day.

03Sourcing

Where the moss comes from

Most of it is propagated in trays behind the studio from stock collected under licence on land we manage in the Peak District. Some species — fern moss, sheet moss — establish quickly from fragments. Others, like Leucobryum, take two seasons to make a saleable cushion, which is why the price is what it is.

The glass is offcut and second-quality laboratory glass from the glassworks next door, plus hand-blown vessels from a studio in Stoke. Lids are ground to fit, not siliconed: a sealed jar should be openable. Substrate materials are peat-reduced where possible, and we will substitute coir in any commission on request.
