Layer 02How it holds

A sealed jar is a water pump with no moving parts

Water evaporates from the moss and substrate, condenses on the cooler glass, and runs back down. In a well-built vessel that loop closes: the same water is used for years. What you are managing is not the water but the balance — too much liquid sitting in the drainage layer, or too little vapour in the headspace, and the loop stalls.

Moss makes this easier than a fern would. It has no stomata to regulate, so it simply dries and waits. A closed vessel buys you the humidity that a radiator would otherwise take away, and the charcoal and open substrate keep the root zone from going anaerobic. The single biggest cause of failure we see is a jar built in soil that compacts: the water cannot move, the base goes sour, and the moss lifts off the surface.

You are not growing moss. You are keeping a small weather system steady. Wren Ashby, maker

That is why every Hollow Glass vessel is built to a measured recipe: a drainage reservoir you can see, a mesh that stops the mix washing down, a low-nutrient substrate with enough open structure to stay aerated, and a planting surface firmed so the moss makes contact rather than hovering above it.

Layer 03Light

Bright, indirect, and never a south-facing sill

A sealed glass vessel heats like a small greenhouse. Two hours of direct summer sun will cook moss that has been fine for a year. Aim for bright indirect light and judge it by the plant, not by the compass.

40–90 µmol/m²/s

North window

Enough for shade mosses and most ferns. Expect slow, dense growth and very little algae.

80–140 µmol/m²/s

East window

The best default in a UK flat: morning sun, bright afternoon. Keep 300 mm back from the glass in June.

60–110 µmol/m²/s

LED, 12 h

A full-spectrum bar at 250 mm, on a timer. LEDs still add heat; leave a gap above the lid.

Temperature 16–21 °C suits most native bryophytes. Keep vessels off radiators and away from a winter windowsill that drops below 10 °C.
