The bench / Workshop

Above a bookshop, with the window open

The workshop is one room with two lathes, a stereo microscope and a shelf of inks that is not allowed to grow. Mara Hollis started with a box of broken nibs and a copy of Marshall & Oldfield; the bench has been in Lewes since 2014.

01 — The maker

A pen is a tool that has to disappear

Mara trained as an instrument maker, which is where the microscope came from. A flute key and a nib have the same problem: a small, hard edge has to meet a softer surface without either one winning. She spent four years repairing pens for a shop in Brighton before taking the Lewes room, and still keeps the first nib she ever ground in a drawer, badly.

Pens are turned on a Vicmarc lathe from solid rod, drilled on the lathe rather than a drill press so the bore stays concentric, and threaded with a die held in the tailstock. Nibs are Bock 14k blanks, mostly, with Jowo for the smaller pens and rescued vintage nibs when a customer asks for one. Every nib is ground under the microscope, polished on three films, aligned, and written with for a full page before it is allowed to leave.

If you can feel the pen in your hand, it is too heavy, too slick, or not fitted. The good pen is the one you forget you are holding.

The waiting list is fourteen weeks at the moment. The bench takes four commissions a month, plus nib work by post, which is quicker.

02 — Nib work

Six stages, and the last one is a page

A regrind is not a sharpen. The tipping is a welded pellet of hard alloy at the end of the tines; grinding it changes the shape of the surface that meets the paper, and with it the width, the feedback and the direction the line varies. The stages are always the same, whatever the grind.

Strip and inspect

The nib is removed from the section and the tipping examined at 20×. A cracked pellet, a sprung tine or a chip at the slit ends the job before it starts; we photograph the nib and send it back untouched rather than grind a fault into a new shape.

Shape

The tipping is cut on a diamond lap at 800 grit, with the nib held in a jig set to the customer's writing angle. The angle is the one number that cannot be guessed: an architect cut ground for 45° will scratch at 70° no matter how well it is polished.

Polish

Three films, from 3 micron to 0.3, on a glass plate, with the nib rotated through the full range of motion the writer uses. The inner edges of the tines get the same treatment as the outer, because that is where a left-handed push catches.

Align and set the flow

The tines are aligned under the microscope and the slit opened or closed by a few microns to set the flow. A dry nib at 4/10 is a different cut from a wet one at 8/10, even with the same tipping width, because the ink film changes the line at the edge.

Write a page

Every nib is filled and written with for a full A5 page, at the customer's angle, on the paper the customer named. If the line is not right, it goes back to the lap. This is the stage that adds the days to the turnaround, and the one that matters.

03 — Materials

Ebonite, acrylic, and what the feed can keep up with

Ebonite

Hard rubber, turned from rod, warm in the hand and grippy when wet. Cumberland and Japanese ebonite are the two stocks kept; both are cured for a month before turning. Ebonite oxidises to a brown olive with use, which some people love and some do not.

It is also the material for the feeds. An ebonite feed is cut with a comb of fins that hold ink in reserve; it keeps up with a flexible nib in a way a moulded plastic feed often cannot.

Acrylic and vintage

Brooks and Conway Stewart blanks are acrylic, stable, available in patterns ebonite never was, and heavier for the same size. The Caburn uses a rescued vintage barrel and cap with a new ebonite section and a modern converter.

Vintage parts are bought at auction and inspected; anything with a hairline in the cap threads is scrapped rather than sold.

04 — Care

What to do, and what not to

Flush the pen with cool water every three or four fills, and leave it to dry overnight with the nib wrapped in a tissue. Do not use alcohol, ammonia or an ultrasonic cleaner on ebonite. Do not lend a flexible nib to someone who writes with a ballpoint; the tines will splay and it will come back writing double.

A pen used daily should come back to the bench once a year for a clean, a re-grease of the piston and a check of the tipping under the microscope. That service is £25, and it is free for the first two years on a pen made here.

If a nib is dropped and the tipping chips, it can sometimes be retipped. Retipping is £120 and takes six weeks; the nib comes back with new tipping, ground to the same angle as the original grind. Not every nib can be saved, and we will say so before taking the work.
