A bench, a stone barn, and nine months of waiting

Tom Harrow has been making saddles since 2003, in a barn on Beggars Lane with a north window and a wood stove. There are two of us, a hand-cranked sewing machine for repairs, and a rack of trees drying.

01 — The make

The tree comes first, and it comes from elsewhere

We do not make trees. A tree is a laminated beech structure built under pressure to a pattern, and the people who do it well have done nothing else for forty years. We buy ours from a maker in Walsall, half-finished, and fit it: gullet angle, seat length, point length and the web are set to the horse and rider before the first piece of leather goes on.

The leather is English bridle butt from a tannery in the south-west: vegetable-tanned, stuffed with tallow and wax, 4 to 4.5 mm at the butt. A hide yields two saddle seats at most; the rest goes to billets, reins and girth points, which is why we buy nine hides a year and no more.

A saddle is a bridge between two backs that move differently. The tree sets the span; the panel softens the load.

Thread is 18/3 linen, waxed by hand with a beeswax and rosin block. Every seam is a saddle stitch — two needles passing through the same hole from opposite sides, locking in the middle of the leather. A machine lockstitch is faster, and it fails as one continuous thread.

02 — The order

Twelve steps, in this order

1. Fitting

The horse is seen, traced and measured; the rider's seat, thigh and lower leg are measured in the school. Photographs of the back with a template are taken for the file.

2. The tree

The pattern is ordered to the tracing, then adjusted on the bench: width, angle, seat and point length. A trial tree is checked on the horse before the leather is cut.

3. The panel

Flocked in three passes with wool, gusseted where the wither needs volume. The panel is stitched to the tree web and checked for even contact before covering.

4. The seat

Cut from the butt, skived at the edges, damped and moulded over the tree, then left to dry for four days. The seat is the one part that cannot be patched invisibly later.

5. The flaps

Cut to the rider's thigh length and forwardness, lined with calf, and stitched with a 7-to-the-inch seam. Blocked over a wooden form while damp.

6. Billets and points

Three billets, each folded three times and stitched through every layer, riveted with brass. Points are cut long and skived thin so they do not bulk under the flap.

7. Assembly

Seat, flaps, skirts, panel and stirrup bars go together on the tree. The balance is checked on a saddle horse before the final stitching.

8. Delivery and check

Forty hours of riding, then back to the bench: the flocking settles and is adjusted to the sweat pattern. This first check is included in the price.

03 — Materials

What the saddle is made of, and why

  • LeatherEnglish bridle butt, vegetable-tanned, 4–4.5 mm
  • Liningcalf, for flaps and skirts
  • Treelaminated beech, made in Walsall
  • Panelwool flock, long-fibre, gusseted
  • Thread18/3 linen, beeswax and rosin
  • Hardwaresolid brass or stainless, never plated zinc
  • Stirrup barsstainless, recessed and hand-finished

Nothing on a saddle is glued where it should be stitched, and nothing structural is riveted where it should be sewn. Brass is used where it will be touched — D-rings, billets, buckle tongues — because it wears warm and does not rust through a rein.

What we do not use

Foam panels, synthetic flock, bonded leather, chrome-tanned upholstery hide, or "genuine leather" of any kind. A saddle is a tool that has to be repaired for thirty years; the materials have to be capable of repair.

04 — Fitting

Why the horse has to be seen

A gullet width taken from a photograph is a guess. The angle of the bars has to match the angle of the withers, and that angle changes with condition, age and work. A saddle that fits a horse in April can pinch it by July, which is why the first check at forty hours matters as much as the fitting.

We fit in the stable and in the school: the horse is seen standing, walking and in trot, with and without a rider. The rider is measured for seat size and thigh length, and asked how the current saddle feels at rising trot — the answer is usually more useful than the tape measure.

If the horse is not within reach, we will not make a saddle. There are good saddlers who work from tracings and video; we are not one of them.
