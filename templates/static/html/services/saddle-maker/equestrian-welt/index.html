Plate II · the hide

Five years, and what saddle soap does

Bridle butt is vegetable-tanned and stuffed with tallow and wax. It starts a pale havana and darkens with use: sunlight, sweat, neatsfoot and the friction of a boot. Drag the handle to compare the same pattern new and after five winters.

Plate III · the work

Three things a photograph cannot show

A saddle is a structure before it is an object. These are the three places where a cheap one fails and a made one lasts: the tree, the billets, and the stuffing.

01 · The tree

Angle, not width

A gullet measurement says nothing on its own: two saddles with the same width can sit at different angles. We fit the angle of the bars to the angle of the withers, then check it in motion.

02 · Billets and points

The part that takes the pull

Girth strain lands on the billets, so they are cut from the same butt as the flap, folded three times at the point and stitched with linen. A billet that is stitched through only one layer will tear at the rivet.

03 · The panel

Wool moves; foam does not

Wool flock settles and can be adjusted; a latex panel cannot. We flock in three passes, ride the saddle for forty hours, then add or take out wool where the sweat pattern says it is needed.

Plate IV · the stitch

Saddle stitch, and what tension does

Two needles, one waxed linen thread, and a stitch that locks in the middle of the leather. Pull too light and the thread sits proud and wears through; too hard and the leather puckers along the seam. Set the tension and watch the seam.

At six the thread sits in the leather, the seam is flat and the two layers are drawn together without a pucker. This is the tension a bridle is stitched at.

Plate V · care

Leather is a skin, not a surface

Clean with a damp cloth and saddle soap, let it dry away from heat, and dress it with neatsfoot twice a year — not every week. A saddle that is over-oiled goes soft at the seat and stretches at the billets, which is worse than dry leather.

Bring a saddle back every twelve months for a check: flocking, billets, tree points and stitching. Most of the saddles we made in 2010 are still in work, on their second or third owner.

Fitting

We come to the horse, with tracings and a template tree

A fitting takes about two hours: the back is traced, the withers measured and the rider's thigh and seat checked in the school. The saddle is built to those numbers and checked again after forty hours of riding.
