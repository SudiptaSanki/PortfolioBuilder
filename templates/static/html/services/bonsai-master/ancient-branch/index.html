A tree is a decades-long argument with time.

Ancient Branch is a working bonsai nursery outside Portland, Oregon. The benches hold about 140 trees — pines, junipers, maples, elms — most of them in training for a decade or more, each with a written record of where it came from and what has been done to it. A few trees are sold each year. The rest of the work is styling commissions, boarding, and keeping other people's trees alive and improving.

01 — The Register

Six trees from the benches

02 — The Year

The work is seasonal, or it is wrong

Almost everything in bonsai has a window of a few weeks, and doing the right work in the wrong month is how trees are lost. This is the nursery's calendar — the same one your tree is kept to when it boards here.

Visits by appointment · Boarding book open

February – April · before the buds move
  • RepottingThe year's root work, done as the buds swell and before the leaves open. Maples first, then elms, pines last.
  • Root pruningTease out the root ball, cut back the thick downward roots, keep the fine feeders. A tree is repotted every 2–5 years depending on species and age.
  • Soil renewalOld soil is combed out and replaced with a fresh akadama, pumice and scoria mix, graded to the tree's size.
  • First feedOnly once the tree has settled after repotting — feeding a freshly root-pruned tree burns the new roots.

On the benches this season

The Brothers (No. 07) is due its repot this spring — four years since the last. The Gatekeeper is not: old junipers are repotted rarely, and never on a schedule alone.

May – August · growth, watched daily
  • Candle cuttingOn black pines, the spring candles are cut in June so the tree pushes a second, shorter flush with smaller needles.
  • DefoliationMaples in strong health may be partly defoliated once, to force a finer second set of leaves. Never two years running, never on a weak tree.
  • WateringDaily checks, twice a day in heat. A bonsai pot holds a teacup of soil; a hot weekend without water can end a decade.
  • Pest watchAphids on new maple growth, spider mite on junipers in hot dry spells. Caught early, both are a water jet and patience.

On the benches this season

Shade cloth goes over the maples when the forecast passes 35 °C. Boarded trees are watered on the same rounds as the nursery's own — there is no second-class bench.

September – November · setting the shape
  • WiringAs growth hardens off, branches are wired and set. Aluminium for deciduous trees, copper for pines and junipers.
  • Needle pluckingOld needles are pulled from the pines to let light and air into the interior buds the tree will need next year.
  • Wire checkAny wire from spring is inspected weekly now — a growing branch swells fast, and wire left a month too long scars for years.
  • Last feedA low-nitrogen feed to harden growth for winter, then feeding stops. Soft late growth is what frost takes.

On the benches this season

Autumn Ledger (No. 12) colours in late October. Its photograph for the register is retaken every year in the same week, so the record shows the colour evening out across the canopy.

December – January · protection and planning
  • ProtectionPots are mulched and moved to the cold frames when nights fall below −5 °C. It is the roots, not the top, that need sheltering.
  • Structural pruningDeciduous trees are pruned bare, when the branch structure can be read like a drawing. The year's one big decision per tree is made now.
  • WateringLittle, and only on mild mornings. Frozen soil cannot take water, and a soaked pot that freezes splits.
  • The recordEvery tree's notes are written up: what was done, what worked, what next year's single improvement will be.

On the benches this season

The winter workshops run in January and February, on The Student (No. 18) and on students' own trees. Groups are capped at six, because wiring is taught hand over hand.

03 — Work Taken On

Styling, boarding, and the unglamorous essentials

Most trees that arrive here do not need artistry. They need repotting, feeding, and someone to notice the wire biting in. The prices below are the nursery's own — an assessment comes first, and it is credited against any work that follows.

What moves the price: the tree's size, how long the work must be staged over seasons, and whether the roots need a year of recovery before any styling can honestly begin. Weak trees are charged less, not more — the work is mostly waiting.

How a styling commission runs

  1. 1

    Photographs first

    Send photographs from four sides and whatever history you have. Half of all enquiries are answered at this stage, free, with advice or a referral.

  2. 2

    A week at the nursery

    The tree stays for assessment: roots, pests, vigour. You receive a written report and a plan before any money is committed to styling.

  3. 3

    A plan staged over seasons

    Bonsai work cannot be hurried into one visit. The plan names each step and its season — some steps are a year apart, and the plan says so plainly.

  4. 4

    Deposit, then patience

    A 30% deposit books the work. It begins in the right season, not before, and the tree's record is updated and shared at every stage.

04 — From an Owner

On buying No. 09, The Widow's Juniper

“I bought the tree because of its record as much as its shape. Nine years of notes — every repotting, every wire, the winter it nearly died. I wasn't buying a finished object. I was taking over a job someone had done carefully, and the notes tell me how to go on doing it.”

Margaret Ellison, Portland — bought The Widow's Juniper in 2024, and boards it at the nursery each winter · Read the tree's case study →
