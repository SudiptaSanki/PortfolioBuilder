The workshop

A bench, a lamp, and forty years of other people’s repairs

I am Nell Fraser. I trained at WOSTEP in Neuchâtel, spent six years in a brand service centre, and have worked for myself in Leith since 2011. The bench is small on purpose: one person, one watch at a time.

Why independent

A brand service centre is a good place to learn volume: you see four hundred of the same calibre and you learn exactly where they wear. It is a poor place to learn judgement, because the answer is always the same — replace the module, return the watch.

Independent work is the opposite. A 1954 Longines with a snapped staff has no module to replace. You make the staff, you fit it, you find the second fault the first one was hiding, and you decide how much of the original finish you are willing to lose to make it run. That decision is the job.

How the bench is set up

Two benches, one for stripping and cleaning and one for assembly, because dust travels. A Witschi Chronoscope for rate and amplitude in six positions, a cleaning machine with four jars, a lathe for staffs and pivots, a staking set, and a drawer of about nine hundred screws sorted by calibre family.

Lighting is a single adjustable lamp at 5,000 K. Anything warmer hides the difference between a blued screw and a browned one, and that difference is the whole point of a blued screw.

Parts, and what “original” means

Where a manufacturer still supplies a part, I use it. Where it does not, the part is made here to the original drawing, or found from a donor movement with a documented history. Nothing is 3D-printed, and nothing is glued.

Every part that comes out of a watch goes into a small labelled bag with the date and the reason. Some clients want them back; some do not, and they are kept here for two years either way. It is the only honest way to answer the question “what did you change?” ten years later.

What I will not do

  • Refinish a dial. Conserve it, yes; repaint it, no.
  • Polish a case until the lugs are round. A light clean and nothing more.
  • Fit an aftermarket part and call it original.
  • Service a watch I cannot get parts for without saying so before the estimate.
  • Work on anything I cannot test properly — a broken timing machine means the bench is closed.

A short history

Four dates

2011
Took the Leith bench after six years in brand service. First job: a Smiths cal. 0104 that had been in a drawer since 1979.
2014
Bought the Witschi Chronoscope second-hand from a closing workshop in Glasgow. Still the most important tool here.
2018
Started making balance staffs in-house rather than sourcing them. The lathe paid for itself in eleven months.
2023
Began the bespoke calibre programme. Four movements so far, eighteen months each, all still with their owners.

On brands

This workshop is independent and not authorised by any manufacturer. Rolex, Omega, Longines and the rest belong to their owners. That independence is what allows me to work on a watch the brand no longer supports, and to tell you when a repair is not worth doing.

Where a watch is still under manufacturer warranty, I will say so and recommend the brand service first. It is rarely the cheaper option; it is often the right one.
