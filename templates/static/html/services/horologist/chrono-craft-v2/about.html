The maker · Story, method, honesty, terms

One bench, one pair of hands, one record

Anselm Vogt trained as a watchmaker, spent eleven years restoring other makers' watches, and has made his own since 2019. This page carries the three things a buyer is owed before the watches themselves: the story without varnish, the definition of “in-house” in full, and the terms in print.

The story

Restoration first — it teaches what making hides

Vogt trained at a watchmaking school in Saxony — the kind where the first year is files, saws and patience, and a student's own name goes on nothing until the work earns it. He then spent eleven years in restoration workshops, the last of them as the restorer a regional manufacture trusted with its archive pieces: other makers' watches, opened, diagnosed and returned to their records.

Restoration is an education in other people's shortcuts. You learn exactly which component a maker bought in and hoped nobody would count, which rate was claimed and never measured, which “manufacture” calibre arrived from elsewhere with the bridges re-engraved. In 2015 he began drawing a calibre of his own, in the evenings, under a self-imposed rule that has governed the workshop since: anything he had ever criticised in another maker's watch was forbidden in his own — beginning with the paperwork.

The first calibre took four years, told in full in Case File 01. Since 2019 the workshop has completed seventy-four watches. It remains one bench. A second bench would double production and halve the point: the name on the dial is the name of the person who made the watch, and there is only one of him.

The working page

How a watch runs through the workshop

  1. 1

    Design, once per calibre

    A calibre is designed once and then made for years — layout, train counts and plate architecture are settled before production and changed only between calibres, never between buyers. AV-31 took from 2015 to 2019; AV-24 followed in 2021 on the same discipline.

  2. 2

    Component making

    Plates, bridges, wheels and the balance are cut, drilled and crossed out at the bench from bar and plate stock. The bought-in groups arrive from their makers in the same weeks and are checked against the plate's counts before they are allowed near a movement.

  3. 3

    Finishing

    Bevels polished, bridges striped, screws blued — including the screws and wheels no owner will see without opening the back. They are finished anyway; a watch whose hidden parts are rough is telling you something about its maker.

  4. 4

    Assembly

    One movement at a time, start to finish, by the maker. No line, no hand-offs: the person who cut the escape wheel is the person who fits it, and the making record is written as the watch is built, not reconstructed at the end.

  5. 5

    Regulation

    Each watch is timed in five positions over several days and adjusted until it sits inside the class it was sold under. The measured range — not a target, the measurement — is written on its rate card and entered in the record the buyer receives.

The honesty page

What “in-house” means here — the whole definition

On this site, a component counts as made in-house if its material was cut, formed and finished inside this workshop. A component counts as bought in if it arrived finished or semi-finished from another maker — even if it is blued, polished or fitted here. By that definition, counted component by component from the printed plates, Calibre AV-31 is 43.1% in-house and Calibre AV-24 is 46.1%.

The bought-in groups are the same ones almost every independent maker buys, and they are named here in the open: jewels from a Swiss jewel maker (31 in AV-31 — the largest single group in the movement); hairsprings and mainsprings from a Swiss spring works, because spring-making is a separate trade with its own century of practice; balance timing screws, shock-protection parts, hands, cases, crystals, gaskets and dials from their respective specialist makers, the dial from a dial atelier whose work the workshop could not better and does not pretend to.

Any maker claiming a rounder number is counting differently — by value, by weight, by groups instead of components, or simply not counting the jewels. Ask them which. The plates on The Watches show this workshop's arithmetic, and the planner there will recompute it in front of you.

The terms, on the table

Terms of commission & service
Applies to both calibres unless a line says otherwise
TermThe basis, as printed
Joining the listA name joins the making list on a signed order at the printed price. The quoted price is fixed from that day; a longer wait never raises it.
Deposit30% of the order total on joining. The balance is due when the watch is finished and regulated — not before, and not in stages.
The waitComputed from Table 4 on The Watches: names ahead ÷ watches per year. Currently about 8 months (AV-31) and 14 months (AV-24) before a watch enters the bench, then about 11 or 9 weeks on it.
Regulation promiseThe watch is delivered inside the measured range of its class in Table 3, with its own five-position readings on its rate card. No tighter range is promised, in writing or otherwise.
Service intervalEvery 5–7 years. Full service at the printed line (€640 AV-31 · €590 AV-24); regulation only, €180. Only this workshop's watches are serviced here.
WarrantyFive years on making defects, from delivery. Wear, damage and third-party opening are outside it — and the record shows whether a case has been opened elsewhere.
The recordEvery watch leaves with its making record: component plate, choices made, measured rate, and the name of the person who made it. Services are appended to the same record for the watch's life.
