The bench · the horologist

One bench, one pair of hands, one book per watch

Kaspar Aebischer trained for three years at a Swiss horological school, taking the full movement curriculum — escapements before breakfast, hairsprings after — and then spent six years on the bench of a London restoration house, where the work was older, rarer and less forgiving than anything school had shown him. In 2009 he took a small room in Clerkenwell, bought a timing machine before he bought a sign, and opened the first service book. The atelier has been one bench ever since. Since 2023 an apprentice, trained at a British horological institute, works the second stool; entries from that year on are initialled by two hands, and the book says which two.

His position on the trade is simple enough to print: a service is maintenance, not theatre. The atelier does not restore, refinish or reimagine — a sibling trade, practised elsewhere, with its own ethics. It keeps vintage watches running, writes down what it did, and charges for exactly that. “Most watches that reach my bench,” he says, “do not need everything. They need the thing the readings point at, done properly, and the honesty to leave the rest alone.”

The working page · how a service runs

Six steps, in the order the book records them

  1. Intake & timing readings first. Before a case is opened, the watch goes on the timing machine in five positions: rate, amplitude, beat error. Those figures open the entry. A watch that cannot be read cannot be scoped, and nothing at this bench is scoped by guesswork.
  2. The scope decision, made from evidence. The readings and the interval are set against Table S2. The owner is told the scope, the price from the tables, and — where the arithmetic requires it — the watch's value beside the cost. No work begins before that conversation.
  3. Disassembly, cleaning, inspection. For service scopes, the movement is stripped completely, cleaned, and every wheel, jewel and spring inspected under magnification. Findings that change the price are reported before they are acted on — never discovered on the invoice.
  4. Wear parts, on evidence. Mainspring and seals are renewed as the scope provides. Anything beyond Table P1 — a balance staff, a wheel, a crystal — is quoted as its own line and agreed first. The replaced parts are returned with the watch, in a labelled envelope, because they are the owner's property and the entry's evidence.
  5. Regulation, over days. The reassembled movement is regulated and then observed — on the machine, on the wrist-simulator, and across its power reserve. A rate is only written in the book when it has held for the observation period, not when it first appears.
  6. The entry, and the stamp. Rate and amplitude after, parts fitted, price as agreed — written into the watch's own book, stamped, and copied into the atelier's master ledger. The book goes home with the watch. It always goes home with the watch.

The honesty page · the rules of the book

What the book believes, in full

On intervals

The trade's customary advice — service every three to five years, whatever the watch says — is a calendar doing the work of a diagnosis. Modern lubricants do dry and thicken with age, and a watch run dry wears its pivots; that much is true, and it is why a watch at seven years, or stopped, is scoped a full service without argument. But wear is evidence, and evidence can be read: a movement holding its rate and amplitude at three years is not improved by being stripped, and every opening of a case is a small risk taken with someone else's property. So the book's guidance is printed as guidance, with its reasoning attached: every 5–7 years for a daily wearer; sooner when the readings say so; later, without guilt, when they don't. A watch that keeps its rate does not get opened to satisfy a calendar.

On wear parts

Parts are replaced on evidence, not on routine, and every replacement is a named line with a printed price (Table P1). The mainspring is the honourable exception: at a service scope it is renewed as a matter of course, because a set spring is the commonest cause of the low amplitude that brings a watch in — and the old spring is returned with the watch, so the owner holds the evidence either way. Nothing is ever replaced silently, and nothing serviceable is thrown away unrecorded.

On value — the rule with no exceptions

When a service costs more than the watch is worth, the owner is shown the arithmetic — both figures, side by side, in the book — before any work begins, and the decision is theirs, signed. Sentimental value is a perfectly good reason to proceed; it is recorded as one, without embarrassment on either side. What the book will not do is take the money first and let the owner discover the proportion afterwards. Thirty-seven owners have declined on this arithmetic. Their entries are kept with the same care as the services.

What this bench will not touch

  • Fashion quartz watches. Their movements are sealed units their makers replace whole, or not at all; there is no honest service to sell you, and this bench will not invent one.
  • Counterfeit watches. They are not opened, not timed, and not entered in the book. The owner is told plainly, in person or in writing, and the watch is returned as it arrived.
  • Watches needing parts that no longer exist and cannot be made at this bench. These are referred on — to the restoration trade, by name — and the referral is written in the correspondence, not whispered. Maintenance is this atelier's ground; it does not pretend to the whole map.
  • Any watch whose owner asks for the years to be taken off it. Cases are not polished and dials are not refinished during a service here. That is a different decision, belonging to a different trade, and it should be made deliberately — never as a default added to a service.

Terms of engagement · printed like everything else

The terms, in the book's own hand

Assessment£45 per watch (Table E1), credited in full against a service booked within 60 days. The assessment is yours either way — readings, scope recommendation and figures, in writing.
Deposit40% of the agreed scope price, taken when the scope is agreed — never before, and never on an assessment.
TurnaroundRegulation 1–2 weeks · mainspring & seals 3–4 weeks · full service 6–9 weeks, in the order assessments are entered. If a finding will move the date, the owner hears it before the date does.
Postage & insuranceWatches travel insured both ways; return postage is £18 per watch (Table E1), due on completion. Collection from the atelier is free and, the bench admits, preferred.
The book's custodyThe service book stays with the watch — it is the watch's document, not the owner's and not the atelier's. A duplicate of every entry is kept in the atelier's master ledger. If the watch is sold, the book goes with it, and the new owner inherits the whole record, including the entries that say we advised against this.
GuaranteeWorkmanship is guaranteed for two years from the entry's date. A watch that drifts out of its printed figures inside that period is re-regulated at no charge, and the visit is entered in the book like any other — guarantees are entries too.
