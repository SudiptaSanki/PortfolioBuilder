Story 01 · The Tasting Series

From Field to Fire — six evenings, one direction of travel

The brief

The still room's guided tastings drew the already-converted: fourteen seats filled with people who arrived loving peat and left unchanged. The ask was a series a beginner could enter at the shallow end — and that a sceptic could attend without being preached at.

The decisions

Six evenings across one autumn, one variable changed at a time. The progression was printed on every ticket and on the wall: 0 → 8 → 20 → 45 ppm in the malt, same cask class (refill hogsheads) for the first four pours, so smoke was the only thing moving. Evenings five and six held the band steady and moved the cask instead — bourbon, then sherry — teaching the second variable only after the first was understood. Every pour was 15 ml, water and oatcakes stayed on the table, and the first twenty minutes of evening one were spent on what ppm does not tell you.

The outcome

71 of 84 seats taken across the series; evening six — the heaviest pour of the year — sold out first, which was not the prediction. Eleven of the fourteen seats on the final night were people who had started at evening one. The series now runs every autumn, unchanged in structure, and it is where the testimonial on the home page comes from.

Story 02 · For the Trade

A twelve-bottle back bar for a Glasgow kitchen

The brief

A neighbourhood restaurant — call it the Galley Kitchen, because its owners prefer their own name kept for their own menu — was opening a small back bar: twelve single malts, a stock budget of £1,900, and a young team who had never sold whisky and were quietly afraid of the questions.

The decisions

Value first: four refill-cask bottles as the working heart of the list, where the spirit does the talking and the margin survives. One sherry butt as the single top shelf, chosen because the kitchen's food — slow, dark, dried-fruit cooking — could meet it halfway. And the menu printed each bottle's band and malt phenols in plain figures, so the team could talk about smoke from a number on the page instead of bluffing from memory. Two list-building days on site: one to select, one to taste the whole list with the team and write their one-line descriptions with them.

The outcome

The list went live inside three weeks, £1,847 of the £1,900 budget spent. In the first quarter, malt whisky went from roughly 2% of drinks sales to 9% — from a small base, and the owners say so themselves; the point is the direction, and that the ppm column on the menu did most of the selling. The list is re-tasted and re-printed each season under the same day rate.

Story 03 · The Verification

The 1962 that wasn't

The brief

A private buyer was offered a bottle described as a 1962 single cask, at £2,800, by a seller with a good story and no paperwork. Before paying, the buyer commissioned the practice's fixed-fee verification: is this bottle what it is said to be?

The checks

Four things, all at practice level, none of them destructive. The capsule foil was a modern material under a claimed 1960s closure. The fill level stood too high for six decades in cask and bottle — old whisky loses volume; this bottle had lost none. The label paper fluoresced under UV the way post-1980s brightened papers do. And the cask number, checked against auction and merchant records, had already been sold — as a different bottling, years earlier. Any one of these might have an innocent explanation. Four together do not.

The outcome

The verdict went to the buyer in writing, as it always does: not as described — do not buy. The buyer did not buy. The seller, shown the letter, withdrew the bottle from sale rather than argue with it. The fee was £150, fixed, agreed before the bottle was seen, and it is the same fee whether the answer is yes or no — a verification that pays more for good news would not stay honest long.
