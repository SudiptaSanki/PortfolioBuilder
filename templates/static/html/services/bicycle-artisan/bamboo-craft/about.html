Workshop / The builder

Rowan Pike

I started with a broken steel frame and a length of cane.

Cane & Lug is one builder, one bench, and a jig that has not moved since 2016. Sixty-one frames later the method is still the same: measure the rider, select the culms, wrap the joints, test the result.

Ten years in other people's workshops

I trained as a mechanical engineer, spent six years in a composites workshop making boat parts, then four years in a bike shop in Bedminster where half the repairs were frames that had failed at a weld. The shop is where I first rode a bamboo frame, borrowed for a week from a customer. It was heavier than my aluminium bike and considerably more comfortable, and it had a visible repair at the seat cluster that had been done with a file and a roll of flax.

That repair is what convinced me. A steel frame can be brazed and a titanium frame welded, but a bamboo frame can be repaired by anyone with a saw, a jig and a pot of epoxy. That is a rare property in a performance object.

The first frame I built, No. 01, is still in the rack behind the bench. It is 20% heavier than what I build now and the head angle is half a degree out. I keep it because it is a useful reminder that the fit comes before the craft.

What the bench actually does

  • Fit first. Measurements, current bike, riding position and the kind of road you actually ride. The drawing follows.
  • Culm selection by hand. Straightness over 3 m, wall thickness at both ends, node spacing, and a proof load on every tube.
  • One frame in the jig. No batch work. The jig holds a frame for the whole cure, which is 24 hours at 60 °C.
  • Test, then finish. Alignment to 0.5 mm, a 240,000-cycle fatigue test at ±1,200 N, then the oil cure and the log book.
  • Repair for life. Any frame I built comes back to the bench for a repair at the cost of materials.
A frame is a set of numbers and a set of joints. Get the numbers wrong and the joints cannot save it. Rowan Pike, frame builder

Where the cane comes from

Tonkin cane (Arundinaria amabilis) from a plantation supplier in Guangdong, imported by a Bristol timber merchant in 3 m bundles. The species matters less than the selection: a culm that is straight, thick-walled and close-noded at the bottom is worth ten that are not. Roughly one culm in five becomes a frame tube. The rest become jigs, spacers and the kindling box.

The cane is heat-treated to drive off moisture and set the sugars, then sealed with a linseed and tung oil mix. That finish is renewable: a frame that has been ridden through five winters can be sanded back and re-oiled in an afternoon.
