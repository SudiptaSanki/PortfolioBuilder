Hardened, then tempered — by colour

A blade leaves the quench too hard to use: it would chip like glass. So it is polished bright and warmed again, gently, and the smith reads the oxide colours that crawl across the steel. Each colour is a temperature, and each temperature is a bargain between hardness and toughness. Straw keeps an edge. Blue gives a spring its flex. Pick a colour to see what it is for.

The colours only read true on bright, polished steel in daylight — one of the reasons the forge still judges heat treatment by eye, the way it has been done for centuries. The full steel list is on the about page.

240°C Straw

The blade temper. Hard enough to hold a keen edge through a day's work, tough enough not to chip on a bone or a knot. Every knife that leaves this forge is tempered here, at straw — about 60 HRC for the chef's knife in Job 209.

Colours shown are the oxide tints on polished carbon steel (1095/1084 family). Stainless and alloy steels colour differently and are tempered in the furnace, by the dial.

How a piece gets made

Five stages, the same for a gate or a knife. Nothing starts at the fire — by the time iron is hot, every decision that matters has already been taken on paper.

1

Design and drawing

Architectural work is drawn full-size, on paper or chalked on the hood, from measurements taken on site. A gate has to fit an opening that is never square and never level; the drawing is where that gets solved, not the fitting day. You approve the drawing before anything is cut.

2

Stock and fire

Steel is chosen for the job — mild steel for frames, salvaged wrought iron for restoration, 1095 and 15N20 for a damascus billet — cut to length, and the coke forge is lit. A forge weld wants a clean, clinkered fire and patience; a dirty fire will not weld, whatever you do.

3

Forging

The iron is drawn out, tapered, upset, punched and bent hot, a heat at a time. Scrolls are forge-welded to the frame, joints are collared or tenoned and riveted. This is the slow part, and the part that cannot be hurried: steel moved too cold cracks, and steel burnt in the fire is scrap.

4

Heat treatment

Blades and tools are hardened — heated past the point where a magnet stops sticking, quenched in oil — then tempered back by colour, as above. Architectural work is simply allowed to cool and is checked for truth: a gate leaf that is not flat on the bench will never hang right.

5

Finishing and fitting

Work for indoors is wire-brushed and hot-waxed. Work for outdoors is galvanised or zinc-sprayed before painting, so the protection does not depend on the paint alone. Then it is fitted on site, hung, swung and adjusted until it shuts the way it should.

Commissioning, and what things cost

Most work here is commissioned. The route from first email to fitted gate is fixed, and the prices below are the honest starting points — every job is quoted in writing before the deposit is taken.

  1. Enquiry and site measure

    Send photographs and rough measurements, or book a visit. For gates and railings I measure the opening myself within the Sheffield area — old openings are never the size they look.

  2. Drawings for approval

    You get a drawing and a written quote with the steel, the finish and the fitting all specified. Changes happen here, on paper, where they are cheap.

  3. Deposit and the queue

    A 40% deposit books the job into the forge queue and buys the steel. The balance is due on fitting for architectural work, or before posting for knives and tools.

  4. Making, with photographs

    You get photographs as the work comes off the anvil — most clients see their gate as a frame, then as scrolls, then whole. If something needs deciding, it is decided then, not at the end.

  5. Fitting and aftercare

    Architectural work is fitted by the forge, not dropped at the kerb. Every job leaves with care instructions, and anything I have made I will repair — see the promise on the about page.

What moves the price: overall size, how much scrollwork there is and how fine it is, the finish (galvanising adds cost and years), and site access — a gate that has to be carried through a house costs more to fit than one that comes off a drive. Restoration is charged by the day because nobody knows what is inside a rusted joint until it is opened; the assessment gives you the likely number of days before you commit.

What the Marlowe gates' owner says

“The old gates were taken for scrap before I was born, and all we had was one photograph of my great-grandparents in front of them. Joe worked from that photograph and the bit of railing that survived. When the new gates went up, my mother stood in the drive and cried. They are not a copy of something new pretending to be old — they are the gates the house was missing.”
