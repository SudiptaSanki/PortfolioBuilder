The order book, told in full

Three entries, chosen because each one turned on a decision: a trial shoe that caught what a tape measure could not, a fourteen-year-old pair worth rebuilding rather than replacing, and a welt chosen for weather rather than for looks.

Order book open · Resoles taken by post · Northampton, England

No. 231 · First pair

The Oxford that had to fit two different feet

The brief

The client had worn ready-made shoes for twenty years and blistered in nearly all of them. His left foot measures half a size longer than his right; both insteps are high, so shoes that fit his length crushed his instep, and shoes that fit his instep slipped at his heel. He wanted one pair of plain cap-toe Oxfords he could wear all day, and he was frank that he thought bespoke was an indulgence. The brief, in his words: “Prove it isn't.”

At the bench

Two lasts, effectively: the right carved to its own measurements, the left built up at the heel and waist rather than simply made longer, because a long shoe on a shorter heel is a blister with better leather. The trial shoe — rough calf, unlined — was worn for a fortnight and came back with the truth marked on it: heel slip on the right, pressure across the left instep. The lasts were adjusted in both places, the pattern re-cut, and only then was the tan box calf clicked. Welting at seven stitches to the inch; a close-channel leather sole, because he walks to work on pavement.

The outcome

Delivered ten months after the first appointment, worn the same afternoon for a full working day without a plaster. The entry records the result the way the book records everything: he ordered a second pair — suede, on the same lasts — within the year. The lasts stay on the shelf under his name; every future pair starts from them.

R-118 · Refurbishment

The 2011 pair, brought back instead of replaced

The brief

A pair made in this workshop in 2011 came back by post with a note: “Soles gone, uppers tired, but they're my shoes — can anything be done?” Fourteen years of wear, three resoles (two of them not mine, one of them glued work I had to undo), heels worn down into the stack, and uppers dry and lightened at the flex points. A new pair on the same lasts would have cost £3,450. The question was whether the old pair still had a shoe inside it.

At the bench

It did, because the construction allowed the question to be asked: the welt was sound along both shoes, so the bottoms were stripped to it, the cork renewed, new oak-bark soles stitched into the original welt, and the heels rebuilt lift by lift. The uppers were cleaned, fed over several days — never stripped — and re-coloured to their original tone. The honest limit was stated to the owner before work began and is written in the book: the crease cracks at the flex point are stabilised and fed, not erased. Leather remembers. A refurbishment that promises to make fourteen years invisible is promising something it cannot do.

The outcome

Back with its owner in three weeks for £485 — roughly a seventh of a new pair — with an honest estimate of another eight to ten years in them, given rotation and one more resole in due course. The pair's entry in the book now runs to two pages. That, more than the price, is the argument for welting a shoe properly in the first place.

No. 219 · Country boot

A boot welted for weather, not for the photograph

The brief

An estate worker who is on wet ground from October to April and had worn through three pairs of factory boots in five years — each failing the same way, at the seam where upper meets sole, where the water gets in and the stitching rots. He did not want a handsome boot. He wanted one that would still be a boot in ten years, and that could be repaired by the man who made it.

At the bench

The answer is an older construction than the standard welt: a storm (Norwegian) welt, where the upper is turned outward and chain-stitched through a raised flange, so the seam that matters sheds water instead of holding it, and there are two rows of stitching between the weather and his socks. The upper is waxed kudu — a leather whose scars and scratches are part of its surface, so a working boot need not pretend to be a new one. The sole is a studded rubber unit stitched to the welt: chosen for grip on wet grass, and replaceable at resole time without disturbing anything above it.

The outcome

Delivered in eleven months (boot lasts, and the queue). After its first full winter the pair came back for inspection at my request: the welt seam dry inside, the kudu scuffed exactly as promised and sound, the rubber soles worn a third down. The entry's estimate, written at delivery and standing so far: fifteen years, three resoles.
