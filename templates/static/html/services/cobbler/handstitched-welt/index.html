The making

Five stages, and no stage skipped

A pair passes through the same five stages whether it is a first pair or a fifth. The trial shoe is the one stage ready-made and factory bespoke leave out — and the one that saves the most pairs.

  1. 01

    Measurement & the last

    Your feet are measured seated and standing — length, joint, instep and heel girths, and a tracing of each foot, because the two are never the same. From those figures a last is carved in beech and refined by rasp and scraper until it holds the shape of your feet at their best: not a copy of their faults, but room for them.

  2. 02

    Pattern, clicking & closing

    A pattern is cut from the last's shape, and the uppers are clicked — cut by hand with a knife, panel by panel, from the soundest part of the hide, avoiding scars and loose grain. The pieces are skived, stitched together (that sewing is the closing), and any broguing is punched by hand.

  3. 03

    The trial shoe

    For a first pair, a rough shoe is made in inexpensive leather on your new last and worn by you for a fortnight of ordinary days. Where it pinches, slips or creases wrong is marked, the last is adjusted, and only then is the real hide cut. It costs weeks. It is cheaper than a wrong pair.

  4. 04

    Welting & bottoming

    The upper is lasted, and the insole — thick oak-bark leather — has its rib carved from its own substance, feather and holdfast cut by knife. The welt is sewn through upper and rib with waxed linen thread, two needles passing each other in every hole. Then the shank, the cork filling, the sole, and a heel built lift by lift.

  5. 05

    Trees & finishing

    The last comes out and a pair of lasted trees — shaped from your last, so the shoe keeps its form for decades — goes in. Edges are trimmed, inked and waxed; the sole is finished; and the pair is checked against its order-book entry before it is wrapped: measurements, leather, construction, price agreed. Then it is yours.

Before you commission

Three ways to attach a sole — and what each one costs you later

Almost every argument about shoe quality is really an argument about this one seam. Here are the three constructions you will meet, stated as plainly as I would state them across the bench — including the honest strengths of the two I do not sell.

Hand-welted — what this bench does

The insole is a thick piece of oak-bark-tanned leather. Its rib — the wall the welt is sewn to — is carved out of the insole itself with a knife. The welt, a strip of leather, is then sewn through the upper and that rib by hand, with waxed linen thread and two needles, at about seven stitches to the inch. The outsole is stitched to the welt.

Because the seam is leather-to-leather and thread, not glue, the shoe can be taken apart at that seam and resoled again and again without touching the upper — which is why a hand-welted shoe is a twenty-year purchase and a cemented one is not.

The strongest construction there is, and the slowest. It is what you are paying for here; everything else on this page is cheaper for a reason.

Goodyear machine-welted — the good factory shoe

The same architecture, mechanised: a machine named for Charles Goodyear Jr. sews the welt at speed. The difference hides inside: the rib is usually gemming — a canvas strip glued to a thinner insole — rather than leather carved from the insole itself.

Stated honestly: a well-made Goodyear-welted shoe from a good factory is a sound shoe and far better than cemented. It resoles well. Its weakness is the glued rib, which can shift or fail with age, heat and hard wear in a way a carved leather rib cannot. "Goodyear welted" describes a machine process — it is not hand work, whatever the advertisement implies.

The right choice for most people who want a resoleable shoe without a bespoke price. I will say so across the bench if it fits your need better than my order book does.

Cemented — most shoes sold today

There is no welt and no structural stitching: the sole is glued to the lasted upper. It is light, flexible from the first wear, cheap to make, and it lets makers use shapes and materials welting cannot. Most trainers and most fashion shoes are built this way.

The cost arrives later. Glue ages and lets go, and when the sole wears out there is usually nothing worth resoling — the shoe is finished. A cemented shoe is a consumable, priced like one or not.

Nothing wrong with it for a shoe you expect to wear out. Everything wrong with paying welted prices for it — check what you are actually buying.

Commissions

How a pair is ordered, and what it costs

Prices are published because a commission you cannot price is a commission you cannot plan. The figures below are the shape of it; your own quote is fixed in writing at the first appointment, before any leather is cut.

  1. First appointment

    About forty-five minutes in the workshop: your feet measured, your old shoes read for how you walk, leather and style talked through, and a fixed written quote. A deposit of one third books the work into the order book.

  2. Last & trial shoe

    First pairs only: the last is carved and a trial shoe made and worn for a fortnight. Adjustments are made to the last, never to the finished pair.

  3. The making

    Your pair takes its turn in the book. I write when welting begins and again when the pair goes onto its trees — the two moments worth hearing about.

  4. Fitting & delivery

    The pair is tried on its trees' shape checked against your feet in the workshop. The balance is due on collection. Your lasts go onto the shelf with your name on them.

Resoling & refurbishment

A welted shoe is bought twice — once new, once resoled.

If your shoes were welted — by me, by a Northampton factory, by anyone — they were built to come apart at the welt and go back together. A full resole strips the old soles and heels, checks and restitches the welt, and rebuilds the bottom of the shoe on its original last shape. Send them by post with a note of what you want; I confirm receipt and a fixed price before any work starts, and unrepairable shoes come back to you at my cost with the reason written down.

Cemented shoes I will look at honestly and usually advise against repairing — the arithmetic rarely works, and I would rather tell you than take the money.
