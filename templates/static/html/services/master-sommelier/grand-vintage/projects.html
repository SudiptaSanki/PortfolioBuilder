Story one · Kent, 2024

The retiring collector's cellar — and the two bottles that came down in price

The ask

A Kent collector, retiring abroad, asked me to value and sell a cellar of 214 bottles built over thirty years — mostly Bordeaux and Burgundy bought on release. He wanted one buyer, one account, and no auction.

What I did

I spent two days in the cellar with a torch and a notebook before any number was mentioned: every bottle's level, capsule and label recorded, invoices matched against the racks. Two hundred and twelve bottles were as he believed. Two were not. A left-bank Bordeaux from a great year sat at mid-shoulder — I repriced it from £310 to £185 and sold it described as a drink-now bottle, its level stated in the first line of its notes. A white Burgundy had a cork slipped 4 mm below the lip of the bottle; I withdrew it from sale entirely. We opened it at the end of the second day. It was oxidised. It went down the sink, and the cellar's account shows it as a write-off, not a sale.

The outcome

The cellar sold through in five months, bottle by bottle, at the prices in the written schedule — including the repriced one, which went to a buyer who wrote back to say it drank beautifully. The collector received an itemised account of all 214 bottles: 212 sold, one repriced and sold, one poured away. Bin 04 in the current book came from this cellar.

Story two · Bath, 2025

A mature list for a forty-cover restaurant, built over one season

The ask

The Copper Room, a forty-cover bistro in Bath, had a good young list and no old wine at all. The owner wanted a mature section — a page of bottles with age on them — but had been burned before by a merchant who sold the restaurant wine it then could not sell.

What I did

We built the section over one season, fourteen bottles at a time, each with its drinking window printed on the list itself — restaurants almost never do this, and it is the whole trick: a guest who is told a wine is at its peak this winter orders it this winter. I trained the floor team across three evenings on the only things that matter with old wine: standing bottles up a day ahead, decanting off sediment, and what to say when a bottle is corked — which was: replace it without a discussion, and tell me. My fee was a season retainer, and the restaurant bought every bottle at the same prices printed in this book. I took no margin from any supplier, and the owner has that in writing.

The outcome

By the end of the season the mature page was turning over roughly once a month, with two corked bottles across the whole period — both replaced at my cost under the credit I offer every buyer. The section is now a permanent part of the list, restocked a case at a time.

Story three · London, 2025

The bottle I refused

The ask

I was offered a single bottle of left-bank Bordeaux from the early 1960s, attributed to one of the great estates, at a price that assumed it was everything it claimed to be. Bottles like this are the most forged objects in wine, and the most bought on hope.

What I did

I asked the questions this book asks of every bottle, in order. The paper failed first: invoices covered the bottle from 2009, but nothing accounted for the eleven years before that — no cellar record, no merchant, no auction lot. The bottle failed second: the capsule's print and crimping did not match what that estate used in that era, and the fill — into neck — was, for a bottle of that age with that history, not reassuring but implausible. Either failure alone would have meant a heavy discount and a description full of caveats. Together they meant the bottle could not be honestly described at all, and a bottle that cannot be honestly described cannot be sold by this practice. I declined it, in writing, with the reasons.

The outcome

The bottle was later sold elsewhere; I do not know at what price or with what description, and it is not my business. What would have closed the gap: one document — an invoice, a cellar book entry, an auction record — placing the bottle somewhere for those eleven years, and a capsule consistent with its claimed age. Provenance is not pedantry. With old wine, provenance is most of what you are buying; the liquid is the rest.
