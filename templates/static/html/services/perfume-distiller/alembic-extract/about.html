A still, a record book, and a short season

Orris & Copper is two people and a 60-litre alembic on the rue des Fleurs. We make materials, not perfumes: oils, concretes, absolutes and tinctures, sold to the people who compose with them.

01 — The workshop

Why a small still, when a big one is cheaper

A 60-litre charge is about as much as one person can watch properly. The fire, the water temperature, the colour of the first distillate and the smell at the condenser are all read by hand; a 2,000-litre still runs on a program and a sample port. We are not against the big stills — they make the materials that keep the industry running — but the batches we want to sell are the ones where somebody was standing there.

The workshop was a flower-packing shed before it was ours. Stone floor, north light, a drain in the middle and a door wide enough for a charge of rose. The still came from a closing distillery near Vallauris in 2011 and was re-riveted by a coppersmith in Nice; the condenser was replaced in 2019 with a longer coil so the water can run warmer without losing the top notes.

A batch is a date, a field and a weather. The same material from the next week is a different material.

We work from March to October, when the material is fresh, and spend the winter on absolutes, tinctures, maturation and paperwork. The workshop is open by appointment in season; in January it smells of vetiver and nothing else.

02 — The process

From field to bottle in one day, or three years

Harvest

Rose is picked before six; jasmine before dawn, because the flower closes and the indole turns. The grower writes the plot, the date and the weather on the delivery note, and that note becomes the first page of the batch record.

Charge and fire

Fresh material is charged within four hours. Water is added to cover the charge, the head is clamped, and the fire is brought up slowly — a fast start scalds the material and the top notes go flat.

Distillation

Rose runs about ninety minutes at 35–45 °C at the condenser. The first oil is drawn off; the aqueous distillate is cohobated — returned to a second still — and the second oil is kept separately, then blended to the batch.

Extraction

Concretes are made with hexane in a closed vessel, the solvent recovered under reduced pressure. Absolutes are washed out of the concrete with ethanol and chilled so the waxes drop. A tincture is a maceration in ethanol at 10–20%, and we label it as the dilute thing it is.

Maturation

Oils rest in glass for at least a month; vetiver for six; orris butter for a year. Maturation is not a mystery: the sharpest molecules react and the material rounds out. It is also the part that cannot be hurried with heat.

Release

Every batch is analysed by gas chromatography before release. If the profile does not match the reference for the material, the batch is kept as a trial, labelled as such, and sold only with the deviation written on the certificate.

03 — Growers

Named fields, not regions

We buy from four growers and two cooperatives, all visited. The rose comes from two plots above Isparta, the orris from a cooperative in Tuscany that dries the rhizome for three years before selling it, the jasmine from a single family in Grasse, and the vetiver from a cooperative in Les Cayes that distils its own roots.

Prices are agreed before the season and paid in two instalments, half on delivery. We do not buy at auction and we do not ask a grower to hold stock for us without a deposit.

04 — Standards

The vocabulary is the specification

ISO 9235 defines aromatic natural raw materials by the process that made them, and we use its terms exactly. An essential oil comes from steam, water or dry distillation, or from cold-pressing citrus peel — a solvent extract is never an essential oil, whatever the label says. A concrete comes from fresh material and a solvent. An absolute is washed out of a concrete with ethanol; there is no such thing as an absolute made directly from a plant.

What each batch carries

A batch manufacturing record with the charge, method, still, operator and measured yield; a certificate of analysis with the gas chromatograph; an IFRA conformity statement for the intended use; a safety data sheet with the allergen declaration; and a harvest note naming the grower, plot, date and weather.

What we do not claim

We do not call a material "therapeutic grade", "wildcrafted" without a certificate, or "organic" without a current certificate in the file. Natural materials vary; the record is how we make the variation legible instead of hiding it behind a brand story.
