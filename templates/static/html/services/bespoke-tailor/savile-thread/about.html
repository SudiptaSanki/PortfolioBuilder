The tailor

I learned this trade the slow way, because there is no other way. At seventeen I was taken on as an apprentice at a house on Savile Row — sweeping the cutting room, then sewing seams, then making coats under a coat maker who undid my work in silence when it was not good enough, which at first was most days.

Tailoring is three trades wearing one coat: the coat maker who sews, the cutter who drafts the pattern and answers for the fit, and the finisher whose buttonholes and pressing are what the world actually sees. I served years in each before I was allowed to call myself a tailor, and I still do all three at this bench — in a one-man workshop, there is nobody else to blame.

In 2011 I left the Row and took this room off Shepherd Market. The rent is lower, the light is better, and the work is the same work: one client, one pattern, one pair of hands. I take on about forty commissions a year. When the book is full, I say so on this page rather than quietly letting the waiting time grow.

What I believe, after twenty-five years: a suit should be judged at the end of a long day, not the beginning of one — and a tailor should publish his prices, keep your pattern forever, and tell you when bespoke is not what you need.

Cloth: weight is the decision

Clients arrive wanting a colour. What they should choose is a weight — grams per metre decide how a cloth drapes, breathes, creases and wears, and no cutting can rescue a cloth chosen wrong for its job.

Weights are grams per metre of cloth. The bunches in the workshop run from tropical worsteds to estate tweeds; cloth from Holland & Sherry, Dormeuil, Fox Brothers and the Huddersfield mills is ordered by the length, and its price per metre is always shown before you choose.

Everything I make is full canvas. But the other constructions are not frauds — they are answers to different budgets, and you should know what each actually is before anyone sells you one.

Keeping a suit: the care guide

A bespoke suit should last fifteen years. Most are killed early, and nearly always by kindness — too much cleaning, too much pressing, no rest. This is the guidance that goes home with every garment.

  • Rest itNever wear a suit two days running. Wool recovers its shape on the hanger in 24–48 hours; worn daily, it never gets the chance, and the seat and knees go first.
  • Brush, don't cleanA clothes brush after every wear, two minutes, downward strokes. Dry clean at most once or twice a year — the solvents strip the wool's oils and attack the canvas — and only at a cleaner who will press by hand.
  • Steam, don't pressHang the suit in a steamy bathroom to drop creases. Never iron the fronts at home: a domestic iron flattens the canvas and shines the cloth. Pressing is a bench job with a heavy iron and a damp cloth.
  • Hang it properlyOn a wide wooden hanger that fills the shoulders — the wire ones from the cleaner will cut the shoulder line within a year. Trousers by the hems, upside down, so the weight pulls the creases out.
  • Store it breathingIn a cloth garment bag, never plastic, with cedar against moths. Moth damage is the one injury I cannot repair invisibly; check anything stored through a summer.
  • Bring it backAdjustments on my own garments are free in the first year and quoted honestly after. Bodies change; a pattern on file means a new suit starts from where you are, not from zero.
