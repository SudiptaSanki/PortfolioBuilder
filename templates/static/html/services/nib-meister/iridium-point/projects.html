Bench cases

Three jobs written the way they happened: what arrived, what was decided and why, and what the pen wrote when it left. The figures are the ones on each pen's bench card.

The Journal Pen — a broad nib, ground down to a voice

Pelikan Souverän M800 · Bench № 251

The brief

The owner writes a journal by hand every morning and had bought the M800 for its piston and its weight — but the broad nib laid down a 1.1 mm line that filled his ruled pages and flattened his handwriting into print. He asked for “character, without having to learn calligraphy”: variation that comes from the nib's shape, not from pressure or technique.

Decisions

Cursive italic over crisp italic: he rotates the pen a few degrees as he writes, and a crisp italic's sharp corners would catch and dig at that angle. The cursive's slightly softened corners forgive rotation. Width 0.9 mm rather than 0.7 — his writing is large, and a narrower grind would have felt scratchy at his speed. Flow set wet, 7 of 10, because he uses shading inks and wanted the line to show it.

Outcome

Downstroke 0.90 mm, cross-stroke 0.20 mm — a 4.5:1 variation that reads as handwriting, not lettering. The pen wrote its full test page without a catch in any direction and went home in 12 days, charged at the £80 quoted. The owner's words are on the home page, attached to this pen and no other.

The Foil Edge — a 1951 nib, retipped, not replaced

Parker 51 · Bench № 250

The brief

The pen had been the owner's grandfather's, carried daily for decades. The tipping — the hard pellet welded to the nib's point — had worn through entirely, leaving the gold itself worn to a thin, sharp foil that scored the paper and tore on upstrokes. The owner wanted the pen to write again, and was clear that a modern replacement nib unit was not the pen he had inherited.

Decisions

Retip, keeping the original nib. A new pellet of platinum-group tipping alloy was welded to the point, then ground and lapped back to the pen's original fine profile — 0.35 mm — using the unworn shoulders of the nib as the template for its shape. The honest trade-off was stated in the assessment and accepted in writing: the tipping material is new, and no retip can be anything else. What is original — the nib, its stamp, its spring — is untouched.

Outcome

The pen writes a true fine line, smooth in both directions, at its original 6-of-10 flow. Three weeks door to door, £135 as quoted. It is, again, a pen that can be carried daily — which is what its owner has done with it since.

Parker 51 (1951), 14k fine
Work
Retip, regrind to original profile

0.35 mm fine · flow 6/10

3 weeks · £135 as quoted

The Skipping EF — the grind that wasn't sold

Lamy 2000 · Bench № 249

The brief

A nearly new extra-fine that skipped on every upstroke and started late on downstrokes. The owner arrived expecting to book a needlepoint grind — the internet's answer to a troublesome fine nib — and had budgeted for it.

Decisions

Under the loupe the tipping had baby's bottom: the contact point was rounded on its inner faces, so the ink channel never quite met the paper and only the tipping's edges wrote. A grind would have removed good material to fix a shape fault that lapping corrects in minutes. The needlepoint was declined, and the reason was written on the assessment: the pen did not need a smaller line — it needed its existing line to arrive.

Outcome

The inner tipping was lapped flat, the tines aligned, the flow left exactly as it was. An even 0.30 mm line that starts on every stroke, in every direction. Charged £45 — a tune — against the £80 grind the owner had expected to buy. Nine days, door to door.

Pen / nib
Lamy 2000, 14k extra fine
Work
Baby's-bottom correction, tune
Measured result
Even 0.30 mm · flow 5/10
Turnaround / charged
9 days · £45 (grind declined)
