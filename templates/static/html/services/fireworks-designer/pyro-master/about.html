Six weeks, a music map and a site plan

A display is designed backwards from the fire authority's conditions. We start with the site, not the shells: the largest shell the ground can take sets the whole show, and the music is arranged inside that limit.

01 — The process

From first call to the last sweep

Week 1 · Brief and site

A call, then a site visit. We measure the field, note the overhead cables, the trees, the prevailing wind and the neighbours, and check what the fire authority will require. The largest shell follows from the distance to the nearest spectator.

Week 2 · Music map

The track is marked bar by bar against a click. We note the hits, the builds and the quiet passages, and decide where the sky is allowed to be empty. A show with no silence has no shape.

Week 3 · Cue sheet

Every cue gets a time to the tenth, a shell, a position and a colour. The sheet is checked against the product inventory and the fall-out: nothing that breaks low goes over a road.

Week 4 · Permits and paperwork

Risk assessment, site plan, insurance certificate, storage licence, product list and the fire authority application. We do not book a firing crew until the permit is in hand.

Week 5 · Load and check

Mortars are set, angled and sandbagged; the firing system is tested with dummies; the site is walked at dusk with the fire officer. The show is fired from the system's internal clock, not a stopwatch.

Week 6 · Show and sweep

Firing from a protected position at least 75 ft from the mortars. After the last shell, a 30-minute cool-down and a sweep of the fallout area before anyone leaves site.

The cue sheet is the show. If it is not on the sheet, it does not go in the sky.

02 — The kit

What fires the show

Shows are fired from a PyroMate with a backup controller and a hard-wired firing line. Cues are pre-programmed and timecoded to the master audio; the operator can hold or abort at any point, and there is a dead-man switch on the firing line.

  • Firing systemPyroMate, 96 cues, timecode
  • Backupmanual controller, separate supply
  • MortarsHDPE and steel, 3–12 in, angled racks
  • Storagelicensed magazine, 25 miles from Bristol
  • Crew9 licensed, 2 on the firing line
  • Insurance£10m public liability, certificate on request

Product comes from three UK importers and one Italian manufacturer. Every shell is logged by batch and stored in the magazine until the day of the show; nothing is kept on site overnight.

03 — Safety

The numbers are not negotiable

The site radius is 70 ft per inch of the largest aerial shell: a 6-inch finale makes a 420 ft radius, an 840 ft secured diameter. Spectators, vehicles and combustible material stay outside it. Healthcare and detention facilities take twice the distance.

Shell trajectories must clear overhead objects by 25 ft. If the discharge is more than 25 ft above or below the spectators, the separation distance increases. Angled mortars can reduce the distance on the side they lean away from, but only if the tilt is drawn on the plan and accepted by the fire officer.

We turn down shows that cannot meet the distances. It happens two or three times a year, usually a town centre site where the budget assumed a 6-inch finale in a 3-inch field. The alternative is a smaller show, and it is usually a better one.

04 — The people

Nine licensed, two on the line

Nell Ashgrove founded the company in 2011 after ten years as a firer for a display company in the Midlands. She designs the shows and writes the cue sheets; Jon Rees runs the site and the crew, and has fired the harbour show every year since 2014. The rest of the crew are licensed pyrotechnicians, most of them with day jobs and all of them on the same training weekend in March.

The office is on Feeder Road, a unit with a rack of mortars, a wall of cue sheets and a kettle. Visits are welcome, by appointment, and never on a show day.
