The diver

I have never been the cleverest person on a dive spread. I am the one who reads the schedule.

Twenty-two years in the water, the last eleven of them in saturation. Aberdeen for the oil, Bergen for the research work, and a growing share of the year running landers where no one can follow.

From a harbour in Cumbria to a bell at 190 metres

I started as a commercial scallop diver because it paid better than the fish farm and I was 22 and impatient. The first proper lesson came at 30 m on a low-visibility scallop bed with a fouled hose: the surface had no idea where I was, and I had 40 bar of gas and a knife. I surfaced with the bag half full and a very clear understanding that the plan is the equipment.

Surface-supplied work followed, then the closed bell in 2015. Saturation changed the job completely. You stop being a person who goes down and comes up, and become a person who lives at pressure for three weeks while other people go down and come up around you. The skills are patience, arithmetic and the ability to stay useful while damp.

Since 2020 I have split the year between bell work on the UK and Norwegian shelves and lander operations on the deeper slope, where the whole dive is a plan written before the thing leaves the deck. The two halves teach each other: a lander is a bell dive with the human removed and the failure modes made visible.

“The sea does not care how experienced you are. It cares whether the schedule was right.”

What I am actually good at

Cutting a schedule that a diver can execute at 190 m with cold hands, writing an abort that a supervisor will actually call, and running a deck where the person who spots the problem is allowed to stop the job. The last one is a management skill disguised as a safety rule, and it is the hardest of the three to build.

I am not a great underwater photographer and I am a mediocre welder. I mention this because the divers who get hurt are usually the ones who think they are good at everything.

Two rotations a season

I take two offshore rotations and one survey a year. If you are planning a deep excursion beyond 200 m, the conversation needs to start eight weeks before the vessel sails, not two.
