01 — Limits

Three numbers end a deep shift

Depth is the number everyone asks about and the least useful one on its own. What actually decides whether a shift is safe is how dense the gas is, how much oxygen the tissue has seen, and how much heat the suit is putting back in.

5.2 g/L

Gas density

Above about 5.2 grams per litre, the work of breathing rises faster than the gas can be moved, and CO₂ builds up in a diver who is working hard and cannot feel it. At 190 m on heliox the mix sits near 7 g/L; the shift is planned around short bursts of work and long recoveries.

1.30 bar

Oxygen partial pressure

A working ppO₂ of 1.3 bar leaves room for the unexpected and a 1.6 bar ceiling for the ride. The same mix that gives 1.3 bar at 190 m gives 2.1 at 300 m, which is a seizure waiting for a diver to make one mistake with a valve.

1.4 kW

Heat

At 4 °C, a working diver loses roughly 1.4 kilowatts through a dry suit. A hot-water suit puts most of that back through a hose from the surface; when the hose kinks, the diver has twenty minutes of useful hand function and no way to tell anyone until the hands stop answering.

02 — A shift

Nine hours in a bell, and forty minutes of it working

Saturation diving is mostly waiting in a warm can. Four divers live at storage depth for 21 days, two at a time in the bell for the shift. A 190 m excursion gives you 30 minutes on the bottom, an hour of ascent with stops, and a long afternoon watching the depth gauge and your own hands.

“Nobody is brave at 190 metres. The ones who survive are the ones who are bored correctly.”

The work itself is ordinary: a flange, a clamp, a survey point, a valve that will not turn. The extraordinary part is that every one of those tasks is being done by a body that is breathing gas at 20 bar, losing heat through its hands, and has a hard ceiling on how long it can stay.

Shifts and surveys

    Gannet manifold replacement, North Sea

    Fourteen excursions over a 21-day saturation stint to replace a valve manifold on a subsea wellhead. Visibility under 2 m for nine of the fourteen, with a hot-water hose that had to be re-routed around the bell frame on day six.

  2. 240 m

    West of Shetland pipeline tie-in

    Deepest working excursion in the log. Two divers, 26 minutes on the bottom per excursion, with a supervisor-mandated abort if gas density drifted above 7.2 g/L. It did, twice, and both dives came up early.

    2024
  3. 1,180 m

    Lander survey, Rockall Trough

    No divers. Three landers, 14 deployments, and a camera frame that came back with 40 hours of footage and one very confused grenadier fish living in the sample box.

    2024
  4. 2,760 m

    Cold-seep transect, Porcupine Seabight

    ROV work from a survey vessel, 11 days, mapping seep fauna along a 6 km transect. My job was the dive plan and the deck, not the sticks.
