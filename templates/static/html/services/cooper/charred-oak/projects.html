Case studies

Three commissions, written up

A first fill, a rescue, and a slow journey to Spain and back. Each one is written the way it happened: what was asked, what was decided at the bench, and what came of it.

Case 01 · New casks

Forty barrels for a first bourbon

The brief

Hartfield Distilling Co. was a new Kentucky distillery coming up to its first production season. They needed forty 53-gallon casks in a single season, tight grain, heavy char — and because a first fill carries a young company's name, every cask had to arrive ready to work. There was no warehouse stock to hide a bad barrel in.

Decisions at the bench

Appalachian-grown white oak for the tighter grain, taken from the top of the 36-month seasoning stack — the longest-rested wood in the yard. Char #4 alligator, heads charred to match the staves so the cask seasoned evenly. Six galvanised hoops rather than four, because these casks would be moved and rolled by a small crew for years. The run was raised across March and April so the whole set shared the same stave stock and the same fire.

Outcome

Delivered over two weeks in April 2026 and filled within the month. Every cask passed its 24-hour water proof; at the distillery's first check there was not one leaker in forty. Head distiller Mara Ellison's eighteen-month note: “The colour and the vanilla are exactly where we wanted them.” A second run of forty is booked for spring 2027.

Case 02 · Restoration

The 1892 port pipe

The brief

A family's port pipe, dated to about 1892, had started to weep at the chime and six staves were split. The heads carry the family's brand from the year the pipe was made. The instruction was plain: make it hold again, and do not take its history off it. A new pipe would have been easier to sell them. It would also have been the wrong job.

Decisions at the bench

Minimum intervention, in the order a restorer would defend: the six split staves replaced with seasoned oak from the repair stack — old wood for an old pipe, so the movement matched. The original heads kept, re-crozed and re-fitted. Hoops replaced like-for-like in black steel. Deliberately no re-char: the pipe was steam-cleaned and given a light toast only, because its decades of seasoning are the point of it. Re-charring would have made it newer and worse.

Outcome

Five weeks on the bench. The pipe holds, and it is back in the family's cellar as a working vessel in their tawny programme — not an ornament, a tool. About 80% of the original material remains, including both branded heads. They were left with one instruction of their own: keep it wet. Pipes that dry out between uses come back to benches like mine.

Case 03 · Seasoned casks

Butts that spent a year in Jerez

The brief

A malt distillery in Virginia finishes its whisky in sherry butts and was tired of buying ex-commercial casks with unknown histories — wood that might have held anything, for any length of time, dried out in transit. They wanted seasoned butts whose whole life was documented: our wood, our toast, real oloroso, and a known number of months.

Decisions at the bench

Four new 500-litre butts in American white oak, medium toast — no char, because the wine needed to reach the wood. They were shipped to a partner bodega in Jerez and filled with oloroso for fourteen months. The detail that matters: they were drained and shipped back bung-tight, still wet with wine. A seasoned butt that dries on the dock is just an expensive barrel; a wet one arrives carrying the sherry in its staves.

Outcome

Filled with six-year-old malt within days of arrival. The first release drawn from them came out in 2025, and a second order of six butts followed — seasoned the same way, on the same fourteen-month clock. That clock is the product. There is no version of this cask that takes less time.
