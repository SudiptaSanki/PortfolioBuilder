The fire is the specification

Toast is patience. Char is a decision.

Toasting heats the oak slowly, without a char layer, and coaxes out vanilla and caramel. Charring sets the inside alight: the cracked charcoal that forms filters harsh compounds from new spirit and gives up colour fast. Pick a fire and read what it actually does — this is the conversation we will have before you order.

Char #4 — Alligator

The inside is set fully alight until the surface cracks into the deep, scaled pattern coopers call alligator char.

What it gives the spirit
The fastest colour and the strongest filtration of the seven fires: deep vanilla, dark caramel, smoke at the edges, and the rough edges of new-make spirit pulled through the charcoal.
Choose it for
Bourbon and rye that will rest four years or more, and any spirit that arrives at the cask loud and needs calming down.
The honest cost
A heavy char can bully a delicate spirit. If your new-make is light, this fire will bury it.

Times are for our fire, on 200-litre casks, and every shop's flame differs — treat them as a guide, not a standard. Char level never changes the price of a cask. What changes it is the oak and the wait.

How a cask is raised

Six stages, most of them waiting

The fastest part of a cask's life is the day it is raised. The rest is seasoning, fire and proof.

Stage 01

Staves

Quarter-sawn American white oak, split or sawn to follow the grain, then stacked outdoors for 24–36 months. Rain and weather leach out the harsh green tannins that kiln drying leaves in the wood.

Stage 02

Jointing & raising

Each stave is jointed to its angle, then the set is stood upright inside raising hoops — the cask "raised" by hand, still straight-sided and open like a bucket.

Stage 03

Bending over fire

The raised cask sits over a low fire with water inside. Heat makes oak pliable; truss hoops are driven down to pull the staves into the bilge — the belly that gives a cask its strength.

Stage 04

Toast & char

While the wood is hot from bending, the fire does its second job: a slow toast, or open flame to the char level ordered — #1 through #4. Heads are toasted or charred to match.

Stage 05

Heading & hooping

Heads are cut and set into the croze groove, the bung is bored, and six galvanised steel hoops are driven home and riveted. No glue, no sealant — the fit is the seal.

Stage 06

Proving

Every cask is filled with water and stood for 24 hours before it ships. A weeper gets fixed or the cask goes back on the bench. It does not leave with a promise — it leaves proved.

How an order runs

  1. Tell us what you're filling

    Spirit or wine, fill date, how many casks, and the char if you have one in mind. If you don't, the fire conversation above is where we start.

  2. Stave stock is reserved

    Your oak comes off the seasoning stack in order. Tight-grain and French-oak orders are quoted against the stock actually standing in the yard.

  3. 50% deposit books the bench

    Standard casks run 8–12 weeks from deposit. Seasoned butts are the exception: the 14-month seasoning is the wait, and it cannot be hurried.

  4. Raised, charred, proved

    You get the yard-book entry for your casks — oak source, fire, raising dates — with the invoice.

  5. Balance on collection or shipping

    Single casks up to 30 gallons can be ordered one at a time. Distillery runs start at ten casks.

Stated plainly

What a cask can't do

It can't fix a flawed spirit

Char filters some harsh sulphur compounds and the oak adds flavour of its own. It cannot remove faults that should never have gone into wood, and no cooper honest with you will say otherwise.

It can't be rushed

A hot warehouse pulls colour fast, but colour is not maturity. Time is an ingredient in everything a cask is for, and it is the one ingredient we don't sell.

It gives less every fill

By the third fill, a bourbon cask is close to a neutral vessel — still sound, still useful for finishing and for beer or cider, but the first-fill flavour is spent. Plan your programme around that.
