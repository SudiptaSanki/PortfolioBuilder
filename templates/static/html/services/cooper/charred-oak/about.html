The cooper

Trained on a line of thousands.
Working now in twos and forties.

I started at seventeen as an apprentice on the floor of a large Kentucky cooperage, where the line turns out casks by the thousand and a young cooper learns two things fast: the sound a tight stave makes, and that nobody downstream can fix what you got wrong. Fourteen years there — apprentice, journeyman, then the man they gave the difficult repairs to.

After that, a winter in Cognac and a harvest season in Jerez, watching tonnellerie work at wine pace: slower fires, finer grain, toasts measured in patience rather than seconds. It changed how I char. A bourbon barrel and a barrique are the same craft at different temperatures, and each has something to teach the other.

Charred Oak opened in Louisville in 2016 with one bench and a stack of Ozark oak. It is still small on purpose: two coopers and an apprentice, about six hundred casks a year, every one raised by hand and water-proved before it leaves. Small enough that if your cask weeps in its first fill, you know whose bench it came off — and so do I.

What I won't do: rush a seasoning clock, re-char a cask that should be retired, or tell you a barrel will fix a spirit it can't. The rest of this page is the practical side of the trade — the timbers, and how to look after the casks once they're yours.

Materials

Timber we work

Four woods cover nearly everything that comes to the bench. We buy stave wood air-dried only — 24 to 36 months outdoors — and we will tell you which stack your casks came from.

TimberCharacterUsed for
American white oak
Quercus alba		 Tight-grained and watertight by nature — its vessels are plugged with tyloses, which is why it holds liquid where red oak would weep. Rich in vanillin and oak lactones: vanilla, coconut, caramel under char. Bourbon and rye (by law, in new charred oak), most American whiskey, seasoned butts.
French oak
Quercus petraea / robur		 Finer tannin, more spice than sweetness, a slower and more elegant extraction. Split rather than sawn, and it costs roughly two and a half times the American stave. Wine barriques, prestige and finishing casks, delicate spirits that a heavy hand would bury.
Spanish chestnut Traditional in parts of Spain and Italy. Generous tannin, a darker, more rustic note. Not a whisky wood in our yard — it bullies grain spirit. Vermouth, some wines, and regional work where tradition asks for it by name.
Acacia Pale, hard and aromatic in a floral direction; used as head wood so the staves can stay oak while the heads keep a wine's aromatics bright. Heads for wine casks, and small casks for aromatic white wines.

Looking after wood

Cask care, from the bench

Most cask problems are storage problems. Six rules cover nearly all of them.

Keep it wet

Oak shrinks as it dries, and a shrunken cask leaks. Store casks filled, or — if one must stand empty — with 20 litres of clean water inside, changed monthly, in the coolest, dampest space you have. A cask that stands dry in a heated room for a winter may never fully come back.

Swelling a dry cask

Fill it with hot water, stand it 24–48 hours and keep it topped up as the wood drinks. Most weeps close as the staves swell. If it still weeps at the chime after two days, the joint — not the wood — is the problem, and it needs a bench, not more water.

Re-charring has limits

Re-charring means shaving the spent inner surface back to fresh wood and firing again. Each pass takes wood off the stave, so expect two or three re-chars in a cask's working life, not an endless renewal. We will measure your stave thickness and tell you honestly if yours has one left.

When a cask is spent

For bourbon flavour, a cask gives most of itself in the first fill and is close to neutral by the third. Spent is not useless: neutral casks are exactly right for finishing, for cider and beer, and for resting spirits that need time more than wood. What it can no longer do is pretend to be new.

The angel's share is real

Spirit evaporates through the oak as it rests — a few percent a year, more in a hot, dry warehouse. That loss is the price of the exchange the cask exists for. Anyone who promises you maturation without loss is selling you a tank with a costume on.

Never bleach, never seal

Chlorine taints oak permanently — a bleached cask is a ruined cask. And never wrap an empty cask in plastic: trapped moisture grows mould in the grain. Clean water, steam, and air are the whole cleaning cupboard.
