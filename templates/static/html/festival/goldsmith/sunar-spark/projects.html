Each piece of work at this bench is an entry in a book: what was asked, what was decided, what it weighed, what it cost. Three entries are copied out here in full — including the numbers most benches keep in the drawer.

ENTRY № 01 · REMODEL

The grandmother's bangle, weighed in the open

The askTwo sisters inherited their mother's 22k bangle — heavy, beautiful, and worn by neither. They wanted it remade as two slimmer bangles, one each, with nothing bought that the old gold could cover and nothing of the old gold quietly disappearing.

What was doneThe bangle was weighed on the counter, in front of the elder sister: 31.4 g. At the card rate of ₹8,800 a gram that is ₹2,76,320 of metal; less the printed 2% refining deduction, a credit of ₹2,70,794 — written in the book before any design was discussed. The new bangles were designed at 14.2 g each: 28.4 g of metal (₹2,49,920) and two Class II makings (₹15,000). The melt was refined, the pair made, hallmarked and delivered.

The outcomeThe credit covered the metal and the making with ₹5,874 left over — returned to the clients, not absorbed. The sisters paid nothing beyond the gold their mother left them, and the entry shows every figure that proves it.

“She weighed my mother's bangle on the counter, in front of me, and wrote the number in the book before she said a word about design. The credit for the old gold is printed in the entry, and so is the ₹5,874 she handed back. Nobody had ever handed me money back at a jeweller's.”

Ritu Sharma — the elder daughter, client for this entry

ENTRY № 02 · REPAIR

The clasp two benches refused

The askAn antique 18k filigree necklace whose box clasp had failed. Two benches had refused it: the filigree wires are thin, and a cell of enamel inlay sits close enough to the clasp to be at risk from heat.

What was decided — including what was refusedThe assessment (₹600, credited against the repair) said two things in writing before work began. The clasp could be rebuilt in matching 18k, handmade, with the heat kept away from the enamel by working the clasp off the piece. And one enamel cell, already hairline-cracked, could not be restored at this bench — a resin fill polished to look like enamel was offered by no one here, because it would have been a disguise, not a repair. The client accepted both sentences and the work went ahead on those terms.

The outcomeClasp rebuilt and the necklace back in wear. The cracked cell is exactly as it was — declared in the entry, photographed for the client's insurance record, and priced at ₹0 of pretend restoration.

ENTRY № 03 · COMMISSION

The wedding set, and the price that moved

The askA wedding set — necklace and earrings, 22k, one centre stone — quoted in September for a December wedding, by a family who had watched gold climb all year and asked the obvious question: what happens to our price if it climbs again?

What was doneThe quote was itemised from the card: 46.8 g of 22k at ₹8,800 (₹4,11,840), Class III making (₹14,000), centre stone (₹38,000) — ₹4,63,840. The deposit cleared on a Tuesday; the metal was bought on the Tuesday. That is the whole mechanism, and it is printed in the terms: once the deposit buys the metal, the metal line of the quote cannot move, because the gold is already in the safe.

The outcomeBy November the card had risen to ₹9,350 a gram for 22k — the same metal would have cost ₹4,37,580, a difference of ₹25,740. The set was delivered at the quoted ₹4,63,840. The bench absorbed nothing and conceded nothing; it had simply bought the gold when it said it would.
