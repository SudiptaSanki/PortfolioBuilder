I am Sravani Vedula. I dance Kuchipudi, the dance-drama form that comes from a village of the same name in Krishna district — the one that still begins with a prayer, tells a story, and ends with a dancer standing on the rim of a brass plate.

The repertoire

A Kuchipudi evening is built, not improvised. These are the items I actually dance, with the tala each one is set to and how long it runs in performance — not in rehearsal, where everything takes longer.

Where you hear it
Jatiswaram in Kalyani

Adi tala is the commonest cycle in the repertoire: a laghu of four beats and two dhrutams of two. At 80 beats a minute one cycle is six seconds, and the dancer, the mridangam and the cymbals all have to arrive at the last syllable together.

How an evening is built

  1. 0

    A prayer, not an overture

    Ganesha Vandana, three minutes, no announcement. The audience is still sitting down and the evening has already begun.

  2. 1

    Pure dance to warm the stage

    A jatiswaram or a thillana. No story yet — the point is the rhythm and whether the dancer’s feet are worth watching for nine minutes.

  3. 2

    The story item

    Bhamakalapam or a sabdam. This is the centre of the evening and the part that needs the audience to lean forward.

  4. 3

    The plate

    Tarangam, if the stage will take it: a brass plate, a water pot on the head, and a jati that must finish with the last beat of the mridangam.

  5. 4

    Something light

    A javali, a little sly, to send people out smiling rather than solemn.

  6. 5

    The close

    A thillana, fast, and a single beat at the end. No encore; the form does not really have one.

“The audience should not have to know the tala to feel that the last syllable landed.”

Sravani Vedula, note to a student, 2025
