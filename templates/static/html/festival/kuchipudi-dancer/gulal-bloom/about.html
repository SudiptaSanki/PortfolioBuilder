A village, a story, and a dancer standing on the rim of a plate.

Kuchipudi is one of the eight classical forms of India and the only one named after a village. It began as dance-drama performed by men, and became, in the last century, something a single woman could carry on a stage for an hour.

The village

Kuchipudi is a village in Krishna district, Andhra Pradesh, and the form takes its name from it. The tradition is credited to Siddhendra Yogi, a fourteenth-century devotee who is said to have composed Bhamakalapam — the story of Satyabhama — and handed it to the Brahmin men of the village on the condition that they perform it and that the part of Satyabhama be played by a man.

For centuries it stayed that way: troupes of men travelling from village to village at festival time, performing all night on a temporary stage, with a sutradhara who introduces the story and a harmonium and mridangam at the side. The dance was one part of the evening; the drama, the singing and the comedy were the rest.

“The form was never only dance. It was theatre that happened to be danced, and the dance is what survived the journey to the proscenium.”

How it came to the solo stage

In the twentieth century the repertoire was shortened, the items were separated, and Kuchipudi became a solo form on a concert stage like Bharatanatyam. Vempati Chinna Satyam, who moved from the village to Chennai in 1963, is the reason most of what you see today looks the way it does — the faster jatis, the sharper lines, the tarangam on a brass plate as a showpiece rather than a scene in a play.

That shift is still argued about. What was lost was the patience of an all-night form and the comedy, which almost never survives the move indoors. What was gained was a technique precise enough to be taught in a syllabus, and an audience outside Andhra Pradesh.

The body, and what it is asked to do

A Kuchipudi dancer is asked to be a percussionist with the feet, a singer with the face and an actor with the hands. The jati — the rhythmic phrase — is spoken in syllables before it is danced: ta ka dhi mi, ta ki ta, and the feet have to land on every one of them while the upper body tells a story that has nothing to do with counting.

Then there is the plate. In tarangam the dancer stands on the rim of a brass plate, balances a water pot on the head, and performs a jati with the feet on a surface that moves. It looks like an acrobatic trick and it is; it is also the only item in the repertoire where the audience holds its breath, which is worth something.

How I was taught

I started at seven in a converted garage in Secunderabad, with a guru who had learned in the Vempati style and who made us speak every syllable before we were allowed to move. For eight years I was a student in a class of eleven; for another six I was her assistant, which is where the teaching actually happens — you learn a piece by correcting someone else’s foot.

I still practise for two hours in the morning and an hour in the evening, and I still start with the syllables. The day I stop hearing them is the day the dance has become exercise.

Three things a Kuchipudi item can be

The old treatises divide performance into nritta, nritya and natya. In practice every item mixes all three, and the proportion is the choreography.

Nritta — pure rhythm

No meaning, no story. The feet, the neck and the hands follow the tala and the melody follows the rhythm. A jatiswaram is this, and a dancer who is not clean here will not be believed anywhere else.

Nritya — rhythm with feeling

The same technique, now carrying a line of poetry. A padam is almost entirely this: the movement is small, the face does the work, and the audience is expected to know the story already.

Natya — the drama

Character, dialogue, comedy. This is the part that came from the village and the part most often cut when an item is shortened for a festival stage — which is a loss, and I say so in the programme note.

What a student actually does in the first year

Not a full item, and no stage. This is the order I teach in, and the order I was taught in.

  1. 1

    Speak the syllables

    Until the mouth can produce a jati at tempo without the feet. A student who cannot say ta ka dhi mi cleanly will not dance it cleanly, and it is easier to hear a mistake than to see one.

  2. 2

    Stand

    The basic stance, the turned-out knee, the arm that has to be held at a particular height for four minutes. Most of the first month is standing still and being corrected.

  3. 3

    Walk in tala

    Steps across the floor while keeping the cycle. The first time a student walks a full adi tala without losing the count is the first real lesson.

  4. 4

    The first jati

    Eight aksharas, one cycle, repeated until the last syllable lands with the hand. Then the same jati at double speed, which is where the work begins.

  5. 5

    A shabdam

    The first item with words. By the end of the year a student should be able to stand on a stage and not look at their feet.

I take a small number of students, and only those who can come twice a week. Kuchipudi is not learned in a workshop, and a weekend course is a nice way to spend a Saturday, not a way to become a dancer. I say that at the start so nobody is disappointed in March.

“The feet keep the count. The face keeps the story. If either one is thinking about the other, the audience can see it.”

Sravani Vedula, to a student before a first solo, 2023
