Finished houses

Finished houses, with the numbers kept.

Four installs from recent Decembers, told the way they happened: what the owner asked for, what went up, what it drew, what it cost in hours — and what the owner says now. Every one of them started as a spec sheet like the one on the home page.

9.6 m of mini string · 96 bulbs · ≈5 W total · 3 crew-hours

Stars over a brick terrace

The ask. Margaret wanted the full festive front on her narrow brick terrace — two stars, wreaths in both windows, a garland round the door — and one hard condition: not a single fixing in the brickwork. Another installer had quoted her for drilled anchors and rawlplugs. She'd rather have no lights at all.

What I did. The roofline run is short — 9.6 metres of warm-white mini string along the parapet, held by clamps that grip the coping stones, never the brick face. The star frames hang from the window frames on padded hooks. The door garland sits on a clamp that closes over the door frame like a vice, padding and all. Everything — stars, wreaths, garland, parapet — runs back to one photocell timer by the door, and the whole front draws about five watts.

The outcome. Zero holes. The stars hang level, which on an old terrace with nothing straight on it took longer than the wiring. It was the first house on the street to go up and, by the owner's report, the one the neighbours measured theirs against.

"No holes in the brick, no cable across the path, and the stars are dead level. I checked with a spirit level because Dana said I would. The January takedown took twenty minutes and you'd never know any of it had been there."
— Margaret H., the terrace's owner

96 m of mini string · 960 bulbs · 48 W · two evenings

The oak that took 960 bulbs

The ask. The family oak, wrapped the way it has been wrapped every Christmas since the children were small — trunk and main limbs in multicolour minis. The difference this year: the youngest ladder-climber in the family is sixty-one, and the family had agreed, over his objections, that nobody was going up into the tree.

What I did. Two evenings of work. 96 metres of mini string — 960 bulbs — worked from a ladder against the trunk and a telescopic pole for the limbs. Nothing is tied tight and nothing is nailed: each wrap sits loose on the bark, held by its own turns, so it comes off clean in January and nothing girdles a growing limb. The feed runs down the back of the trunk to the same timer as the roofline.

The outcome. 48 watts for the whole tree — less than the porch light it replaced. The sixty-one-year-old inspected every wrap from the ground with binoculars, and passed it.

"Dad checked it with binoculars and couldn't find a wrap out of place, which is the highest praise that tree has ever had. Booking again next year before he'd finished the sentence."
— Ellie R., who booked the job for her father

18.6 m of C7 + 6 m of mini · 93 + 60 bulbs · 59 W · one GFCI circuit · fitted $716

A bungalow built for snow

The ask. Grace's bungalow sits in a dip where the weather lands. She wanted one eave lit and the porch rail done — but her real questions were about January: would the lights survive snow sitting on them, and would the takedown actually happen if the steps were ice?

What I did. C7s along the eave at 20 cm spacing, minis woven along the porch rail, and every connector joint lifted off the ground, taped against melt-water and run back to a single outdoor outlet. I tested the GFCI trip before the ladder came out of the van — on a house that takes weather, the protection matters more than the bulbs. Total load 59 watts, one circuit, nothing trailing across the steps.

The outcome. Three snowfalls, no trip, no dark section. The January takedown was done in forty minutes on a clear morning between storms — coiled, labelled, two bins, gone.

"It snowed three times and nothing flickered. In January Dana was up and down in forty minutes and the bins went into my garage labelled by run. That's the whole review: it all just worked, in weather."
— Grace O., the bungalow's owner

22.4 m of green C9 · 75 bulbs · 60 W + cone tree ≈30 W · fitted $902

One colour, on purpose

The ask. Chris wanted green. Not green accents — the whole house green: the brick washed green, a cone tree of green strands in the front garden, and red bows, his own, as the only other colour. He had been told by two people that it would look like a traffic light. He wanted it anyway, done properly.

What I did. Green C9s at the standard 30 cm spacing along the eave and the awning line — 75 bulbs, 60 watts — aimed so the brick takes the wash evenly instead of in stripes. The cone is a 3-metre frame wrapped in green minis, staked and guyed against December wind. His red bows went on the hedge line, wired in series so they keep his spacing, not mine.

The outcome. The street's landmark. Cars slow down. Whether it looks like a traffic light is a matter of taste; that it was measured, aimed and wired like a job of work is not.

"People slow down driving past. Dana warned me they would, and also warned me about the traffic-light thing, and fitted it anyway — straight, level and exactly as green as promised."
— Chris D., the green house's owner
