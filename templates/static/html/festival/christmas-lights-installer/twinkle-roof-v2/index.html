I'm Dana Merritt. I measure rooflines for a living, and from mid-November to Christmas I put Christmas lights on other people's houses across Asheville — then I come back in January and take them down again.

My one decorating rule: the roofline is the drawing, everything else is furniture. An inflatable Santa has never once improved a roofline, and a gable with its icicles hung straight will beat a yard full of figures every time. If you want figures, I'll wire them properly — but we talk about the roofline first.

Recent work

Finished houses, numbers kept

Every job leaves with a spec sheet like the one above. Three from last December — the full stories, with what the owners say, are on the finished houses page.

9.6 m of mini string · 96 bulbs · 3 crew-hours · Dec 2025

Stars over a brick terrace

The ask. The full festive front — stars, window wreaths, a garland at the door — on a narrow brick terrace, with not one fixing in the brickwork. The owner had been quoted for drilled anchors by someone else and didn't want them.

What I did. A 9.6 m run of warm-white minis along the parapet on clamps that grip the coping, two star frames hung from the window frames, and the garland on a door-frame clamp. One timer runs the lot; the whole front draws about 5 watts.

The outcome. Zero holes in the brick. The stars are level — the owner checked with a spirit level, because I told her she would want to.

"No holes in the brick, no cable across the path, and the stars are dead level. I checked with a spirit level because Dana said I would."
— Margaret H., the terrace's owner

96 m of mini string · 960 bulbs · 48 W · two evenings · Dec 2025

The oak that took 960 bulbs

The ask. Wrap the big front-garden oak — trunk and main limbs — in multicolour minis, the way the family has always done it, except this year nobody in the family was going up a ladder into a tree.

What I did. 96 metres of mini string over two evenings, worked from a ladder and a telescopic pole. Nothing tied tight, nothing nailed to the tree; the wraps sit loose and come off in January so nothing girdles a limb.

The outcome. 48 watts total, on the same photocell timer as the roofline, and a tree the whole street now navigates by.

18.6 m of C7 · 93 bulbs · one GFCI circuit · Dec 2025

A bungalow built for snow

The ask. A small bungalow, one eave and a porch rail — and an owner whose real worry was weather: snow on the roof, ice on the steps, and whether the January takedown would happen at all.

What I did. C7s along the eave, minis on the porch rail, every connector joint raised off the ground, taped, and run back to a single tested GFCI outlet. Total load 59 watts.

The outcome. Three snowfalls without a trip or a dark bulb, and the January takedown took forty minutes, bins labelled, done before the next storm.

How a job runs

Measure, spec, fit, check, take down

  1. 1

    Measure

    A visit with a ladder, a tape and a camera. I measure at the eave, count the gables, check the outdoor outlet and its GFCI, and photograph every run the lights will take.

  2. 2

    Spec

    Within two days you get the spec sheet — metres, bulbs, load, clips, crew time, fitted price — the same sheet that sits at the top of this page. Nothing is ordered until you've seen it.

  3. 3

    Book the week

    Install weeks are real and they close. Two December weeks are already full this year; when a week fills, the estimator and the booking form both say so.

  4. 4

    Fit & check

    Clips matched to your roof material, strings cut to length on site, every bulb tested at the ground before it goes up. One mid-season visit is included — if a bulb dies, I swap it.

  5. 5

    January takedown

    Everything comes down in January, coiled and labelled by run — "front eave", "oak, lower limbs" — so next year's install starts from a tidy shelf, not a tangle.

The season, plainly

Bookings open in October. Installs run mid-November to mid-December, takedowns through January. If the roof is icy, we rebook — a late install beats a hospital visit.
