The work Panels The studio Commission

A workshop in a house, run by a family that never quite stopped being apprentices.

Meenakshi Raghavan is the third person in this family to pipe gesso onto a board. Her grandfather painted processional panels; her father painted film posters to keep the studio open; she paints gods, and teaches four students a year to do the same.

The studio is the front two rooms of a house on South Main Street that my grandfather bought in 1974 with money from a commission for a temple in Kumbakonam. The ceiling is high, the light comes from the west, and in the afternoon the gold on the drying rack catches it and throws a stripe across the wall. Nobody in the family has ever wanted to move.

My grandfather, R. Srinivasan, learned the craft in the 1950s from a painter in Thanjavur who had learned it from a painter who worked for the Maratha court. The line is not grand — Tanjore painting was a workshop trade, not a fine art — and that is exactly why it survived: it was taught by doing, in a room, with a master who corrected your hand a hundred times a day.

My father added the gold. Not the material — that was always there — but the density of it. He had a period in the eighties when the studio painted film hoardings to stay alive, and when the commissions came back he brought a poster-maker's sense of scale with him: bigger borders, heavier gilding, a figure that reads from the far end of a hall. Some purists dislike it. It is the reason our panels are recognisable across a room, which is what a Tanjore panel is for.

The gold is not decoration. It is the light in the room. You are not painting a god on a gold background; you are building a lamp.

What is on the bench

Jackwood from a sawmill near Pudukkottai, seasoned two years in the yard behind the house. Chalk from a supplier in Tiruvannamalai, ground with Arabic gum into a paste that has to be used the same day. Gold foil at 0.2 micron, bought in books of twenty-five leaves from a dealer in Chennai, kept in a tin with a piece of camphor to stop it sticking.

The colours are stone: red ochre, orpiment yellow, indigo, terre verte, lamp black. We grind them on a granite slab, which takes an hour for a colour nobody will notice is slightly wrong. We do it anyway, because the alternative is a tube, and a tube looks like a tube after ten years.

What we do not have: a printer, a projector, a laser cutter, or a computer in the workroom. There is one laptop in the office for invoices and this website, and my daughter runs both.

Restoration, which nobody asks about

A third of the studio's work is repair. Panels come back after forty years with the gesso lifting at the edges, the gold gone green from sulphur in the air, or a figure scrubbed with a wet cloth by somebody's housemaid. We lift the old gesso where it has failed, re-pipe the missing ornament, and re-gild only that area, matching the wear of the rest so the repair does not announce itself. A panel that has been restored should look its age, not newer.

We will not repaint a face. If the face has gone, the panel becomes a border and a background, and we say so before we start.

The students

Four a year, one day a week, eight months. They start with chalk and a palette knife and they do not touch gold for the first three months. Most of them are surprised by how physical it is: you stand to pipe gesso, you stand to gild, you stand to paint. By the end of the year each of them has made a small panel — twelve by sixteen inches — and half of them have decided that they would rather look at panels than make them, which is a perfectly good outcome and I tell them so.
