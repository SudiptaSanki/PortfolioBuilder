The light in a Tanjore panel comes from the gold, not from the room.

We make panels in the old way: jackwood board, chalk and gum gesso raised by hand, 22-carat gold foil pressed into the gesso while it is still tacky, and colour ground from stone. A panel of eighteen by twenty-four inches takes eleven weeks and four pairs of hands, and most of that time is waiting for something to dry.

A Tanjore panel is not a picture of a god; it is a furnished room for one. The gesso lifts the ornament off the board, the gold is pressed into it while the ground is tacky, and the figure is painted last, in flat colour, with no shadow anywhere.

Eleven weeks, in order

People ask why a panel costs what it costs. This is the answer, laid out: six stages, most of them waiting. A panel cannot be hurried by working longer hours; it can only be hurried by taking shortcuts that show ten years later, when the gesso lifts and the gold goes green.

  1. 01

    The board

    Jackwood, seasoned for two years and joined with a batten behind so it cannot cup. Three coats of tamarind seed paste, then a cloth, then chalk. Two days.

  2. 02

    The gesso

    Chalk and Arabic gum, ground and sieved, piped through a cone onto the board in relief. Every bead, every petal, every jewel is raised by hand. Four days of piping, one week of drying.

  3. 03

    The gold

    Gold foil is laid on a tacky ground made from gum and a little oil, pressed with cotton wool and cut away from the areas that will be painted. A slip of the knife and the leaf is gone. Three weeks.

  4. 04

    The colour

    Stone colours — red ochre, orpiment, indigo, terre verte — ground with gum and laid flat, with no shading. The face and hands are painted last, in one sitting, because a face done twice loses its expression. Two weeks.

  5. 05

    The finishing

    Gold outlines over the colour, the eyes opened, the jewels dotted, a thin lacquer over the gold so it does not tarnish in a coastal house. One week.

  6. 06

    The waiting

    Then it hangs in the studio for a fortnight before it is framed and sent, because a panel that is going to crack will crack in that fortnight and not in your house. Two weeks.

Teaching, and the workshop in July

I take four students a year into the studio, one day a week for eight months, starting with the gesso and ending with a small panel of their own. It is not a craft-class weekend; it is the actual work, at the actual pace, and most people are surprised by how much of it is grinding chalk.

In July the studio runs a five-day workshop for people who want to understand the process without committing to a year. Twelve places, no experience needed, and you will leave with a board you have gilded yourself — badly, probably, but yours.
