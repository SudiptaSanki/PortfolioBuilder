Six garments,
and what they taught me.

A sherwani is a set of decisions made in a fixed order: collar, closure, cloth, length, work. These are six of the last hundred and eighteen, each with the one detail that had to be solved before the cloth could be cut.

P-118 · 2026

The ivory bandhgala

Cloth
Undyed Bhagalpur silk
Collar
Bandhgala, 1¾ in
Work
Tilla, chest and cuffs
Length
Calf, 42 in

Made for a groom who wanted to wear white without looking like the priest. The solution was texture rather than colour: a slubby silk, tilla only on the chest and cuffs, and a hem taken up two inches so the shoes showed. He was married in it at four in the afternoon and changed for dinner, which his mother still mentions.

P-121 · 2025

The green achkan

Cloth
Cotton velvet, forest
Collar
Bandhgala, 2 in
Work
Zardozi, collar and hem
Length
Floor, 48 in

A sitar player who needed to sit cross-legged on stage for two hours without the garment riding up. We cut the back panel with an extra inch of ease and put a hidden pleat at each side seam; from the audience it looks like a plain achkan, and from the stage he can fold his legs.

P-124 · 2025

The oxblood angrakha

Cloth
Banarasi katan brocade
Collar
Nehru, 1½ in
Work
Chikankari, front panel
Length
Knee, 38 in

For a sangeet, where the client intended to dance and did. An angrakha wraps across the body, so the chikankari had to be worked on a single panel before cutting, not after — the embroidery runs with the wrap, and the join falls where the cloth overlaps. He tore nothing.

P-127 · 2025

The linen jodhpuri

Cloth
Lucknow handloom linen
Collar
Bandhgala, 1¼ in
Work
None
Length
Hip, 32 in

A June wedding in Jodhpur, 44 degrees at four in the afternoon. We made a jodhpuri rather than a sherwani, half-canvassed, unlined at the body, with a softer collar so it did not stand away from the neck. The client’s only instruction was that he should be able to take his jacket off and still look dressed; he did, at nine, and he did.

P-130 · 2024

The grey shawl collar

Cloth
Grey silk, mill-woven
Collar
Shawl, hand-rolled
Work
Tilla, shoulders only
Length
Calf, 42 in

Father of the bride, sixty-one, and unwilling to wear anything that looked like a costume. The shawl collar is the quietest of the three, and the tilla was kept to the shoulder line so that it catches light when he turns but reads as plain from the front. He has worn it twice since, to other people’s weddings.

P-133 · 2024

The black achkan

Cloth
Black Banarasi brocade
Collar
Bandhgala, 2 in
Work
Concealed placket, zari edge
Length
Floor, 48 in

For a theatre actor playing a nineteenth-century courtier, which meant the garment had to survive eight shows a week. The cloth is a sturdy brocade, the placket is concealed so there is nothing to catch on a sword belt, and the hem was faced with a strip of the same cloth so it could be let down twice as he lost weight during the run.

How to read a pattern number

P-118 is the hundred-and-eighteenth garment cut in this shop since my father began numbering in 1994. The number is written inside the left facing, with the client’s initials and the month. When a garment comes back for alteration — and about one in six does — the number tells us which cutter made the armhole, and what we did wrong.
