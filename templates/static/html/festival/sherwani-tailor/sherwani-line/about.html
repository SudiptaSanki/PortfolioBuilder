The tailor · third generation of scissors

I was taught to
take the armhole seriously.

Everything else in a sherwani can be corrected at a fitting. The armhole cannot. My father said that on my first day at the cutting table in 2004, and I have not yet found the exception.

The shop

My father, Ramesh Saxena, opened Sherwani Line in 1987 in a first-floor room above a bookshop in Hazratganj. He had been a cutter for eleven years in a shop near Aminabad, and he left because the owner wanted him to speed up. He took two tailors and a hand-cranked Singer with him, and for the first two years he made blouses and school uniforms while the sherwani orders came in slowly.

I grew up in that room, doing homework on a cutting table and being told not to touch the chalk. I trained as a textile designer in Ahmedabad, came back in 2004, and spent four years being taught the parts of the trade that a degree does not cover: how to read a shoulder from across the room, how to pad a chest so it holds without stiffening, how to tell a client that the cloth he has bought is wrong for the cut he wants.

He died in 2012, in August, with three garments unfinished. I closed the shop for eleven days and then finished them myself — badly, in one case, and the client was kind about it. That garment came back twice; I altered it both times for nothing, and I still have the pattern. It is the last one in the book with my father’s handwriting on the envelope.

Since then the shop has stayed at twelve garments a month, sometimes fewer. I have been asked to expand four times, twice by people with money and once by a television company. Each time I have said no, because the number twelve is not a business plan; it is the number of chests I can pad in a month without one of them being rushed.

The order matters: each measurement is taken against the one before it, so an error shows up immediately rather than at the toile. These are the first eight.

The remaining fourteen are recorded on the client card — biceps, forearm, wrist, back width, front chest, shoulder slope (photographed from behind), neck-to-waist, waist-to-hem, trouser waist, hip, inseam, height, posture, and the one we call “carry”, which is how he stands when he is not being measured.

02

Who actually
makes the garment.

  • Master cutter · 1989

    Iqbal Ahmed

    Cuts every pattern in the shop and has done since my father hired him at twenty-two. He refuses to use a rotary cutter and sharpens his shears on a stone every Friday. When he retires, the shop closes.

  • Tailor · 1998

    Ram Khelawan

    Sets sleeves and collars, which is the part that shows. Has a rule that no sleeve goes in before four in the afternoon, because the light in the room is better then.

  • Embroidery · 2001

    Naseem Bano

    Runs the tilla and zardozi work with two women from her lane. Keeps her own ledger of hours per garment, which is the only accurate record of what embroidery really costs.

  • Presser · 1987

    Bhola Prasad

    The first person my father hired and the last to touch a garment. Presses with a coal iron he has had since 1979. Says a badly pressed sherwani undoes a good fitting, and he is right.

03

The short history.

  1. 1987

    Ramesh Saxena opens the shop with two tailors and a hand-cranked Singer. First order: a school uniform, forty pieces.

  2. 1994

    Begins numbering garments. P-001 is a black bandhgala for a lawyer in Civil Lines, delivered in eleven days.

  3. 2004

    Trisha joins the cutting table after four years in Ahmedabad. Spends the first year on toiles only.

  4. 2012

    Ramesh dies in August. Trisha finishes the three open garments and takes over the shop in October.

  5. 2017

    The shop stops taking film and advertising work, which had begun to crowd out weddings.

  6. 2026

    P-118 delivered in February. The order book opens twice a year and closes at twelve.
