The kitchen

Four cauldrons, one scale, no shortcuts.

The shop is eleven feet wide. Behind it is a kitchen with four burners, two men who have worked here longer than I have, and a brass scale that my grandfather bought in 1961.

Shop opened: 1952, by Bansi Lal Halwai

Third generation: Nandini, since 2011

Kitchen starts: 04:30 · Counter opens: 09:00

I did not want the shop.

I trained as a food technologist in Mysore and spent four years in a factory that made shelf-stable sweets for railways. The work was clean and exact and it had nothing to do with what my grandfather did in that room, where the deciding instrument was his palm.

He died in 2010. I came back for the thirteenth day and stayed because the shop had a queue on it and nobody to run the burners. The first thing I did was throw out the vanaspati and go back to ghee, which halved the margin and doubled the queue.

The second thing was harder: I stopped making fourteen sweets and cut the counter to nine. Kaju katli, motichoor, besan barfi, pista roll, sohan papdi, rose sandesh, dry fruit chikki, gulab jamun, and one seasonal tray that changes. Everything else went.

There is no online ordering from this kitchen and no delivery app. Boxes are packed at the counter, weighed in front of you, and tied by hand. On a normal day that is around forty kilos. On Diwali week it is nine hundred and none of it is made the day before.

Seventy-three years

  1. 1952

    A shutter and two trays

    Bansi Lal opens the shop with besan barfi and motichoor, made on a single burner in the room behind the counter.

  2. 1961

    The brass scale

    The scale still at the counter is bought second-hand from a shop closing in Khari Baoli. It is checked against a set of standard weights every January.

  3. 1979

    Kaju katli arrives

    Cashew becomes affordable enough to sell by weight in Delhi. The diamond cut and the silver leaf come from a Lucknow halwai who trained with the family for a season.

  4. 1998

    The second burner

    The kitchen is extended by six feet into the courtyard. Sohan papdi is added for the winter months and never removed.

  5. 2011

    Nandini takes the counter

    The menu is cut from fourteen to nine, vanaspati is replaced with ghee, and the first festival order book is started — a school notebook, still in use.

  6. 2020

    The year of small boxes

    With weddings cancelled, the shop sells 200-gram boxes to households for four months. That box is now the standard nine.

What the counter checks

Four rules, written on the wall

They are painted in Hindi above the burner, where the men can see them and I can pretend I put them there for hygiene.

  1. 01

    No colour, no essence

    Saffron for yellow, rose water for pink, pistachio for green. If a sweet needs a bottle to look right, the recipe is wrong.

  2. 02

    Weigh after, not before

    Pieces go on the scale as they are, not as the mould says they should be. A kaju katli that comes out 11 g is sold as 11 g.

  3. 03

    Nothing fried twice

    Ghee is filtered and used for one day's batch of laddoos, then it goes to the barfi. It never sees a third day.

  4. 04

    Silver only where it belongs

    Silver leaf on kaju katli and pista roll. Nowhere else. Gold leaf only when the order asks and pays for it.
