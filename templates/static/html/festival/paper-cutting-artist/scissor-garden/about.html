The hand · a short biography

Cutting is a way
of listening.

I work at a table my grandfather built from a door. The drawer under it holds 196 stencils, some of them older than my mother. When people ask what sanjhi is, I say: it is the discipline of taking away paper until only the god is left.

Lineage

  1. 1948

    Gopal Verma

    Set up a stall near the Potra Kund after the family moved from Barsana. Cut festival stencils for the temples of the parikrama road and sold them for four annas.

  2. 1976

    Ram Prakash Verma

    My father took sanjhi off the temple tank and onto paper for sale. He cut the first peacock that was allowed inside a Delhi drawing room, and never quite forgave himself for the frame.

  3. 2003

    Nandini Verma

    I began cutting at eleven, on the floor, with the paper my father rejected. I now run the workshop and teach the Sunday fold to anyone who wants it.

How a sheet is made

Five stages,
one breath each.

A 42 cm sheet takes between nine and twenty hours of cutting. The drawing is never on the paper; it is held in the head and repeated by the fold.

  1. 01

    Choose the paper

    Rag paper, 90 gsm, sized with starch and pressed under a stone for a week. The sheet must be slightly damp when cut, or the blade drags and the rim frays.

  2. 02

    Fold the wedge

    Four, six or eight folds depending on the symmetry of the motif. A peacock needs eight; a lotus wants six; a name in devanagari is cut flat, with no fold at all.

  3. 03

    Cut from the rim in

    The scissors enter at the border and travel inward. Every cut must return to the edge it came from, or the sheet loses a piece and the sheet is over.

  4. 04

    Open and read

    The first unfold is the only honest critic. If the negative space does not hold a figure, no amount of extra cutting will save it. Most of my practice sheets die here.

  5. 05

    Press and float

    Seven days under weight, then a night on water with pigment to test the stencil. Only after it has held colour once does it go into the drawer for sale.

In the drawer

The toolkit

Tailor’s scissors, 1948
Gopal Verma’s, resharpened eleven times. The hinge is shimmed with a sliver of banyan wood.
Straight blade, 6 mm
For interiors where the scissors cannot turn. Handle wrapped in cloth so it does not roll.
Stone slab
A piece of Mathura sandstone, flat enough to cut on and heavy enough not to move.
Tailor’s chalk
Only for the commission sheet, never for the design. Marks are erased with a dry brush, not water.
Pigment set
Six dry colours ground from stone and flower: vermilion, indigo, lamp black, ochre, lime, and the pink of the tank at dusk.
