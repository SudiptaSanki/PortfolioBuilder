01

The telling

No screen. No microphone. One lamp, and everyone close enough to see faces.

I came to storytelling through theatre, which is a polite way of saying I could not stop talking in the wings. At nineteen I heard a dastango in a Lucknow drawing room hold thirty people for two hours with nothing but a register of voices and a walking stick. He did not perform the story at us. He let us watch him remember it.

That is the work now. A baithak is not a lecture and it is not a play. The listener is close enough to change how the room breathes, and the teller uses that. When the audience leans in, the voice drops. When a child laughs in the wrong place, the tale makes room for it.

Every night has the same architecture: a welcome that names the courtyard, a short invocation that is not a prayer for everyone in the room, one long tale and one short one, an interval with tea, and a last story told standing up. The last story is chosen on the night, from whatever the audience has said over the tea.

I carry five long tales and a dozen short ones. Nothing is memorised word for word. The spine is fixed; the skin changes. Two people who come on Friday and Saturday will hear the same tale end differently, and both endings were already inside it.

“You are not here to watch me tell it. You are here so that it can be told at all.”

Opening line, monsoon baithak, 2024
