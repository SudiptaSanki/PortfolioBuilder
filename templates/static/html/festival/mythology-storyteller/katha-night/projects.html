Repertoire · five long tales, fourteen short

Tales I can carry into a courtyard.

Each of these has been told at least six times, aloud, without a book. The length is honest rather than exact: a good night runs over, and a fifty-five minute tale can take forty if the room is cold.

The long tales are the spine of a night. The short ones are chosen after the interval, often because of something an audience member said over tea. If you are booking for a particular occasion, tell me the age of the youngest listener and the oldest, and I will tell you which of these will work.

I

Savitri and the year of waiting

Mahabharata, Vana Parva · Awadhi and Hindustani · 55 minutes

Long taleAdults and age 12+
No interval needed
Courtyard or hall

Savitri is married to Satyavan, who is destined to die in a year. When Death comes for him, she does not fight. She follows. And when she reaches the place where he is being taken, she asks for a boon that is small enough to be granted and large enough to undo everything.

The tale is usually told as a story about a clever wife. I tell it as a story about a person who refuses to accept the terms of a conversation. Death is polite in this telling, and that is what makes him frightening. Savitri wins because she listens more carefully than he does.

“She did not ask for his life. She asked for something he had already given her, and made him watch her use it.”

II

Nala and Damayanti

Mahabharata, Vana Parva · Hindustani · 42 minutes

Long taleAdults
One interval
Courtyard, hall, wedding

A king loses his kingdom at dice and his wife to a plan that was not entirely his own. What follows is a long separation in which both people become someone else: Nala works as a charioteer, Damayanti as a woman looking for a man who may not want to be found.

The famous part is the swan and the choice of husbands. The part I care about is the cooking. Damayanti recognises Nala by the way a dish is cut, long before she sees his face, which is either the most romantic thing in the epic or the most practical. It depends on the night and on the audience.

“Love is not proved by the grand gesture. It is proved by the small habit nobody else would notice.”

III

Hidimba's question

Mahabharata, Adi Parva · English · 38 minutes

Long taleAdults
Discussion welcome
Courtyard, school, festival

Hidimba is a rakshasi sent to kill the Pandavas, who meets Bhima and falls in love instead. Her brother is killed. She marries the man she was sent to destroy, bears him a son, and is left behind when the family moves on.

Most versions treat her as a footnote, the monster wife who conveniently produces Ghatotkacha. I tell the tale from inside her choice, which means the audience has to sit with a woman who betrays her family for a man who will not stay. There is no comfortable ending, and I do not import one.

“What is a promise worth when it is made to someone you were taught to call a monster?”

IV

Ganga comes down

Ramayana, Balakanda · Hindustani · 50 minutes

Long taleAll ages 10+
Water imagery
Open courtyard best

A river is persuaded to leave heaven and fall to earth. The falling is not the difficult part. The difficult part is that somebody has to catch her, and the somebody who tries is not strong enough in the way he thinks he is.

I tell this one outdoors whenever possible, because the sound of the water in the story is easier to hear when there is a real night around it. The audience is usually quiet by the half-hour mark. Children in the front row are often the first to understand that the river is not being punished.

“Catching a goddess is not the same as holding her. Ask the king who tried.”

V

The parrot and the hundred lies

Kathasaritsagara and oral Awadhi · 25 minutes

Short taleAge 7+
Fast, comic
Schools and family nights

A parrot is left in charge of a house and told to tell the truth. By the end of the night he has told a hundred lies, all of them technically true, and the household is in the well. The tale ends with an argument among the listeners about whether the parrot was a villain or a very good employee.

This is the one I take to schools. It works in Hindustani or English, in twenty-five minutes or twelve, and it survives interruption. At the end I ask the children which lie was the worst. They never agree, which is the point.

“A lie that is entirely true is the most expensive kind to tell.”

Booking

A night needs a wall, a lamp and an hour of quiet.

Long tale plus a short one, with an interval: 90 minutes of telling. I arrive ninety minutes early to set the lamps and walk the space. For schools, two 40-minute sets with a break work better than one long one.
