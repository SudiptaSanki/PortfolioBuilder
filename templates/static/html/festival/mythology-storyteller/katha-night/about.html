Paro Mishra · teller, Lucknow

I learned to tell stories by getting the pauses wrong.

Eight years of theatre taught me how to fill a room. It took a dastango in a drawing room to teach me how to empty one. This is the training, the sources, and the shape of a night.

From the wings to the courtyard

I grew up in Lucknow in a house with a wide verandah and a grandmother who told the Mahabharata as if it were neighbourhood gossip. I studied English literature because I liked the arguments, then spent six years in a theatre company doing lights, then props, then finally speaking. Theatre gave me timing and the courage to stand still. It also gave me a bad habit: performing the emotion instead of trusting the words.

In 2017 I went to a dastangoi workshop run by an old dastango in a drawing room in Aminabad. He listened to my first attempt and said, kindly, that I was telling the story to the audience rather than letting the audience overhear it. That distinction has organised my work since. A courtyard is not a theatre. The teller is not on a stage. The tale is happening somewhere just past the lamp, and we are all leaning toward it.

“The story does not need you to be interesting. It needs you to be present.”

My teacher, after a particularly enthusiastic rehearsal, 2017

Where the tales come from

The long tales are drawn from the Mahabharata, the Kathasaritsagara and the Rajasthani collections of Vijaydan Detha, in Hindi, Awadhi and English translations. I do not invent a canonical version. Each telling names its source at the start, in one sentence, because an audience deserves to know whether they are hearing a Sanskrit epic or a nineteenth-century retelling.

Three rules shape the repertoire. First, the tale must survive being spoken aloud for fifty minutes without a book. Second, it must have at least one question in it that I cannot answer. Third, it must not require the audience to already know the pantheon. If a listener needs homework, the telling has failed.

I work with two translators for the Awadhi material and a Sanskrit teacher for the epic passages. Their names are on the repertoire page and in the printed programme. When I change a detail for the stage, I say so in the interval, which usually produces the best conversation of the night.

A night, minute by minute

The shape is fixed so the audience can relax into it. They know when to arrive, when the tea is, and roughly how long they will be sitting. Within that frame, everything is live. The last story is chosen after the interval, based on what people said over the cups.

Money, plainly

Courtyard nights run on a split: a small guarantee for the teller, the rest to the host's costs, with any surplus going to the venue's chai and the lamp oil. Private events and festivals pay a flat fee, which subsidises the public nights. Schools in Lucknow pay half. Nobody is turned away from a public baithak for lack of a ticket; there is a cushion at the back that is always free.

Training

Six years with a Lucknow theatre company, mostly backstage. A dastangoi workshop in 2017, then four years of monthly practice in a friend's courtyard with an audience of seven and a very honest cook.

I still rehearse aloud, standing, without notes. The day a tale stops surprising me is the day I stop telling it.

What a host provides

A courtyard or a room with a wall behind the teller. One table. A power point is optional; I bring two battery lamps and oil for the third.

Tea for the interval, chairs for anyone who asks, and a person at the gate who knows the guest list. That is the whole rider.

Sources named

Mahabharata (critical edition, Pune), Kathasaritsagara (Chandrakanta Tarkaratna's Bengali recension), Vijaydan Detha's Batan ri Phulwari, and the Awadhi retellings recorded with Ramlal Yadav in Barabanki, 2019–2022.
