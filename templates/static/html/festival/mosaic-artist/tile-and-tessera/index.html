A mosaic is not a picture made of squares. It is a drawing made of flow.

Tesserae are cut to follow the form they describe. Around a cheek, the rows curve; along a wave, they run in long, unbroken lines. The Romans called that flow andamento, and it is the difference between a mosaic and a photograph pushed through a grid filter.

02

Four things that decide the panel

A mosaic fails in the drawing, not on the wall. These are the decisions taken before a single tessera is cut.

Andamento

The flow of the rows

Rows follow the form. In a face, they run around the cheek and down the neck; in water, they run in long horizontal bands that break at a ripple. A grid of equal squares reads as a pixelated photo and kills the material.

Opus

Which laying pattern

Tessellatum for regular fields, vermiculatum for small curved forms like eyes and fruit, musivum for the very fine work. Most panels use two of the three.

Joint

The grout is the drawing

A 2 mm joint at 10 mm tesserae gives 20 per cent grout, which is a colour in its own right. Lime grout goes grey; epoxy stays white and is the wrong choice for anything old.

Substrate

What it is stuck to

Cement board indoors, Wedi or fibre-glass mesh outdoors, and a movement joint every 3 metres on a façade. A mosaic is strong; the thing behind it is usually what fails.

03

Cut by hand, because machines cannot see

Every tessera in the studio is cut with nippers from a strip of smalti or a slab of marble. A machine can cut a thousand identical squares; it cannot decide that the third tile in a row should be a millimetre narrower so the row turns without a kink. That decision is made hundreds of times per square metre, by eye and by thumb.

The studio keeps a wall of sample panels, each one a failure or a fix. The most useful is a small blue wave that was laid four times before the rows stopped fighting the drawing. It hangs where clients can see it, because it explains the cost better than any estimate.
