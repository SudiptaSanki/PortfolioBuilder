Practice notes

Eleven years of cutting, and a wall of things that did not work.

I trained at the Scuola Mosaicisti del Friuli in Spilimbergo, where the first six months are spent on a single flat colour field. The point is not the field. It is learning that your hand cuts a different tile in the morning than it does at five in the afternoon, and that the panel will show it.

01

From drawing to wall

Six stages. The first three happen at a table, and they decide everything that follows.

  1. 01 · Cartoon

    The full-size drawing

    Drawn at 1:1 in charcoal on kraft paper. Every colour boundary is marked, then the andamento is drawn over it as a series of flowing lines, not a grid.

  2. 02 · Cartella

    The colour keys

    Each area is assigned a palette of three to five tones. Smalti is chosen wet, because glass changes colour when it dries and again when it is grouted.

  3. 03 · Sample

    A 20 cm square

    One small section laid first and viewed from three metres. Nine panels out of ten change at this stage — usually the background, which is almost always too dark.

  4. 04 · Cutting

    Nippers, not a wheel

    Marble is scored and tapped; smalti is nibbled with a hardie and hammer. Each tile is cut to the row it belongs in, which means the rows have to be laid in order.

  5. 05 · Setting

    Direct or reverse

    Direct on a wall, face up, working in sections the setter can finish in a day. Reverse on paper when the panel is made flat in the studio and lifted onto mesh.

  6. 06 · Grout

    Lime, pressed and wiped

    Grout is pushed across the face with a rubber float, left to set for twenty minutes, then wiped with a damp sponge. Two passes, never three.

02

Materials, and what each one is for

The material decides the drawing. You cannot lay an eye in terracotta the way you lay it in smalti.

Smalti

Glass · 10–20 mm · indoor, outdoor

Opaque glass made in Murano and in Rajasthan. Cuts with a clean edge, keeps colour in sunlight, and is the only sensible choice for gold. The surface is slightly irregular, which is what makes it catch light.

Marble

Stone · 8–25 mm · floors, pools

Softer, cheaper, and it stains. Good for large fields and for anything walked on. White marble goes yellow with age and no amount of cleaning brings it back.

Terracotta

Clay · 15–40 mm · floors, garden

Warm, porous, and usually laid in simple geometric fields. In Goa it is the local material and the one that looks right in a courtyard, but it must be sealed before a monsoon.

Vitreous glass

Glass · 20 mm · pools, kitchens

Cheap, uniform and frost-proof. The colour range is flat and the edges are soft; use it where the panel is seen from far away or under water.

03

The wall of failures

Four panels hang by the studio door. The first is a blue wave laid in a strict grid; the rows run straight through the crest and the wave looks like a staircase. The second is the same wave laid four times until the rows turn with the drawing.

The third is a face in the wrong palette — too many mid-tones, no dark. From across the room it becomes a grey smudge. The fourth is the fix: one dark tone removed, one added, and the whole face reads at six metres.

Clients are shown the wall before they see a quote. It saves a conversation later, and it explains why a square metre of hand-cut smalti costs what it does.

“The drawing is a proposal. The andamento is the argument.”
