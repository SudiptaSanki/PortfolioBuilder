The player · Aaliya Mir

I learned to play around a crack before I learned to play a raga.

My grandfather's santoor had a split in the soundboard that he refused to repair. Every note I learned had to pass over it. That is still how I hear the instrument.

The house in Rajbagh

I was born in Srinagar in 1989, in a house in Rajbagh with a walnut staircase, a small orchard and a room where my grandfather kept his santoor under a pashmina shawl. He had played in the Sufiana ensembles at the shrine for thirty years, not as a soloist but as one of the strings in a larger argument.

When I was seven I lifted the shawl and struck a string with my finger. The sound filled the room and then kept filling it, longer than a sound had any right to. I have been chasing that length ever since. Not the loudness, the length.

A santoor is not a quiet instrument. It is a room with a hundred doors, and every door is a string you have to keep in tune. Aaliya Mir

The cracked soundboard

The split runs about nine inches along the bass side, between the third and fourth bridges. My grandfather said a repaired board sounds like a repaired board. He was probably wrong, and it did not matter: I learned to place the kalam so the crack did not buzz, and that taught me more about touch than any exercise.

When the instrument came to me in 2004, I had it looked at by a maker in Habba Kadal. He offered to fit a new soundboard. I said no. The crack is now part of the instrument's voice, and I mention it in every programme note so nobody thinks the buzz is a mistake.

Learning

My first proper teacher was Ustad Nazir Ahmad Dar, who played the santoor in the Sufiana muqam tradition and had no patience for students who wanted to run before they could tune. For two years I tuned the instrument and played only Sa and Pa. I was eleven and thought it was a waste of time. It was the only part that was not.

At eighteen I began to study the ragas seriously, with a vocal teacher, because the santoor had taught me the strings but not the grammar. Singing the raga first, then finding it on the instrument, is still how I learn a new piece. The voice cannot lie about a komal note the way a mallet can.

I studied music at the University of Kashmir, taught my first student in 2014, and made my first album in 2018. The years in between included the years the valley emptied, and the house did not. Music was not an escape from that; it was the room we kept warm.

What the strings carry

The santoor in Kashmir is not only a classical instrument. It carries the muqam, the Sufiana kalam, the Rouf songs women sing in spring, and the film songs my mother hummed while cooking. I refuse to choose between them. A programme that begins with a muqam and ends with a raga is not a compromise; it is an accurate description of the house I grew up in.

I teach the same way: tuning first, then the voice, then the strings, then the raga. And always with the crack in the soundboard, because a student who can play around a flaw can play around a cold hall, a bad monitor or a broken string.

House rules

Four things the instrument taught me.

01Tune first

A santoor tuned by someone else is only half yours. Learn to move the bridges and hear the beat disappear.

02Sing it first

If you cannot sing the phrase, you cannot play it. The mallet will find the note; the ear has to know why.

03Leave the crack

A flaw you have learned to play around is not a flaw any more. This applies to halls, instruments and people.

04Let it ring

The silence after a note is part of the note. Most beginners strike again too soon. I still do, on bad days.
