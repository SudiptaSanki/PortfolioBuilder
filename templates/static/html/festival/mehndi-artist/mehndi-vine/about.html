About the artist

Eleven Teej seasons at the same table

I learned mehndi the way it is usually learned in Jaipur: at my grandmother's side during Teej, drawing wobbly paisleys on cousins who were paid in roasted corn to sit still. I have drawn professionally for eleven seasons now — brides, sangeets, Eid queues, Karva Chauth evenings — from a small studio table off Johari Bazaar and at kitchen tables all over the city.

The paste

From leaf to cone, by hand

Henna is not bought in a tube in my studio. The paste is the work before the work, and it decides the colour before I draw a single line.

  1. Sift. Rajasthani henna powder, sifted three times through muslin. One lump in the cone and a fine line breaks mid-paisley.
  2. Mix. Powder, strong black tea, lemon juice and a little sugar, rested 12 hours until the dye releases — the surface of the bowl turns the colour of old copper.
  3. Perfume. A few drops of eucalyptus oil, which helps the stain take. Nothing else goes in. If an ingredient needs a warning label, it does not touch skin.
  4. Cone. The paste is spooned into cellophane cones I roll myself, each tied off and cut to the day's work — a fine cut for bridal lines, a wider one for sangeet speed.
  5. Draw. Freehand, always. A design is built the way the home page shows: bands, big shapes, vines, then the palm picture last.

The 48-hour rule

A stain has a timetable

Lawsone, the dye in the leaf, binds to the skin slowly and darkens by oxidation. There is no speeding it up — there is only booking backwards from it. This is why every date on this site is counted in twos.

Hour 0–8 · Pale orange

The paste comes off and the design looks disappointing. This is normal. Every bride is warned; every bride worries anyway.

Hour 24 · Honest rust

A full day in, the colour is a true rust-red. Presentable for anything — this is the Eid-morning stage of a chaand raat sitting.

Hour 48 · Deep maroon

The peak. Two days after the sitting the stain is as dark as it will ever be — which is why the wedding sitting is never the day before.

Aftercare, the short version: keep the paste on at least 6 hours (overnight for bridal); seal it with lemon-and-sugar dabbed on as it dries; when it flakes off, no water on the design for the first day if you can help it; oil it — mustard or coconut — before baths for the first week. Treated kindly, a bridal stain holds its colour 7–10 days and fades over two to three weeks.

Why I will not use “black henna”

There is no such thing as black henna. Henna leaf stains orange, rust and maroon — never black, and never in twenty minutes. The cones sold as “black henna” or “instant henna” are usually hair dye: they carry para-phenylenediamine (PPD), a chemical that can burn skin, blister it, and leave permanent scars and a lifelong allergy to hair dye itself. Market cones with no ingredient list are the worst of them. If a design is black the moment it is drawn, it did not come from a leaf. I refuse it at weddings, at festivals, and when a bride's auntie insists it photographs better — and I will happily explain all of this to the auntie, with tea.
