The guru · Anjali Kulkarni

I learned to sing by being the last one to leave.

Sixteen years with one teacher, in a room with a stone floor and a tanpura that was always slightly out. The tuning was the lesson.

A childhood built around one hour

I was born in Pune in 1974, in a house where my mother sang bhajans while cooking and my father listened to Kumar Gandharva on a cassette player that ate tape. At seven I was taken to Pandit Shankar Rao Kale, a Gwalior gharana vocalist who taught in a room off Laxmi Road and accepted four students a year.

For the first three years he gave me no notation. I sat on the floor, sang Sa until he said stop, and listened to him sing the same phrase forty ways. I thought I was learning nothing. I was learning the only thing that cannot be written down: how a note behaves when a person means it.

I stayed sixteen years. I sang my first solo at nineteen, joined All India Radio as a graded artist in 1996, and began teaching in 1998 — partly for money, mostly because explaining a raga to a beginner is the fastest way to find out whether you understand it yourself.

A raga is not a scale with a mood attached. It is a room you have to live in until the furniture stops being strange. Anjali Kulkarni

What I owe my teacher

Panditji was not gentle. He would stop me mid-phrase with a raised hand and say nothing for a full minute. Then he would sing the phrase again, exactly as I had, wrongness and all, and wait. He never told me what was wrong. He made me hear it. It took longer, and it stayed.

He also insisted that a singer must be able to tune a tanpura by ear before learning a bandish, and that a student who cannot keep laya should not be given a fast taan to hide behind. I have kept both rules, and I have added one of my own: no student sings for an audience until they can sing for an empty room without rushing.

The gurukul, since 2008

The wada in Sadashiv Peth belonged to my grandmother. For years the front room held a dining table and a sewing machine. In 2008 I cleared it, bought two more tanpuras, and hung a cloth on the wall so the sound would not bounce off the plaster. The gurukul has been there since: one room, four tanpuras, a courtyard, and a kettle that is always half full.

There are 141 students now, past and present, of whom twelve perform regularly. The rest sing for themselves, their families, and the Saturday baithak. I am as proud of the second group. A raga does not need an audience to do its work.

What the first month looks like

  • Week 1

    Sa, and only Sa

    We find your natural pitch, tune the tanpura to it, and sing Sa for ten minutes a day. You will think it is too simple. It is the foundation of everything else.

  • Week 2

    The first palte

    Sa Re Ga Ma, Ma Ga Re Sa, in a steady pulse. By Friday you will hear where your voice jumps instead of walks.

  • Week 3

    Your first bandish

    A short composition in Yaman or Bhairav, with a text you understand. We work on the sthayi and where to breathe.

  • Week 4

    Sing for the room

    You sing the bandish for the other students at the Saturday baithak. Nobody claps until you finish; then everybody does.

House rules

Four things I will not teach around.

01

Riyaz before theory the voice learns by doing

We do not begin with raga theory or the history of the gharana. You sing first. The names and the grammar arrive later, when you already have something to attach them to.

02

One raga at a time depth over repertoire

A student who has half-learned twenty ragas has learned none. We stay with one until it can be sung at an hour's notice, in any season, without the notebook.

03

The tanpura is not background tune it yourself

Every student learns to tune. If you cannot hear the fifth string beat, you cannot hear your own Ga. The tanpura is the first teacher in the room and the last one to leave.

04

No audience before an empty room the baithak comes last

You may sing for the class when you can sing alone without hurrying. The empty room is the honest critic. It has heard everything and forgives nothing.

Sit in on a Saturday.

The monthly baithak is open to visitors. Come at eight, leave your shoes at the step, and listen to students who were exactly where you are a few months ago.
