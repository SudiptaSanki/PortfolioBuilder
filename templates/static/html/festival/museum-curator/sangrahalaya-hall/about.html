The curator · Dr Ira Sengupta

I came to curating through the back of a textile store.

Eight years of conservation taught me what an object looks like when nobody is watching. Curating is the work of letting visitors see that, without putting the object at risk.

A store room, not a gallery

I was born in Kolkata in 1968 and came to Lucknow in 2009 with a trunk of books, two pairs of scissors and a strong opinion about humidity. Before that I spent eight years in the textile store of the Calico Museum in Ahmedabad, where the lights are off, the rollers are padded, and the objects are folded in boxes that nobody visits.

That is where I learned to curate, although I did not call it that. An object in a store is not silent. It has a condition report, a maker, a repair, a smell of cedar and dust. The gallery is a negotiation between that object and a person who has walked in out of the heat with no idea what they are about to see.

A good label does not tell you what to feel. It gives you one fact you did not have, and then gets out of the way. Dr Ira Sengupta

What I brought with me

From conservation: a suspicion of dramatic lighting, a habit of looking at the back of things, and the belief that a repair is part of an object's history rather than a failure to be hidden. From history: the understanding that a museum is never neutral, so it may as well be honest about its argument.

When I arrived, the Hall had 4,100 objects, 600 of them on display in cases that had not been opened since 1988, and labels typed on a machine with a broken e. The first thing we did was not a new exhibition. It was a full audit, which took two years and found 38 objects that had been quietly deteriorating and 14 that were not where the register said they were.

How an exhibition is made here

  • 01

    The question

    Every exhibition starts with a question, not a theme. For the current show: how did chikankari move from a court practice to a household income, and who did the moving?

  • 02

    The checklist

    We list objects we already hold, then gaps. Loans come last, and only when a gap cannot be filled honestly from the collection.

  • 03

    Condition and courier

    Every object is photographed, condition-checked and measured. Textiles rest for 48 hours after travel before they are handled again.

  • 04

    The hang

    We draw the elevation before we touch the wall: centre line, gaps, sight lines, and where a wheelchair can stop without blocking the room.

  • 05

    The labels

    Under 150 words, one fact per sentence, no adjectives doing the work of evidence. Hindi first, English second, both at the same size.

  • 06

    The opening

    We open on a weekday morning with the guards, the cleaners and the lenders' families before the public. If it does not work for them, it does not work.

Then and now

  • 1968

    Born in Kolkata

    Into a family of schoolteachers and one uncle who repaired clocks and never threw anything away.

  • 1991

    History at Presidency College

    Where I learned to read a document against its own grain, and to distrust a beautiful sentence without a footnote.

  • 1994

    Conservation, National Museum Institute

    Textiles and paper. My first condition report was on a 19th-century Parsi embroidered border with a broken metal thread.

  • 1996

    The Calico Museum, Ahmedabad

    Eight years in the store. I measured, folded, photographed and learned the difference between a stain and a story.

  • 2009

    Curator, Sangrahalaya Hall

    Arrived to an audit already overdue, a leaking skylight and a display of chikan with the wrong date on every label.

  • 2016

    The permanent collection rehung

    Six rooms, 1,240 objects, and the first honest label about how most of the collection arrived.

  • 2024

    The oral history project

    Ninety-six recordings with embroiderers, brassworkers and musicians, transcribed in Hindi and Awadhi, with English summaries.

House rules

Four things I will not do.

  • 01

    Show an object without provenance

    If we cannot say where it came from, it does not go on the wall. It can sit in the study collection with a note explaining why we are unsure, which is more honest than a beautiful case.

  • 02

    Write a label over 150 words

    A visitor standing up will give you forty seconds. If the object needs more than that, the exhibition is not doing its job; the catalogue is.

  • 03

    Borrow for the sake of a poster

    A blockbuster loan that empties the permanent collection and teaches nothing is an expensive way to rent a famous object. We borrow to fill a gap in an argument, not to sell a ticket.

  • 04

    Hide a repair

    The soldered spout, the darned pallu, the replaced hinge: these are evidence of use. We stabilise, we do not erase, and the label says so.

Bring me an object with a story you cannot prove.
