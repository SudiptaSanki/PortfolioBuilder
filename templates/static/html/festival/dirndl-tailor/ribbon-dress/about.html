Zur PersonLena Brandl, 41

I have measured about four thousand women, and no two the same

I am a dirndl tailor, trained in Rosenheim, with journeyman years in Salzburg and Vienna, and my own atelier on Marktstraße since 2012. I make about sixty dirndls a year, by hand, on one table.

Rosenheim, Salzburg, Vienna, and then home

I grew up in Rosenheim, the daughter of a joiner and a primary school teacher, and I was bad at school in the way that makes a person practical. At fifteen I could take apart a jacket and put it back together; at sixteen I started an apprenticeship with a dirndl tailor who had learned the trade from her mother and did not believe in shortcuts. Three years of hand-finished seams, and I could draft a bodice without a pattern book.

Then the journeyman years. Two years in Salzburg with a costume house that made dirndls for the festival and for the theatre, where I learned that a garment has to work on a body that moves, sweats and sits down. Then a year in Vienna with a tailor who cut for opera, which is where I learned to fit a bodice in twenty minutes because the rehearsal was in an hour.

In 2012 I came back to Bavaria and took the shop on Marktstraße, because Bad Tölz still has a street where people walk to buy things, and because the light in the front room is good until four in the afternoon.

A dirndl is not a costume. It is a dress that has been worn to work, to church and to a wedding for two hundred years, and it was always cut for the body that had to do those things.

How I work

One garment at a time. The table holds one bodice, one skirt and one apron; if a second order comes in, it waits on the shelf in a cloth bag with a label. I have tried working on three at once and the seams started to look like it.

Everything is cut on the flat with chalk and a set square, and the pattern is drafted for the person, not graded from a block. The bodice is lined in cotton, boned at the side seams only, and laced at the front through hand-worked eyelets — no hooks up the back, because you cannot do them up alone.

Seams are 1.5 cm, side seams 3 cm, hems 4 cm. This is not generosity; it is maintenance. Bodies change, and a dirndl that cannot be let out is a dirndl that will be worn twice and then hang in a wardrobe for thirty years.

What I will not do

  • Shorten a skirt to a length the wearer cannot sit down in, whatever the photograph shows.
  • Cut a bodice without a fitting in calico. It is the only way to know.
  • Copy a designer's dirndl exactly. I will make you something in that spirit, with your measurements.
  • Sew a costume for a fancy-dress party that mocks the tradition. I will make a costume, but from a costume house's pattern, and I will say so.
  • Rush a wedding dirndl ordered in the last week of June. It is not possible, and a bad seam is forever.

Why the cloth matters as much as the cut

A dirndl is worn for a whole day outdoors, in whatever the weather does, and then danced in. Cotton and linen take that; silk does not, but a wedding is not a whole day outdoors. I will tell you which cloth suits which occasion, and I will refuse to sell you silk for a festival in August.

The apron is the part people underestimate. It takes the wear and the spills, it is the brightest thing in the outfit, and it should be replaceable. I cut aprons so they can be taken off and washed, and I keep the pattern so a second one can be made in another colour.

On inherited dirndlsIf a bodice has been in the family, bring it. I can rebuild it onto new cloth, keep the original hooks and eyelets, and write down what was changed so the next generation knows.
