Practice Since 2014

I stay long enough to stop being interesting.

The first week in a village, everyone performs for the camera. The second week, they forget it. The third week, they start telling you the truth — which is usually about something else entirely. Most of my films are made in that third week.

The crew is three people

Me, a sound recordist, and a local fixer who is paid from the first day and is never called a fixer in the credits. Anything bigger and the room changes: people start to watch the crew instead of living in front of one camera.

On Kal, Today the crew was four for the flood sequences, because you cannot carry a 16 mm body, a tripod and a sound kit up a broken embankment alone.

Film, then digital

I shoot 16 mm for anything that will be watched in a dark room, and digital for night and for the long observational stretches where the ratio matters more than the grain. The two are graded to sit beside each other; audiences notice the cut, not the format.

Sound is never second. If the recorder fails, the day is lost — the image can be soft, the sound cannot.

A short history

  1. 2014

    First trip to the ghats

    Ten days in Varanasi with a borrowed camera and no film. I came back with forty minutes of the same boatman and the beginning of an obsession.

  2. 2017

    Ghat — 72 min

    Four mornings on the same steps, no commentary. Shot on 16 mm, sound from a boat. First festival screening at DMZ Docs.

  3. 2019

    The flood year

    Filmed through the monsoon in three districts. Nothing was released from that footage; two families asked me not to, and I have kept my word.

  4. 2022

    The Ferryman's Arithmetic — 41 min

    A ferry operator counts his day out loud. The film is built on that counting and on the silence between fares.

  5. 2026

    Kal, Today — in the edit

    Three generations of a family who cremate on the same ghat, filmed across four years and two floods. 84 minutes, looking for a first cut that does not explain itself.

Money

Films are funded by a mix of festival grants, a small broadcast pre-sale, and occasionally my own savings. Nobody pays for a credit. The families who appear are paid for their time at the local day rate, in cash, before the first slate.

After the film

Every subject gets a copy of the finished film, in a format they can actually play, and a screening in their own town if they want one. That is not a courtesy; it is the least the film owes the place it was made in.
