Ledger · shop 214

Three generations, one narrow shop.

The shop is four metres wide and nine feet to the beam. There is a counter, a scale, two grinding stones, and sacks stacked to the ceiling in an order only my father and I can follow. This page is how we buy.

A stall, then a shop, then a habit

Ram Nath Saxena came to Delhi in 1947 with a trunk and a cousin's address. By 1954 he had a licence to trade and a sack of coriander on the pavement of Bazaar Road. In 1974 he took shop 214, and my grandfather's habit — write the village on the sack, not the price — became the way this shop works.

My father, Vinod, added the first stone chakki in 1988. He had noticed that customers were buying whole spices and then taking them to a mill three lanes away, where the miller ground whatever had been in the hopper before. Twenty kilos of cumin came out tasting faintly of the previous customer's chilli. So he bought a stone and started grinding on Thursdays, one spice at a time, sweeping between each.

I came back in 2009. I had spent six years in a food-testing laboratory in Mohali, measuring moisture, colour value and volatile oil for exporters, and I was tired of numbers that never reached the person cooking. The shop had the opposite problem: excellent spice, no record. So I started a ledger.

What the ledger holds

Every sack that enters the shop gets a line: the grower or the mandi, the village, the month of harvest, the weight in, the moisture reading, the price paid, and a note on the nose and the hand. When a customer asks why the cardamom is ₹460 this month and was ₹410 in March, the answer is on the page — the Idukki auction ran short after unseasonal rain.

If I cannot tell you which village a chilli came from, I should not be selling it to you by the kilo.

The ledger is a hardback register, one volume a year, kept in the drawer under the scale. There are 37 of them now. Customers are welcome to read the current one; a few regulars do, and one restaurant buyer photographs the pages to check my prices against the mandi reports.

How buying works

  • Direct where possible. Cardamom, pepper, turmeric and saffron come from growers or their cooperatives, usually six to nine households.
  • Mandi where necessary. Chillies, cumin and coriander are bought at auction in Guntur, Unjha and Kota, where the volumes are beyond any single farm.
  • Taste before price. A lot is bought on the nose and the hand. The lab can confirm a moisture reading; it cannot tell you whether the batch was dried over a wood fire.
  • Small lots, more often. Twenty to forty kilos at a time, refreshed weekly, rather than a season's stock going stale in the back room.

The shop, in section

Drawn from memory, not from a plan. The stone is at the back because my father believed a grinding stone should be heard from the counter and not seen from the street.

The register37 volumes, 1974 to 2026. The 1983 volume is water-stained and the 1991 one is missing; my uncle borrowed it and denies it.

On my deskA moisture meter, a brass sieve, a notebook, and a small jar of the week's best lot that customers are invited to smell.

A note on priceI add between 18% and 35% to what I paid, depending on how much of the sack I lose to stems, dust and shrivelled berries. The ledger shows both numbers.

WholesaleRestaurants and caterers buy from 5 kg. There is no separate rate card; the price is the shelf price less 8% for whole sacks.

The shop year

  • 1954Ram Nath Saxena registers as a trader and sells coriander from a sack on the pavement of Bazaar Road.
  • 1974Takes shop 214 on a nine-year lease. The first ledger is opened in April.
  • 1988Vinod Saxena installs the first stone chakki and starts Thursday grinding, one spice at a time.
  • 2009Sarala returns from the laboratory in Mohali and begins weighing, sampling and tagging every lot.
  • 2016The second stone arrives, bought secondhand from a mill in Sadar Bazaar; the old one becomes the finishing stone.
  • 2024Direct buying starts with four Lakadong turmeric households in the Jaintia Hills.

VisitingThe shop is best before eleven, when the lane is still passable and the scale is free. Bring a list and a cloth bag; we do not keep plastic.

SamplingAny whole spice on the shelf can be chewed, crushed or sniffed before purchase. That is not a favour, it is how the trade works.

What I will not stockPre-ground blends with salt or starch, bleached cardamom, and anything sold by colour alone.
