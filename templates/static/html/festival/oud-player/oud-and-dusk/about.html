The player

Taught by hand in Cairo, and by one winter in Istanbul.

I am Samir Barakat. I play the oud for concerts, for film and for the small rooms where a maqam can be heard without a microphone. I teach fourteen students, most of them adults who came to the instrument late.

The first oud in our house belonged to my father and lived in a canvas case behind the door, where the hallway was coolest. He played it after dinner, badly and happily, and he taught me the names of the notes before he taught me to read them: rast, dukah, sikah, jaharkah, nawa. I was seven. I thought these were the names of people in the building.

I began lessons properly at eleven with a neighbour who played in a wedding band in Shubra. He charged very little and expected a great deal: forty minutes of open strings before anything else, and no melody until the right hand could keep a steady tone while the left moved. That order has never left me. When I teach, the first month is almost entirely the right hand.

At the Cairo Conservatory I studied the Arabic repertoire formally, which gave me the theory I had been playing by ear and also taught me how much of the theory is a description rather than a rule. A taqsim is not an improvisation over a scale. It is a walk through a set of small melodic houses, the ajnas, and the art is in the doorways between them. My teacher there, Ustad Hamza al-Rashidi, used to stop me mid-phrase and ask which house I had just left. For the first year I rarely knew.

The year in Istanbul

In 2015 I spent eleven months in Istanbul, mostly in the rehearsal rooms around Beyoğlu, playing Turkish repertoire on a borrowed oud with a shorter scale. The tuning and the ornament are different and the discipline is the same. I came back with a faster right hand, a habit of practising with a metronome at half speed, and a set of pieces I still play: a Saz semai, two peşrevs, and a taksim in Hicaz that I have never quite managed to leave alone.

“A maqam is not a scale you finish. It is a room you have to learn to leave.”
Ustad Hamza al-Rashidi, in a lesson, 2014

The instrument

I play a 1974 oud built in Damascus, rosewood bowl with a spruce face and a floating bridge, which I have had repaired twice and will not take on a plane without its own seat. It has six courses and eleven strings; the lowest course is single. The scale is 58.5 cm, which is long even for an Arabic oud, and it gives the low C a weight that a shorter instrument cannot quite reach.

My second instrument is a Turkish-style oud made in Izmir in 2016, shorter and brighter, used for the Turkish repertoire and for recording when a cue needs more attack than warmth. Both are strung with nylon trebles and copper-wound basses, and I change the trebles every three weeks in teaching season.

How I teach

Lessons are fifty minutes, one to one, in a room in Zamalek with a piano I do not play and a window onto a courtyard. I teach by hand position and by ear first, notation second. A student who can hear the difference between sikah and a tempered minor third will find the notation later; a student who has only the notation will spend years wondering why the scale sounds wrong.

  • Right-hand drills: open strings, then single-course tremolo, then the two together.
  • One maqam at a time, learned from its ajnas outward, with a recorded drone to practise against.
  • Repertoire from the Arabic and Turkish books, chosen for the student's hand rather than for prestige.
  • Every fourth lesson is a playing lesson: no correction, only music, so that the student remembers why they came.

Where I play

Mostly in Cairo: courtyards, the small theatres around Downtown, and one stone room in Old Cairo whose reverberation I have been trying to record properly for three years. I also play in Alexandria two or three times a year and take one international date each season, usually a festival that can cover the instrument's travel.

Since 2021 I have recorded for film, which is its own discipline: the picture decides the length, the dialogue decides the register, and the director decides when the taqsim may breathe. I like it more than I expected to.
