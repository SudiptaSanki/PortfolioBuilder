How a commission runs

It begins with a drawing, not a price. Shabnam will draw the form and the border at actual size, and you will be asked to approve it on paper before a mould is filled. Changes after that are charged by the piece, because a mould is a plaster commitment.

Then a sample: one piece, fired in the next load, photographed and sent. If the cobalt is too heavy or the border sits wrong, it is corrected on the sample and the correction carries into the run. The sample is yours and is charged at the single-piece rate.

The run is fired in loads of forty to sixty pieces. You are told the firing dates in advance and sent a photograph of every load after it is opened, including the failures. Nothing is shipped until the count is agreed.

What we cannot promise

Exact colour across loads. Cobalt shifts with the position in the kiln and the weather, and a run of two hundred pieces will have a visible range. We sort by shade and tell you how many of each you are getting; we do not pretend they are identical.

The museum set

In 2026 the Albert Hall Museum asked for twelve reproductions from its blue pottery collection, to be handled by visitors in an education room rather than kept behind glass. The originals are between 1880 and 1930, and three of them are the only known examples of their form.

We were not allowed to take them out of the building. Shabnam spent four mornings measuring and drawing in the store room, and the moulds were made from those drawings rather than from casts, which is why the necks are a little heavier than the originals. The museum accepted that, and the note is printed on the label beside the case.

The restoration

The 1930s surahi came in a shoebox, in four pieces, with the handle missing. The break was clean and the body had not crazed, which is the only reason the job was worth doing.

The neck was rebuilt on the original curve, joined with slip, and painted to match the old cobalt by eye — not by chart, because the original cobalt had faded unevenly. The handle was left off. A new handle on an old body looks like what it is, and the owner agreed.
