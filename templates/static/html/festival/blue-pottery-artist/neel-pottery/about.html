One firing, at 820 degrees

Blue pottery is the only ceramic tradition in India that uses no clay at all. That single fact decides everything else: how it is shaped, why it is painted before firing rather than after, and why a plate rings when you tap it.

From Delhi to Jaipur, and nearly lost

The craft came to Jaipur in 1868, when Sawai Ram Singh II brought potters from Delhi to make tiles for the city's buildings. Their technique was Persian: a body of quartz and glass rather than clay, painted and glazed and fired once at a low temperature. It was cheaper than stone and brighter than anything the local potters could make.

By the 1950s the workshops had almost gone. Kripal Singh Shekhawat, a painter who had studied at Santiniketan, began reproducing the old forms in the 1960s with a handful of potters, and the revival that followed is the reason the craft exists today. Rahim Qureshi trained in that revival, in a workshop off Johari Bazaar, and opened this studio on Amer Road in 1979.

His daughter Shabnam took over the kiln in 2004. The studio still uses his plaster moulds for the surahi and the bowl, though the moulds are patched with shellac and one of them has been retired to the wall.

How a piece is made here

Nine steps between a sack of quartz and a plate on the shelf. The firing is the only one of them that cannot be corrected.

  1. 01

    Mixing the body

    Quartz powder, powdered glass, borax, sodium carbonate and fuller's earth, by weight, with water. Left to stand overnight so the borax dissolves.

  2. 02

    Pressing

    Plaster moulds, filled by hand and pressed with a wooden tool. There is no wheel in this studio except for the rims of plates.

  3. 03

    Joining

    Handles, spouts and lids are joined with slip made from the same body. A join that is too wet cracks in the kiln, not on the bench.

  4. 04

    Drying

    Two days in the shade, then a day in the sun. A piece that dries too fast warps at the rim and cannot be fixed.

  5. 05

    Sanding

    Seams are sanded with a stone and the surface wiped. Cobalt will show every scratch that a glaze would hide.

  6. 06

    Painting

    Cobalt oxide, copper oxide or iron, mixed with a little gum arabic, painted onto the raw body with a squirrel-hair brush.

  7. 07

    Glazing

    Feldspar and borax glaze, poured and swirled. The glaze is thin — a heavy coat runs and takes the design with it.

  8. 08

    Firing

    820°C for eight hours, loaded at six in the evening, opened the next morning. One firing; there is no second chance to correct a colour.

  9. 09

    Sorting

    Every piece is tapped and rung. Crazed, warped or run pieces go to the shard pile behind the studio, which the gardener takes away by the sack.

The shard pile

About one piece in five comes out of the kiln unfit to sell. That is not a failure of the studio; it is the material. Quartz bodies warp, cobalt runs, and a glaze that looks perfect in the morning can craze by the afternoon.

We keep the count in a notebook, because it is the honest measure of a kiln. In 2026: 1,240 pieces out, 1,018 sold, 222 to the shard pile. Some are kept — a bowl with a run in the glaze that looks like a river is on the shelf in the studio, not for sale.

Care, and what breaks

Blue pottery is not stoneware. It does not like the oven, the flame, the freezer or a sudden change of temperature; a cold plate under a hot curry can craze the glaze in one meal. Hand wash the painted pieces, and let them dry face down.

Dropped, it breaks into long, clean shards. We do not repair it — a glued shard is a shard with glue on it — but we will replace a piece broken in transit, photographed within three days of delivery.

What we refuse to make

No mugs with company logos, no ashtrays, no pieces copied from another studio's catalogue. We do not sell seconds online and we do not put a cobalt rim on a piece made somewhere else.

We do take restoration work on old blue pottery, but only when the piece is worth more than the repair. A surahi from the 1930s with a clean break at the neck is worth the five weeks; a modern plate with a chipped rim is not.
