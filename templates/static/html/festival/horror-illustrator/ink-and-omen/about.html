The drawer

I came to horror through printmaking, not through films.

A woodblock does not care how you feel about it. You cut away everything that is not the image, and what is left either holds ink or it does not. That is a useful way to grow up as a draughtsman.

Ipsita Roy
b. 1987, Kolkata
Studio: Baghbazar
Working since 2014
Represented by no one

Santiniketan, and the smell of the press

I studied printmaking at Kala Bhavana because I wanted to make multiples — the same image, over and over, until it was right. What I learned instead was subtraction. In a woodcut, everything you cut is white. You cannot add. You sit with the block until you can see the black, and then you take away everything that is not it.

After college I worked for three years in a letterpress and offset shop on Chittaranjan Avenue, setting type and cleaning rollers. The owner, a man named Bimal-da, kept a stack of Indrajal Comics and Sandesh in the back for the boys to read at lunch. That is where I first understood that a page can be built out of black shapes instead of sentences — that the gutter can do the work of a full stop.

My first graphic novel job came in 2014 from a small press that had seen a zine I had photocopied at a shop in Gariahat. The book was about a village that loses its well. I drew it at night, on the floor, and I have not really changed the method since.

A horror plate fails when it shows you the monster. It works when it shows you the room the monster has just left.

What is on the desk

I draw with a brush and a nib, not a stylus. A pigma pen gives a dead line; a nib gives you a line that thickens when your hand hesitates, which is exactly the information I want to keep. The ink is a stick ground on stone for the hero plates and Pelikan 4001 for everything else.

  • Paper

    Fabriano Artistico 300gsm hot-pressed for finished plates; any smooth cartridge for thumbnails. I buy it in sheets and tear it, never trim it with a knife.

  • Brushes

    One Raphael 8404 no. 2 sable, one cheap hog filbert no. 6 for dry brush, and a Japanese brush pen that I use only for hair.

  • Nibs

    Leonardt 300 for hatching, a Gillott 303 for the smallest stipple. They wear out in about six months.

  • Ink

    A Chinese stick ink, ground on a slate stone, for blacks that should sit on top of the paper; Pelikan 4001 for blacks that should sink in.

  • The rest

    White gouache for corrections (I do not correct), a scalpel for scraping, a soft brush for erasing crumbs, and a scanner old enough to have a warm-up.

Where the stories come from

Bengali household ghost stories are not really about ghosts. They are about the correct way to behave at a pond, in a lane, after dark. The rules are practical, the details are domestic, and the horror arrives through the ordinary thing being slightly wrong: the feet, the voice, the door that was always closed. That is the register I want in the drawings.

The written sources I keep on the shelf are Dakshinaranjan Mitra Majumdar's Thakurmar Jhuli (1907) and its companion Thakurdadar Jhuli; the Bengali ghost-story anthologies edited by the Mitra & Ghosh imprint in the 1960s; and a photocopy of a 1911 district gazetteer that describes the cremation ground at Nimtala in terms no living person would use today.

What I will not draw

I am not interested in the horror that needs a real person to be afraid of. So: no communal violence, no violence against children, no “tribal curse” nonsense, no psychiatric wards used as a set. If your story needs a real community to be the monster, it is not a ghost story, it is a prejudice with a soundtrack.

  • Gore for its own sake, or the camera held on a wound.
  • Religious figures used as villains, in any religion.
  • Real people, living or recently dead, in a degrading situation.
  • Anything that requires me to lie about the source material.
