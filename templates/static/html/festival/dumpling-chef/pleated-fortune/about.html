The cook · born 1979, Calcutta

I learned to fold before I learned to read.

At the bench

Six mornings a week since 1994. A dough scraper with a handle worn to the shape of my grandfather's grip, and a steamer lid that no longer matches its basket.

My grandfather, Chen Wei, left Hankou in the winter of 1938 with a canvas bag and a job waiting in a tannery off Canning Street. He was twenty-two. In the bag were three things: a dough scraper, a bamboo steamer lid, and a notebook of weights written in a hand none of us can now read without guessing. The tannery work lasted four years. The dumplings lasted longer.

He set up a stool at the mouth of Sun Yat Sen Street at four in the morning, when the vegetable carts come in from Sealdah and the tea is still only water. He sold pork dumplings to tannery men, to dock hands, to the Tamil clerks from the customs offices, and at six to the Chinese families who had come down from Hakka villages and Canton and could not find their own breakfast anywhere else in the city.

My father, Chen Tsung, took the stool over in 1989 and turned it into a stall with a tin roof. I was ten. My job was to stamp the skins with the rim of a glass and stack them under a wet cloth, and if the stack fell I was not given dinner. That is not a figure of speech. It was the rule.

I left for eight years to work in a hotel dim sum kitchen in Singapore, where I learned to fold forty-two pleats for a competition nobody remembers now, and to work a steamer battery that runs on gas and a timer and no one's wrist. When I came home in 2004 my father had had a stroke and could not feel his left hand. He could still fold with the right one, badly, for about a year. Then he stopped, and I took the bench.

The counter is twelve seats because twelve is what fits under the awning without blocking the lane. The awning is blue. The menu is written on a slate at six and rubbed off at two. Everything I know about dough I know because someone stood behind me and moved my hand.

Fold 02

Three generations, one lane

The dates are the family's, not the food writer's. Some of them are remembered rather than recorded, and one of them is a year nobody in the community talks about lightly.

  • 1938

    Hankou to Calcutta

    Chen Wei arrives with a dough scraper, a steamer lid and a notebook. He finds work in a tannery and rents a bed on the first floor of a building on Sun Yat Sen Street.

  • 1942

    The stool at the mouth of the lane

    A kerosene stove, a bamboo steamer, forty dumplings a day. The recipe is pork, chive, ginger, and a spoon of the tannery's rice wine, which nobody has been able to buy since.

  • 1962

    Deoli

    During the internment of the Chinese community, the family is sent to the camp in Rajasthan. My grandmother keeps the notebook sewn into the hem of a saree. The stall does not exist for six years.

  • 1989

    My father's tin roof

    Chen Tsung rebuilds the stall with a corrugated roof and a second steamer. He adds the potsticker, because the dock hands wanted something they could eat with one hand.

  • 1994

    My first plate

    Fifteen years old, forty plates, all of them leaking. My father made me eat every one before he would let me fold again.

  • 2004

    Home from Singapore

    Eight years in a hotel dim sum kitchen, a wrist that had learned speed. My father's left hand had stopped listening to him. I took the bench and he sat at the end of it for a year, saying nothing.

  • 2011

    Twelve seats

    A counter built from two doors and a length of teak, twelve stools, and the awning painted the blue of a Calcutta shutter.

  • Today

    Six days, one seating

    Morning service for the lane. Friday evening for anyone who books far enough ahead. Saturday mornings, two people at a time, learning to pleat.

I.

The skin is the dish

A good filling cannot rescue a thick skin. Roll the edge thin and leave the heart thick enough to hold soup, and the dumpling will tell you when it is done by going translucent at the shoulder.

II.

Salt late

Salt draws water, and water ruins a fold. Salt the filling ten minutes before it meets the skin, never earlier, and never salt the cabbage until you are ready to wring it out by hand.

III.

Feed the lane first

The counter exists for the people who walk past it at six in the morning. Bookings begin at eight. If you are early and there is a stool, sit down; if there is not, take it away in paper.

Come and watch the fold. Then eat it.
