The house The village Walks Stay

Two hundred and forty households, one road, and a great deal of shade.

Hosalli sits on a slope fourteen kilometres from Sirsi town, in the part of Uttara Kannada where the areca gardens begin and the evergreen forest starts to take over. This page is about the place, not the rooms.

What grows here

Areca nut, mostly — the tall palms you see in rows, grown for the beetle-leaf trade and for the plates and cups that get woven out of the sheath. Under them, cardamom, because it wants exactly the shade the areca gives. Pepper climbs the same trunks. There are bananas at the edges, a few mango trees that nobody prunes, and a kitchen garden that my mother-in-law replants according to a calendar she keeps in her head.

The areca is cut in the cool months and dried on the yard in front of the house for six weeks, turned by hand twice a day. It is the least glamorous work on the farm and the reason the farm exists.

Nobody here performs village life for visitors. The cutting happens whether or not anyone is watching, which is exactly why it is worth watching.

The house

The old part was built in 1948 by my husband’s grandfather: laterite blocks, a tile roof, a verandah with four pillars and a floor of polished red oxide that has been re-done twice. The cow-shed wing came later and was rebuilt in 2017 when we decided to keep rooms. We did it with a local mason and no architect, which is why the upper room has the best light and the awkwardest stair.

We are five in the family when everyone is home: my husband Shivaram, our son who runs the pump and the accounts, my mother-in-law, and me. Our daughter is in Bengaluru and comes back for the harvest and for the Yakshagana season, in that order.

What the village does at night

From November the Yakshagana school at the edge of the village rehearses on Tuesdays and Thursdays, and there are performances at the school ground through the season — a painted face, a drum that you can hear from the plots, and a story that runs until two in the morning if the audience stays. It is not a folkloric show for tourists; the audience is the village, and it is free.

Beyond the village there is more than a week of things to do: the Unchalli falls down a forest stair, the stone shrines at Sahasralinga in the Shalmala river, the Kadamba temples at Banavasi, and the market town of Sirsi with its lanes of areca traders. None of it needs booking, and none of it is crowded on a weekday.

When to come, and what it is like

The house is open October to March. Outside that the roof leaks somewhere new every week and the road to the falls is not worth the risk, so we close and repair.

  • October

    Still green from the rains, the falls full, the paths muddy. The best month for photographs and the worst for shoes.

  • November

    Cool mornings, areca cutting in full swing, Yakshagana rehearsals. Our own favourite.

  • December

    Cold at dawn, clear by nine, the tank full of birds. The busiest month, so book early.

  • January

    Harvest of cardamom and the drying yard full. Warm afternoons, good light, and the market at its best.

  • February

    Dry and golden. Fires at the edges of the plots in the evening, which smell wonderful and are strictly controlled.

  • March

    Hot by noon, still pleasant at six. The last month before we close; the mango is not ready yet.

Who you will meet

  1. Shivaram

    Cuts the areca, fixes the pump, and will tell you exactly what the crop is worth this year whether you asked or not.

  2. Gangavva

    Cooks, keeps the rooms, and decides at six in the morning whether the day’s plan is a good idea.

  3. Ramesh

    The son, who drives to Sirsi for supplies and will take you if there is room in the jeep.

  4. Akka

    My mother-in-law, who is 81, does not speak English, and will feed you until you negotiate a stop.

  5. The Shetty sisters

    Two houses down, weaving areca-leaf plates for the temple, and happy to show you how.
