Naomi Shalev, born in Jerusalem in 1982. Bezalel 2001–2005, then four years in a silversmith's workshop in Jaffa, then this room in the German Colony since 2010. Eight commissions a year, two of them for synagogues.

The first object I made was a mistake

In my third year at Bezalel I made a seder plate that was beautiful and unusable: the bowls were too shallow to hold the salt water, and the whole thing tipped when a hand rested on the rim. My teacher, Yossi Bar-El, put a cup of water in it, watched it spill, and said nothing. I have never designed an object since without filling it first.

That is the whole of my method. Before a drawing is finished, I make a paper or brass mock-up at full size, put it in the position it will occupy, and use it. A mezuzah gets touched with a wet hand; a kiddush cup gets filled to the brim and lifted; a seder plate gets six bowls of real charoset in it. If it survives that, it gets made in silver.

Four years in Jaffa

After Bezalel I worked for Shlomo Mizrahi, a silversmith then in his seventies who made mostly synagogue silver and had no interest in design. I learned from him the things school does not teach: how to raise a cup from a flat disc without thinning the rim, how to set a stone so it will not move in twenty years, and how to work at a bench for eight hours without ruining your shoulders.

He also taught me to keep a scrap box. Every offcut of silver goes into it, and once a year it is weighed and sold back. Nothing is wasted; the scrap pays for the gas.

Why by hand

A pierced filigree can be cut by a laser in four minutes and it looks it: the edges are parallel, the depth is uniform, and the piece has no shadow. Cutting it by hand takes four days and leaves a thousand small variations, which is exactly what makes the surface catch light unevenly and look alive.

I use a jeweller's saw with a 4/0 blade, about forty blades a week when I am piercing. The pattern is drawn on the metal with a fine marker and then, once cut, the marker is polished away and the drawing exists only in the piece.

“If you would not use it on a Friday night, it is not Judaica. It is a sculpture with a Hebrew word on it.”Shlomo Mizrahi, to his apprentice, 2008

What I will not make

I do not make objects that imitate another maker's work, and I do not copy a museum piece for sale, although I will copy one for study. I do not make anything that will not be used. And I do not write scrolls — the mezuzah and tefillin parchments in my pieces come from a sofer, and I name him on the certificate.

I also say no to about a third of the commissions that reach me, usually because the date is impossible or the budget and the design do not match. It is easier for everyone if that is said in the first email.

02 Chronology

A short list of rooms

  1. 2001

    Bezalel, metalwork department

    Four years of raising, scoring, soldering and failing. The seder plate that tipped was in the third year.

  2. 2005

    Jaffa, with Shlomo Mizrahi

    Four years of synagogue silver and repair work. He paid badly and taught well, which I have since learned is the usual arrangement.

  3. 2010

    The studio on Emek Refaim

    One room, second floor, north window, a bench bought from a dentist's estate. The rent was the reason; the light turned out to be the reason I stayed.

  4. 2014

    First synagogue commission

    A ner tamid for a small study house in Nahlaot, in brass, still lit. It taught me that a piece seen from four metres has to read in silhouette first.

  5. 2019

    Bezalel, ten years on

    An exhibition of graduates; four pieces, including the first mezuzah I ever sold. My teacher came and said the hinges were still too stiff.

  6. 2026

    Eight commissions, two synagogues

    The same number as five years ago. Silver has gone up, and I would rather raise the price than the volume.

Materials, honestly

Silver 925 — sterling, hallmarked. It tarnishes; that is not a fault, and a piece that is used weekly needs polishing twice a year at most.

Brass — for larger work and for synagogues with a budget. Lacquered or left to patinate; I prefer left, but I will lacquer if asked.

Copper — patinated, for wall pieces. It will keep changing for a year and then settle.
