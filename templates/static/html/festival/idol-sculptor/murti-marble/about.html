The family · Ramganj, Jaipur

Three generations,
one set of marks
on the wall.

My grandfather Deviprasad came to Jaipur in 1949 and opened a yard behind the Ramganj police station in 1952 with two carvers and a hand-cart. The workshop has moved twice and has never had more than eleven people in it. The marks on the wall — the talam divisions for each standard height — were drawn by him in charcoal and are still the ones we use.

01 Lineage

Who taught whom, and what each one changed

A family workshop is not a school. You learn by holding the other end of a straightedge, then by being trusted with the back of a figure, then by being left alone with a face.

  1. 1952

    Deviprasad opens the yard

    Two carvers, one hand-cart, and a contract for the Jain temple at Sanganer. He cut mostly architectural work at first: brackets, jali screens, the occasional small murti for a household.

  2. 1968

    The first Devi at sixty inches

    A commission from a family in Bikaner that took nine months and nearly ended the workshop. The block cracked along a vein in the fourth month; the replacement came from a different quarry and carved differently. My grandfather kept a piece of the cracked block on the shelf until he died.

  3. 1984

    Mohanlal takes over the bench

    My father introduced the set of eight standard heights by which we still quote — 12, 18, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72 and 84 inches, each with its own talam card. Before that, every figure was set out from scratch.

  4. 2003

    The move to Gali 6

    A larger yard with a north-facing courtyard, which matters more than it sounds: a murti under a north light shows its planes honestly. The old yard is now a hardware shop.

  5. 2011

    Bronze, and a partnership

    We began sending wax models to a casting family in Swamimalai. It is the only part of the work we do not do ourselves, and we say so on every bronze quote.

  6. 2019

    Arjun takes the yard

    The third generation, trained here since he was fourteen and at the Abhijna school in Jaipur for three years. He brought the drawing board into the office: every commission now begins as a measured sheet, which is how this website came to look the way it does.

The marks on the wall

On the north wall, in charcoal under a coat of thin lime wash, there are eight sets of horizontal lines. Each set is one standard height divided into its face-lengths. When a new carver starts, he spends a week copying them onto a plank before he touches a block. It is not a test of drawing. It is a test of whether he can hold a measurement in his eye.

The shastra is often described as a set of rules about beauty. It is more useful than that: it is a set of rules about standing up. A figure whose weight is not over its feet will lean, and a stone figure that leans will crack at the ankle in twenty years. The proportions are engineering as much as devotion.

What changed and what did not

What changed: the drawing. My grandfather set out directly on the block with a string; we now draw a sheet first, at a fifth scale, and the client sees it before the stone is cut. What changed: the dust. A wet grinder and a extraction fan have added years to everyone's lungs. What changed: the telephone, which means a client in Delhi can approve a face from a photograph, for better and worse.

What did not change: the face is polished by one man, the last cut on a finger is made with a riffler and not a machine, and no murti leaves the yard until it has stood in the courtyard for a week so we can see it in different light.

“A machine can copy a figure. It cannot decide where the light should break on the shoulder. That decision is the work.”Mohanlal Shilpi, to his son, 2014

How a new carver is trained

Three years, and no exceptions. The first year is the yard: moving blocks, mixing grinding paste, watching. The second year is the back of figures and the pedestals. The third year is drapery and jewellery, and only in the fourth year — if there is a fourth — does anyone cut a face. We have trained eleven people this way; four are still here.
