01 The rule

Nine faces tall, and not one inch of it arbitrary

The shilpa shastra gives a sculptor a proportion system, not a picture to copy. A standing Devi is nine head-lengths; a seated Ganesha is eight; the pedestal is a quarter of the figure; the shoulders are two faces wide. Change one and the eye knows before the mind does.

01

Select the block

Makrana marble is chosen at the quarry yard for grain and for the absence of a grey vein through the face. A block with a flaw in the wrong place becomes a pedestal.

2–5 days

02

Set out the talam

The height is divided into face-lengths with a string and a charcoal line. Every later mark refers back to these lines, so they are cut lightly and kept visible until the end.

1 day

03

Rough out

Point chisel and mallet, working from the back and the sides toward the front. At this stage the figure looks like a crowd of blocks, and clients are asked not to visit.

3–6 weeks

04

Carve the detail

Claw chisel, then riffler. Jewellery, drapery folds, fingers and the ends of the hair are cut last, because they are the first things a mallet slip can ruin.

4–12 weeks

05

Polish and finish

Wet grinding through four grits, then a hand polish with tin oxide. The face is polished by one carver only; two hands polish differently and the light will show it.

2–4 weeks

03 Stone

Three materials, three behaviours

A stone is a working partner. Marble takes a fine edge and shows every mistake; sandstone forgives the chisel and refuses the polish; bronze is a different craft entirely, cast from a wax model.

Makrana marble

Nagaur, Rajasthan

The white stone of the Taj, still quarried in the same belt. Fine-grained and translucent at the thin edges, which is why a marble hand looks lit from inside. It also shows a chisel mark from across a room.

density 2.7 g/cm³ · 4–5 months

Dholpur sandstone

Dholpur, Rajasthan

Warm, even, and softer under the chisel. Used for outdoor murtis and for large figures where marble would need a crane. It will not take a mirror polish; it takes a fine matte instead, which suits a garden.

density 2.3 g/cm³ · 3–4 months

Bronze

Cast in Swamimalai

Lost-wax casting, made from a wax model in the workshop and cast by a family we have worked with for thirty years. One murti, one mould; the mould is broken to release the figure.

hollow cast · 2–3 months

Marble inlay

Workshop bench

Semi-precious stone set into a marble ground: lapis, malachite, carnelian, mother-of-pearl. Used for thrones, halo rings and the eyes of large murtis, never for the whole figure.

adds 2–3 weeks

Come to the yard before you commission

A murti is easier to choose in the workshop than on a screen. We keep three or four finished figures in the courtyard, and a stack of blocks you can put your hand on. Bring the measurements of the room and the height of the shelf or plinth; bring a photograph if the figure is for a particular niche.

We do not take a deposit until the block is chosen and the talam is marked. Before that, the conversation is free — including the conversation where we tell you the figure you want will not fit.
