A historian who walks backwards for a living

Ritwik Sen was born four lanes from the first stop and has been listening to these stories since before he could read. He guides every walk himself, which is why there are only four a week.

At a glance Born 1988, Shobhabazar
History, Presidency University
Journalist, 2010–2016
Guiding since 2016
612 walks, 6 100 walkers
01The lane he grew up in

A grandfather with a boat

His grandfather rowed a ferry at Nimtala for thirty-one years, mostly at night. He was not a superstitious man, but he had rules: never call a passenger by name before they were seated, never count the people in the boat out loud, and never take the last crossing if the water was too still. Ritwik heard those rules long before he heard any ghost story, and he still thinks they are the most useful part of the folklore.

The family house is two lanes from the Rajbari gate, in a building that has been divided so many times that the stairwell no longer goes where it used to. He grew up with cousins on three floors, a courtyard that flooded every August, and a grandmother who told the petni story at the pond as if it were a piece of weather reporting.

“A ghost story is a record of what a neighbourhood decided not to forget. My job is to read it out loud without adding anything.”

02Journalism, then walking

Six years of night shifts, then a lantern

He read history at Presidency, then spent six years on the night desk of a Bengali daily, which is where he learned the two habits that made the walks possible: to write down what someone actually said rather than what the story needed, and to check a claim with a second person before printing it. In 2016 the paper cut its night staff. He had forty pages of notes from a column about old houses, and a friend suggested he try walking people through them.

The first walk was six people, one of whom was his aunt. The second was twelve, because the aunt told the neighbours. By the end of that winter he had a route, a lantern bought from a shop on Rabindra Sarani, and a rule he has kept since: every story on the walk must be one that somebody living on that lane told him first.

He still writes. The notebook goes out on every walk and comes back with new lines in it; two of the six stops have changed their story twice because a neighbour disagreed with the version being told.

03Method

How a story gets on the route

Every stop passes three tests before it is told to a paying group. The tests are not about whether a ghost is real. They are about whether the story belongs to the people who live with it.

  • Two tellers. The story has to be told by at least two people who did not get it from each other. One teller is an anecdote; two is a local tradition.
  • A named place. It has to attach to a doorway, a step, a tree or a stretch of water that still exists. Stories about “a house somewhere on Beadon Street” do not make the route.
  • Permission. If the story belongs to a family still living in the house, they are asked before it is told at their gate. Two families have said no, and those two stories are not on the walk.

What he adds is only the frame: the walking order, the distance between stops, the instruction that ends each tale. What he never adds is a scare. Nobody jumps out, no recording plays, and the lantern is a real oil lamp because a torch makes people look at their phones.

04Chronology

Ten years of Tuesdays, mostly

  • 1988Born in Shobhabazar, third of four children, in the house with the crooked stairwell.
  • 2006Presidency University, history. Discovers that the colonial archive is silent about the lanes he grew up in.
  • 2010Night desk at a Bengali daily. Learns to check a claim twice and to keep the quotes exact.
  • 2016First walk, six people. The route is four stops and ends at a tea shop that has since closed.
  • 2019Adds Nimtala ghat as the sixth and final stop after his grandfather's death.
  • 2022Two families withdraw permission for their stories. They are removed from the walk the same week.
  • 2026612 walks, roughly 6 100 walkers, four evenings a week from November to March.
05In print & on air

What other people said

  • City pages, 2019“Less a ghost tour than a history of the neighbourhood told by someone who is still annoyed about the drainage.”
  • Radio interview, 2021“I don't believe in ghosts. I believe in people who do, and they are more interesting.”
  • A walking magazine, 2024“The only night walk we have taken where the guide asked us to be quieter, not louder.”

All three are fictional, as is the guide, as is the lantern. The lanes, the ghats and the printing press are real; the stories are folk tales that belong to North Kolkata and were told to the making of this page long before it existed.
