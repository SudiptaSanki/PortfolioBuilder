The route

Six stops, one lantern

Move the lantern along the route, or tap a stop. Each note gives the distance from the previous stop, what people tend to hear there, and the one instruction that matters. The walk is 1.8 kilometres and ends by the river.

Shobhabazar Rajbari gate

0 m — meeting point · guardian

The gate is not the oldest thing on the street, but it is the most watched. Two sentinels are said to stand inside the arch after the household has gone to bed, and they do not like being counted. Walkers are asked to pass without stopping and to keep their voices below the level of the traffic.

Before you come

Three rules, and one promise

These are not theatre. They are the reason the walks have run for ten years without an incident, and the reason the neighbours still let us stand outside their doors.

  1. I

    Do not answer your name

    If someone calls it from a lane you have already passed, keep walking. A nishi borrows a voice you know; answering is how the story gets a second person in it.

  2. II

    Walk in the middle

    Not for the ghosts. For the open drains, the sleeping dogs and the scooters that come around corners without lights. The middle of the lane is the safe part.

  3. III

    No photographs of doorways

    People live in these houses. A doorway is a front door before it is a story, and nobody wants a stranger's flash in their courtyard at eleven at night.

The promise: nothing jumps out at you, nobody touches you, and every story we tell is one that someone in the neighbourhood told us first. If a walk is not frightening, it is still true.

Stop 02 · Kumortuli ghat

The woman who counts the idols

Every autumn the potters carry unfinished Durga idols down to the water to be blessed. A petni is said to stand at the fourth step and count them as they pass. If the count is wrong, one idol is left behind. The potters do not laugh at this; they count twice.

1. finding the fourth step is harder than it sounds

Stop 04 · the printing press

The Brahmin who corrects proofs

A brahmadaitya, the ghost of a learned man, is usually harmless and occasionally useful. This one is said to have worked at the press until 1998, and to still read the galleys left on the desk. Compositors who leave a typo find it corrected in the morning, in a hand nobody recognises.

2. the night watchman leaves one deliberate error

Stop 06 · Nimtala ghat

The ferryman who is not in a hurry

The last stop, and the quietest. The ghat has burned its dead for centuries, and the story here is not a monster but a man with a boat who will take you across for nothing, if you are not ready to be on this bank. The walk ends before the story does.

3. nobody has ever asked him the fare twice
