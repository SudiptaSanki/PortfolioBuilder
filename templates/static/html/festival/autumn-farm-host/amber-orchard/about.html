The orchard

Three terraces, one family,
seventy-four harvests

My grandfather planted the first forty trees in 1952, on ground the village said was too steep for apples. He was wrong about many things and right about that one. The farm is still in the same family, still on the same three terraces, still picked by hand.

01

Who farms here

I am Nirmala Rana. I took over the orchard from my father in 2011 after eleven years in Shimla working for a horticulture extension office, which taught me how much of the advice written for these hills does not survive contact with a slope.

My husband Mohan runs the packing shed and the tractor. Our daughter Tara grades fruit in the autumn and is studying agriculture in Solan. Two families from the village work with us through the season, and a third joins for picking. Nobody here is a labourer in the sense the word usually carries; they are the people who know which tree in Plot 2 always ripens early.

02

The ground

The farm faces north-west, which is not ideal. Apples want sun, and we lose an hour of it in the afternoon behind the ridge. What we gain is a slower thaw in spring, so the blossom misses most late frosts. In 2018 a frost took the entire crop in the valley below us and left ours untouched.

The soil is a shallow loam over slate, about forty centimetres deep before the pick hits stone. Every tree is on a raised terrace with a stone retaining wall, some of them rebuilt three times in my lifetime. When it rains hard in July, the water leaves the terraces clear rather than taking the topsoil with it. That is the whole engineering of the place.

03

How we farm

We spray six times a year, on a schedule set by the state horticulture department and adjusted by what we actually see on the leaves. We stopped using synthetic herbicide in 2009 and mow the rows instead, which costs more diesel and keeps the soil alive. The grass between the trees is not untidiness; it holds the terrace together.

Fruit is picked by hand into cloth-lined crates, never tipped, and graded the same evening under a single bulb in the shed. What does not grade for market becomes cider, chutney, or dried rings. In a good year we sell about seventy per cent of the crop fresh; in a hail year, far less, and the shed smells of vinegar for a month.

“A terrace is a promise you make to the hill every monsoon, and then have to keep.”

Mohan Rana, on rebuilding the wall below Plot 2 for the third time
04

The house

The building was the packing house until 1996, when a hailstorm convinced my father that the family should live next to the fruit rather than a kilometre below it. He raised the roof, put in two rooms and a kitchen, and added a third room in 2004 for the guests who kept asking to stay.

It is warm in a way that modern hill guesthouses are not, because the walls are stone and the stove runs all evening. It is also imperfect: the water is heated by the same fire, the bathroom is shared, and the electricity fails in most storms. We mention this before you book rather than after.

05

What we do not offer

  • No television, no room service, no generator for the whole house.
  • No bonfire parties: the forest above us is dry by October and we are not insured for it.
  • No picking outside your booked harvest slot, so the fruit is not bruised before it is graded.
  • No dogs from outside, because our two will not have it and they were here first.
