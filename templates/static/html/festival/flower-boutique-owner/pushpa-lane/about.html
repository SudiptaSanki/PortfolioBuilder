The florist

I have never sold a flower I did not see cut.

Meera Pillai. Born in Fort Kochi, twenty years around flowers, fourteen of them behind this counter. I buy at Broadway at four, open at eight, and close when the last bucket is empty — which is usually by six.

My aunt had a garland stall outside the temple at Mattancherry, and from the age of eight I was the child who held the needle. She paid me in banana chips and taught me the only lesson that matters in this trade: a garland with one bruised flower in it will be remembered as a garland of bruised flowers.

I worked the flower desk of a hotel on Marine Drive for six years, arranging imported roses in cold water for people who never asked what they cost. It taught me what I did not want. In 2011 I took the lease on this shop, with green shutters and a step that floods in the monsoon, and put three zinc buckets outside.

The routine has not changed since. At four I take the scooter to Broadway market, where the lorries come in from Thripunithura, Munnar and Tamil Nadu. I buy with my hands and my nose, not a list. By seven I am back, the shutters are up at eight, and the first thing I do is strip off everything that will not survive the morning.

Most of my customers are from the street: the men at the tea shop, the women from the fishing quarter, the occasional tourist who wants one flower to put behind her ear. I make the same bunch for a hotel lobby and for a funeral, because the flowers do not know the difference and neither should I.

Weddings are the other half of the year. I take four a month and no more, because there is only so much mogra in Kochi and I will not buy it from a refrigerated van in Ernakulam when the men at Broadway have it fresh.

The years

A trade learnt at a stall

There is no floristry diploma on the wall. There is an aunt, a needle, and fourteen years of being wrong about how much mogra a wedding needs.

  • 1993

    Holding the needle

    Eight years old, at my aunt's garland stall outside the Mattancherry temple. Paid in banana chips.

  • 2001

    The hotel flower desk

    Six years at a hotel on Marine Drive, arranging imported roses I could not afford and did not like.

  • 2011

    The green shutters

    A lease on Princess Street, three zinc buckets, and a step that floods every monsoon. It still floods.

  • 2014

    The first wedding

    Two hundred stems for a family I knew from the church. I undercharged by half and have never regretted it.

  • 2018

    Anju at the counter

    My niece takes the morning counter so I can stay at the market longer. She makes a better garland than I did at her age.

  • 2022

    No imported roses

    A decision made after one bad consignment and a bride in tears. Local, seasonal, or nothing.

  • Now

    Four weddings a month

    No more, because the market has only so much mogra and I will not buy it from a cold van.

How I work

Three things I will not do

I.

No cold-stored flowers

A flower that has spent three days in a chiller looks fine and smells of nothing. I would rather sell you six stems that were in the ground yesterday than twenty that were not.

II.

No hidden substitutions

If the mogra did not come in, I will call you at seven and say so, and offer what I have. A bunch that is not what was promised, delivered quietly, is the end of a customer.

III.

No more than four weddings

A month of five means something gets rushed. I have seen what a rushed garland looks like on a bride's neck, and it is not something I am willing to hand over.
