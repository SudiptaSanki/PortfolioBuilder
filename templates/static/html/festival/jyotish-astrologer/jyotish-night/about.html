Mylapore, and a mother who read at the kitchen table

My mother read charts at the kitchen table after dinner, with a pencil and a printed ephemeris, while I did my homework beside her. She never made a performance of it. If a neighbour came with a question about a wedding date, she would ask for the birth details, calculate for ten minutes, and give three possible dates with the reasons. Then she would add: “But if the hall is only free on Thursday, take Thursday.”

I read mathematics at Presidency College because I liked the certainty of it, and worked as a statistician for six years. That work taught me to distinguish a pattern from a coincidence, and to say “the sample is too small” out loud. It has made me a more cautious astrologer than I might have been, and a more useful one.

In 1998 I began to sit with S. Iyer, my mother's teacher, in a room above a stationery shop in Triplicane. He had been reading charts since 1948. He charged what people could pay, refused to read for anyone he had not met, and had a rule that I have kept: before you interpret, write down what you actually see. The chart first, the story after.

“Timing is not permission. A good period makes a thing easier; it does not make it wise.”

How the reading works

I use the Lahiri ayanamsa, whole-sign houses, and the Vimshottari dasha. I look first at the lagna and its lord, then the Moon and its nakshatra, then the current dasha and the transits of Saturn and Jupiter. That is the skeleton. The rest is the conversation: what you are actually asking, what you have already tried, and whether the question is the one the chart can answer.

I write the chart by hand before I open any software. The hand is slower and notices things. Then I check the calculation against a computer, because I am a statistician and I do not trust a single source.

What I owe the tradition

The vocabulary — houses, karakas, dashas — is not mine. It comes through my mother, through S. Iyer, and before them through a line of readers in Tamil Nadu whose names I know only from a notebook. I use it respectfully or not at all.

I also owe it the honesty to say when it fails. Some charts do not describe the person in front of me. When that happens, I say so rather than force the chart onto the life.

What I will not do

  • ✕Predict death, serious illness, the sex of a child, or the failure of a marriage.
  • ✕Sell gemstones, yantras, or “remedies” beyond a book, a fast, or a small charity. The expensive ones are not astrology.
  • ✕Read for a third party who has not asked. A mother asking about a son's marriage is a conversation, not a chart.
  • ✕Give medical, legal or financial advice, or tell anyone to stop a treatment.
  • ✓Say “the chart does not answer that” when it does not.
  • ✓Recommend a doctor, a lawyer or a therapist when the question belongs to them.
  • ✓Keep the chart and the conversation confidential, and delete a recording if you ask.

The rest of the week

Six readings a week, no more. Fridays are for teaching: four students, by hand, no software for the first year. I write a fortnightly column in Tamil for a Chennai daily, which pays for the lights and keeps me honest, because a column has a deadline and a reader who will write in if I am vague.

I am not on social media. The website is here so that people can find the fee and the rules before they call. Word of mouth has been enough for twenty-two years; I would rather it stayed that way.
