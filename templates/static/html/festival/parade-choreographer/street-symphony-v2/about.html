The person with the clipboard

About · Aitana Ruiz

I spent nine years inside parades before I started designing them — first in a comparsa in Cádiz, dancing somebody else's spacing, learning in my feet what a closing gap feels like from inside the block. You feel it before you see it: the music of the band behind you gets louder, and suddenly there is nowhere for the turn to go.

Since 2016 I have designed the movement of 23 parades for carnival associations, festival offices and city councils. My opinion, after a decade of route sheets: everyone rehearses the dancing; nobody rehearses the gap — and the gap is where parades are lost. So the gap is what I rehearse, measure, print and defend.

On parade day I walk the tail, never the head. The head is theatre. The tail is where the timings are true.

How a parade gets designed

The method · five stops
  1. 01

    The route walk

    The street is measured on foot, at walking pace, with a wheel and a notebook: widths at every turn, pinch points, crossing streets, the exact metres where a float becomes a wall. Ten weeks out, minimum. A route designed from a map is how San Juan 2023 happened — that file is on the Parades page, unedited.

  2. 02

    The block audit

    Every group is timed at rehearsal: its real length on the move, its real pace, its turning behaviour. Groups are measured as they are — a children's block is not a short adults' block, and a samba school has the pace of a river, not a band.

  3. 03

    The running order as a safety document

    The order is written, costed in minutes against the closure window, and signed — by me, by the association, and by the marshal coordinator. Gaps at crossings are printed in red. Once signed, the order changes only in writing; a parade is not the place for improvisation at scale.

  4. 04

    The marshal briefing

    Marshals walk their own section with the sheet in hand before parade day: where their gap is, what a closing gap looks like, and their written authority to hold a block. A marshal who cannot stop the parade is decoration.

  5. 05

    Parade day — and the morning after

    I walk the tail with the sheet and a stopwatch. The day after, the tail marshal's timings are compared with the plan and the differences are written into the file — including, especially, the ones that make the plan look bad.

The crowd remembers the parade, not the choreographer

That is as it should be, and this site keeps the credit in its proper order on purpose. A parade belongs to its association and its city. Each group's choreography belongs to that group's own captains — where I have choreographed a block myself, the register says so, block by block; where I haven't, their names stand, not mine. The floats belong to their builders. The music belongs to the bands.

What is mine is narrower and, I would argue, harder: the spaces between — the order, the gaps, the pace, the minutes. When those are right, nobody notices them at all. That invisibility is the job done properly, and it is also why this portfolio is made of timings and route sheets rather than applause.
