01 The director · Pasadena

I started in the back rank, and I never left it.

Twenty years of marching, twelve of teaching, and a notebook full of routes. This is how a trumpet player from East LA ended up drawing charts for four hundred people on New Year's morning.

A trumpet in the third row

I grew up in Boyle Heights, in a house where the radio was always on and the Dodgers were a religion. In seventh grade I chose trumpet because my cousin had one and because the band room was the only air-conditioned room in the school. I was not a prodigy. I was a kid who liked being part of something that moved together, and who noticed, early, that the bands that sounded good were usually the ones whose feet landed at the same time.

At Garfield High I marched four years, and in my senior year I was drum major. That job is mostly logistics disguised as showmanship: you are the person who decides when the block turns, how many counts it takes, and whether the tuba line has room to breathe. I loved it. I have been doing some version of it ever since.

Twelve years of teaching

I taught high school band in the San Gabriel Valley from 2004 to 2016. In that time I charted more than sixty field shows and maybe two hundred parades, including a run of eight years taking a 180-piece band down Colorado Boulevard. Teaching taught me the thing that charting software cannot: a move that reads beautifully on paper can be impossible for a sixteen-year-old carrying a sousaphone into a headwind. Every chart I draw now is tested against that memory.

In 2016 I left the classroom to chart full-time. It was a strange transition — from a job with a bell schedule to one with five clients a year — but the work was already finding me. The first independent commission came from a band director I had judged at a competition in Fresno; she wanted a parade chart that would let her keep the woodwinds in the middle of the block without the whole formation looking like a rectangle.

The music tells you what the feet should do. The route tells you whether they can.

How I work

  • A route walk first. Every chart starts on foot, at parade hour, with a tape measure and a camera. Trees, curbs, crown, camera positions, the place where the road narrows by six feet.
  • Tempo before formation. The tempo decides step size, and step size decides how much street a formation needs. Changing tempo late means redrawing everything.
  • Counts, not gestures. Every move is written in eight-count phrases with a coordinate for each rank and file. If a section cannot walk it while playing, it is redrawn.
  • Rehearse to the camera. The final rehearsal is run the way the broadcast will see it, from the curb, not from the tower.

What I will not do

I do not chart a move that requires a player to run to make the count. I do not take a parade I cannot walk first. I do not write a cadence that only works in a rehearsal hall with perfect acoustics. And I do not promise a formation the street cannot hold, however good it looks in the sketch.
