Chai tastings · north Kolkata

Chai is a small weather you make in a cup.

I brew single-estate leaf and masala blends for tastings above a printing press in Jorasanko. The work is mostly arithmetic — grams, millilitres, minutes — and then the last ten seconds, which are not.

The flight

Five cups, in the order I pour them

The tasting is 45 minutes and the cups get smaller as the tea gets stronger. Water is always on the table; so is a bowl of khari biscuits.

  1. Darjeeling first flushcup 1

    Risheehat, 2026. Brewed at 85°C, no milk, three minutes. Tastes of green apple and wet stone, and goes bitter if you look at it too long.

  2. Nilgiri Korakundahcup 2

    High-grown, bright, a little floral. The cup I use to explain what “brisk” means without saying the word.

  3. Kangra orthodoxcup 3

    Softer and rounder, with a honeyed finish. Served with a sliver of jaggery instead of sugar.

  4. Assam CTC, Halmaricup 4

    The everyday cup, malty and strong. This is where the milk argument happens, and where most people discover they want more leaf than they thought.

  5. House masalacup 5

    Assam base, green cardamom, clove, ginger and a little fennel. Boiled with milk, the way it is made at the stall on the corner, only weighed.

Where the leaf comes from

Four gardens, one cupboard

I buy small quantities directly from two gardens and through a broker for the rest. The Assam leaf is from Halmari, in the Brahmaputra valley; the Darjeeling from Risheehat, on a slope above the Teesta. Nilgiri comes from Korakundah, and the Kangra leaf from a co-operative near Palampur that sells mostly to Delhi.

Everything is tasted before it is bought, in a room with no music and a scale on the table. A garden that cannot tell me the plucking date does not get a second order.

“Anyone can make chai. The trouble is that most people stop tasting it at the moment it starts to work.”

Nilima Basu
