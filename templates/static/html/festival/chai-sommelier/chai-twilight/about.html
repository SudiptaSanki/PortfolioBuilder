The sommelier · since 2016

I weigh the leaf because I cannot weigh the morning

Nilima Basu left a job in market research to open a four-table tasting room above her family’s printing press. She buys small lots, tastes them in a silent room, and does not believe in “strong” as a compliment.

My family has owned the printing press downstairs since 1954. I grew up above it, falling asleep to the offset machine and waking to the smell of ink and, from the corner, the chai stall’s kettle. The stall is still there. That is where I learned that the same leaf can produce two entirely different cups depending on who is holding the strainer.

I spent eleven years doing consumer research for a tea company, which meant watching people say one thing about a cup and drink another. In 2016 I left, took the first floor, and put four tables in it. The room has no music, no printed menu, and no sugar on the table unless you ask.

The difference between a good cup and a great one is about ten seconds at the end. You cannot rush those ten seconds and you cannot automate them.

I taste between thirty and fifty samples a month. The method is the same as any cupping: three grams, boiling water, a covered cup, four minutes, and a spoon. I do not swallow. I write down what the cup does at the back of the throat, because that is where the difference between a ₹90 leaf and a ₹320 leaf actually lives.

Buying is the other half. A garden that cannot tell me the plucking date does not get a second order. A broker who sends a sample that does not match the lot does not get a third. Small lots mean I run out, and running out is better than stretching a blend with something I would not drink.

People ask whether chai can be a career. It can be a table with four chairs and a scale, which is what this is. The rest — the weddings, the festivals, the restaurant kitchens — grew out of people sitting at that table and asking how I did it.

How a tasting runs

  • 01

    Look at the leaf

    Dry leaf on a white saucer, in daylight. Colour, evenness, how much dust.

  • 02

    Weigh, don’t guess

    Three grams, 150 ml, four minutes. The same numbers every time, or the comparison is meaningless.

  • 03

    Smell the lid

    Before the first sip. This is where the fruit and the flowers are, and where a stewed cup gives itself away.

  • 04

    Slurp, then wait

    The back of the throat is the honest part. Count five seconds after swallowing before writing anything down.

  • 05

    Write the second cup

    Everything tastes different after four cups. The notes that survive the fifth are the ones worth buying.
