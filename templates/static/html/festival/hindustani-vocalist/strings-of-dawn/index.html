Eight prahars, twenty-four hours

Hindustani music keeps a clock. Each raga belongs to a stretch of the day, and singing it at the wrong hour is like opening a door into the wrong room — the notes are all there, but the light is wrong. The wheel below is set to your own clock.

I keep to it more strictly than most singers I know. Marwa at noon sounds like a rehearsal; Marwa at dusk, when the sky is doing the same thing, does not.

The practice room

A tanpura and twelve swaras, tuned to a Sa you choose. Nothing here makes a sound until you press start — the page is silent when it loads, and stays silent if you leave it.

Four strings, tuned Pa – Sa – Sa – Sa below your chosen Sa.

Swaras over the drone

Press a note to hear it against Sa, with the just ratio it is tuned to. The cents column is the distance from a piano's equal temperament.

Play a swara to see its ratio and its distance from equal temperament.

Learning the slow way

I take six students at a time, in the old arrangement: one hour a week, sitting on the floor, no notation in front of you. The first month is one note. The second month is three. Nobody has ever complained about this except the two who left.

What I ask for is a tanpura at home, twenty minutes a day, and the willingness to sing the same phrase for a year until it stops sounding like practice.
