The singer, and the twelve years it took to be allowed to sing one raga in public.

Saanvi Chatterjee sings khayal in the Banaras gharana and thumri in the Poorab style. She lives in Kothrud, Pune, teaches six students, and is at present arguing with Marwa.

The first thing I remember is not a song. It is my mother's hand on my back, between the shoulder blades, pushing me upright at five in the morning on a January morning in 1994. I was six. She had a small harmonium with a broken reed and she made me hold Sa until the room stopped moving. I have been doing that same thing for thirty-two years, in better rooms, and it has not yet stopped working.

My mother was not a professional singer. She had learned to sing in the Arya Samaj way, bhajans and a little light classical, and she thought that a girl in Pune in the nineties should have one thing that was entirely her own. She chose this. I did not choose it until much later, and I am glad about that, because the choice I made at nineteen was made with my eyes open.

The years with Anjali tai

In 1997 my mother took me to Vidushi Anjali Deshpande, who had moved to Pune after thirty years in Banaras and was taking a small number of students in a room behind her house in Sadashiv Peth. She was seventy-one, she smoked, and she had no patience at all. For the first two years I sang nothing but the ten-minute sargam exercises in Bilawal and Kafi, and I was not allowed to sing a bandish until I could do those without thinking about where my voice was going.

What she taught me was not repertoire. Repertoire is available everywhere now; you can find fifty recordings of Darbari before lunch. What she taught me was how to sit with a raga long enough for it to stop being a set of rules and start being a place.

You are not decorating the raga. You are trying to find out what it already contains, and you will not find out today.

Anjali Deshpande, in a lesson, some time in 2004

She died in 2014. I had twelve years with her, which is more than most people get, and it was not enough. There are still phrases I hear in my head in her voice, and when I sing them I am not sure whether they are mine or hers, and I have stopped worrying about it. That is what a gharana is, in the end: a set of habits you did not invent and cannot fully explain.

What I actually do for a living

It is worth saying plainly, because the websites of classical musicians tend to skip it: almost nobody in India makes a living from khayal. I teach. I teach six private students, I take a class of twelve at a school in Kothrud twice a week, and I sing at about thirty baithaks and small festivals a year. In 2021, when the baithaks stopped, I taught over video for eighteen months to thirty-one students in eleven cities, and that is the only reason I still have a house.

I also sing for films — three so far, all Marathi, all of them using a thumri or a dadra rather than a khayal — and I have made peace with the fact that this pays better than the thing I have spent my life learning. The films are how I afford the tanpura repairs.

What I am working on now

Marwa. I have been working on Marwa for four years. It is a sunset raga, six notes, no Pa in the ascent, and it is the most difficult thing I sing because there is nowhere to hide in it — every phrase is exposed, and the smallest lapse in tuning turns the whole thing into something else. My teacher would not let me sing it in public. I have sung it twice, both times badly, and I am going to keep at it until I have a version I would be willing to leave behind.

Alongside that, I am slowly making a record of the bandishes Anjali tai gave me that nobody else seems to sing — eleven of them so far, recorded in my front room with two microphones, no editing, mistakes included. It will take another two years at this rate.

Off the stage

I am not a person who is interesting on the internet. I read a great deal of Marathi fiction, I keep three kinds of basil alive on a balcony that gets too much sun, and I walk in the morning before I sing, because the voice sits better when the body has been used. I do not have a guru anymore, which is the strangest thing about being fifty-adjacent in this tradition: you become the person others ask, and there is nobody left to ask.

On the shelf

Five recordings I go back to when I have lost the thread.

  • Kumar GandharvaBhimpalasi, 1968 — for the way he refuses to hurry the uttaranga.
  • Kishori AmonkarBhoop, live in Bombay — proof that a raga can be simple and still unanswerable.
  • Amir KhanMarwa, 1965 — the recording I am not allowed to imitate.
  • Gangubai HangalDarbari, any year — the lowest voice in the tradition, and the steadiest.
  • Bhimsen JoshiMiyan ki Malhar — the rain arrives in the third minute.
