The craft

Paper, paste,
and patience

Cartonería is the Mexican craft of building figures from paper and paste. A piñata is its most impatient expression: something made slowly, to be destroyed quickly.

Workshop since 2011IztapalapaNo plastic armature

The bench

  • Workshop 2 rooms, 38 m²
  • Drying rack 12 places
  • Paste Wheat flour, water, salt
  • Paper Newsprint, 48 g
  • Fringe Crepe, 1 cm strips
  • Output 34 a month

What I will not use

  • Plastic or wire frames
  • Printed plastic sheeting
  • Glitter on anything a child touches
  • Balloons as the body

A balloon piñata is fast, and it fails at the first hit. The paste shell is what makes the party last long enough to be worth having.

My grandfather made Judas figures for Holy Week and sold them at the market in Iztapalapa. I learned cartonería from him without ever calling it that; to us it was just the table in the back room with the paste bucket under it.

The armature

A piñata begins as a reed and a cone of newspaper. The reed becomes the neck or the hanging point; the cone becomes the core the rest is built around. You add crumpled paper, tie it with string, and keep turning it until it stands up on the bench by itself.

This stage is not about the shape you want. It is about the shape that will hold four layers of wet paper without sagging. A burro's belly has to be rounder than it looks in the drawings; a star's points have to be filled with paper so they do not collapse under their own weight when the paste dries.

Four layers, four days

The paste is flour, water and a spoon of salt to keep it from souring. Newspaper is torn, never cut: a torn edge feathers into the layer below, and a cut edge leaves a hard line that splits when the piñata is struck. Layer one is coarse, layer four is almost smooth.

  • Layer oneBig torn strips over the armature, no care taken with the surface.
  • Layer twoSmaller pieces, pressed into the corners. The star points are filled here.
  • Layer threeThe smoothing layer. Finger-width strips, laid in crossing directions.
  • Layer fourA thin final skin. This is the layer that takes the crepe paper.

Each layer dries a full day on the rack. In the rainy season it takes two, and I do not argue with the weather; a piñata that is wrapped damp grows a smell that no amount of crepe paper hides.

The fringe

Crepe paper is folded into a packet, cut into strips a centimetre wide, then unfolded. Each strip is glued in a row, starting at the bottom of the figure and working up, so every row covers the seam of the one beneath it. A medium burro takes about two thousand strips and most of a day.

The colour order matters more than the colours. A row of one colour reads as a band; three colours alternating read as a moving surface, which is what you want when the piñata is swinging on a rope and nobody is standing still to look at it.

The door

The last thing is the flap: a square cut at the top of the back, the candy loaded through it, and a strip of the same crepe paper glued over the cut. The flap has to be strong enough to hold three kilos of candy and weak enough that the first real hit opens it.

That is the whole craft, honestly. A container that opens once, at the right moment, in front of the right people.
