Four builds, and how long each one stood.

Every piece leaves the workshop with a number on the base, a panel thickness in the notes, and a date it was taken down. These are the four I would show an architect.

Manor no. 4

Built for the lobby of Hotel Norge, 42 centimetres long and 48 tall, on a base board of 16 mm ply edged in icing. Two storeys with a full interior wall, because at that length the front wall would have bowed under the roof.

The gable ends are one piece each, cut with the roof pitch built in, so the ridge closes without filler. The windows are isomalt poured into paper moulds; they caught the lobby lights and made the whole thing look lit from inside.

Stave church study

A study of the Borgund stave church, built for the competition at Pepperkakebyen and taken to a silver. Fifty-two centimetres tall on a base 24 wide, which is a tall, thin thing to keep upright in a room full of visitors.

The tiered roofs are separate panels stacked and iced one level at a time, with a night of drying between each. The whole upper structure is carried on four internal columns, the same way the real church carries its roof. It survived a child leaning on the table and did not survive the van home.

Longhouse for a wedding

Fifty-six centimetres long for a wedding in Ålesund: a single long hall with a shallow gable, a low ridge and a door at each end, so the guests could be served from both sides. The longest span I have built without an interior wall, which was a mistake I have not repeated.

It held for the evening and sagged overnight because the roof was carrying its own weight across 56 centimetres with nothing under it. The couple kept it anyway; I rebuilt the roof for them in January with a hidden interior wall and a ridge beam.

Bergen row house, three bays

The first commission, and still the one I use to explain the practice. Three narrow bays under one roof, the way the old wooden houses on the hillside are built: each gable its own panel, each window its own small square of icing.

It is 34 centimetres long and only 22 tall, which makes it a good test of edges rather than structure. The whole thing is 8 mm, and the icing between the bays is doing real work, holding three separate fronts in line.

What the numbers mean

The panel count and thickness are the only figures that matter when a client asks why one build costs more than another. A 6 mm cottage can be cut and built in a day. A 10 mm competition piece with an interior frame takes a week and a half and uses three times the dough.

  • 6 mmSmall cottages, roofs under 24 cm, decorative pieces1 to 2 days
  • 8 mmStructural walls, anything over 30 cm, hotel work3 to 5 days
  • 10 mmCompetition pieces and anything that travels6 to 10 days
