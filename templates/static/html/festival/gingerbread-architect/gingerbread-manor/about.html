I was trained as an architect. Now I bake buildings.

Same drawings, same concern with load paths, different material. Gingerbread behaves like a brittle masonry wall: strong in compression, useless in tension, and ruined by a bad joint.

The first gingerbread house I made was in 2019, for my niece, from a kit. It fell over on the second day because the roof was too heavy for the walls and the icing had been mixed too thin. I fixed it the way I would have fixed a building: I drew it, worked out the panel weights, thickened the walls and put an interior wall in.

Before that I spent eleven years in an architecture office in Oslo, mostly on schools and one library. I left in 2018, moved back to Bergen, and now I bake between September and December and draw the rest of the year. The skills transferred more literally than I expected: a cut list is a cut list, and a client who wants a taller tower without a wider base gets the same conversation.

The dough is a structural material

Most gingerbread recipes are built for eating: butter, sugar, a soft crumb. That is the wrong material for a wall. My recipe is drier, heavier on rye, and baked until it is hard through the middle. It does not taste bad, but nobody asks for a second piece.

One batch makes roughly 1.1 kg of dough, which is one manor of about 30 cm. Rest the dough cold for 24 hours before rolling; it will fight you otherwise.

From drawing to standing manor

1. Draw at full size

Every panel is drawn at 1:1 on paper, cut out, and used as the template. The drawing includes the gable angle and the roof slope, because a roof cut two degrees wrong will not meet at the ridge and no amount of icing hides it.

2. Roll to a gauge

Two steel bars of the right thickness sit either side of the dough, and the rolling pin rides on them. 8 mm for structural panels, 6 mm for roofs on small builds, 10 mm if it has to survive being moved in a van.

3. Bake hard, cool flat

175 °C, 12 to 15 minutes for 6 mm, 18 to 22 for 8 mm. The panel comes out of the oven slightly flexible and warps as it cools if it is left on the tray. It goes straight onto a flat board with another board on top and a weight. This is the step people skip, and it is the step that decides whether the house stands.

4. Trim square, then mortar

Edges are trimmed with a fine saw while the panel is still warm. Royal icing at 8 parts icing sugar to 1 part egg white is the mortar; it goes on 8 mm thick, the panel is pressed into it, and a support stays in place for six hours.

5. The roof goes on tomorrow

Never the same day. The walls need a night to reach full strength, and the roof is the heaviest thing in the build. Before it goes on, I add corner gussets inside and, on anything over 30 cm, a full interior wall.

6. Base, trim, repair kit

The manor is iced to a board wider than the eaves, finished with piped trim and isomalt windows, and sent out with a small bag of icing sugar, a tube of ready icing and a one-page repair sheet. Hotels lose a chimney every season.

What goes wrong

Warping, mostly, from cooling on the tray. Cracks at the corners when the icing is too thin. Roofs that slide because the joint was made on a humid day. And once, memorably, a manor that was perfect until the hotel put it next to a radiator and the isomalt windows slumped by the second week.

I now send a one-page sheet with every build: keep it out of direct heat, do not refrigerate, repair with the icing provided, and do not let the night porter move it by the chimney.

A cold, dry room matters more than a good oven. Bergen in November is ideal, which is the only nice thing I have ever said about Bergen in November.

Teaching

Two workshops each autumn at the studio in Skiveien, six people each, and a gingerbread architecture unit at the Bergen School of Architecture every October. Bring an apron and a steady hand.

Ice it like a building, not like a cake. The icing is doing structural work, and it should look like it.
