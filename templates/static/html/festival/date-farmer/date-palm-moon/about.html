The almanac · 1978 to now

Four generations,
two wells, and a
great deal of salt.

The grove has outlasted a cyclone, three dry years and one very bad decision about a tractor. What follows is the honest account: the water, the salt, the work, and the year as the palms measure it.

My grandfather, Suleman Hingora, bought this land in 1977 because nobody else wanted it. It sits a few kilometres from the Little Rann, where the ground water turns brackish in April and the topsoil is more sand than soil. He had been a date trader in Bhuj and had watched enough good fruit come in from Sindh to believe he could grow it himself.

The first sixty palms went in along the northern edge, where a shallow well gave water at four metres. For eight years the grove was a hobby that lost money. Then in 1986 the second well was dug to eleven metres and the block of Khalas came into its own. My father, Yusuf, took over the picking in 1994 and the accounts in 2001, and it is his handwriting on the yellow ledgers in the office.

The salt is the constant. Every year the well is tested in March, and every year the number is a little higher. We have learned to irrigate in the evening, to keep a wide dry margin between blocks, and to plant the new offshoots on the eastern edge where the water is least bad. The palms tolerate what wheat will not, but they are not indifferent to it: a salty year gives smaller fruit with a thicker skin.

In June 2023, Cyclone Biparjoy came ashore near Jakhau and went straight across us. We lost a hundred and eighty palms outright and the roofs of the mat house and the pump shed. The insurance paid for the roofs and nothing else. What we replanted came from our own offshoots, which is slower than buying nursery stock and much cheaper, and the first of those palms was pollinated in 2026.

The grove is now nine blocks and 1,240 palms. My son Aamir runs the picking crew; my daughter Hafsa keeps the records and has begun selling direct, which is the first new idea the farm has had in twenty years. The office still has the yellow ledgers, and she still writes in them.

The well

The second well is eleven metres deep and gives about 9,000 litres an hour in February, half that by May. In a dry year we pump at night to lose less to evaporation, and the pump runs on a diesel engine that my father rebuilt twice and refuses to replace.

The March salinity reading has risen 210 ppm in ten years. It is the number that decides what we plant next.

Water, salt and work

The year as the palms measure it

Not every month has a harvest in it. Most of them are irrigation, pruning, repair and waiting, which is the part of date farming that the photographs never show.

The crew

Nine people, six of them in August

Picking is climbing work and it is paid by the palm, not by the day. The crew has been the same six men for eleven years, which is longer than any of the machinery has lasted.

Picking

Four climbers

Rope ladder, waist belt, a knife and a basket. A good climber does sixty palms in a day and knows which bunch to leave for the next pass.

Mat house

Two handlers

Spread, turn, grade. Aseel needs turning twice a day for eight days; Khalas is more forgiving and dries in five.

Office

Two, plus family

Weighing, ledgers, boxes, the traders' calls, and the slow work of selling the fruit before it is picked.

The next planting

Forty Medjool palms on trial, and room for sixty more

The eastern block is the test: if the Medjool finishes before the September rain, we will put in another sixty palms in 2028. If it splits, the ground goes back to Khalas, which has never let us down.
