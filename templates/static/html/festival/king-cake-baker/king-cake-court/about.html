King Cake Court/The bakery

Two ovens, one rule: nothing leaves before it is iced.

King Cake Court has been on Magazine Street since 1998. It is still a two-oven bakery, still braiding by hand, and still closing the order book when the ovens are full rather than when the freezer is.

Clémence Boudreaux opened the bakery with her mother's brioche recipe, a second-hand proofing cabinet and a hand-painted sign that has been repainted twice. The first Carnival, they sold forty-one cakes. In 2025, the two ovens turned out just over nine thousand.

The recipe did not change

Brioche, butter, egg yolks, a cold ferment and a braid. That is the whole trick, and it cannot be hurried. The dough is mixed on Tuesday for a Thursday pickup, held overnight at 4 °C, braided on Wednesday morning, given a slow warm rise, baked, cooled completely, then iced four hours before collection. A cake that is iced warm sweats under the glaze and goes soggy by the second slice.

The fillings changed because customers asked. Cream cheese arrived in 2004 after a nurse at Touro wrote a note; praline came from a batch made for a wedding and never left the list. The cinnamon ring remains the measuring stick. If a new baker can braid a cinnamon ring that Clémence will put her name to, they stay.

“The cake is a ring because a ring is shared. Cut it in the middle first, then outwards. Nobody wants the first slice from the end.”

Clémence Boudreaux, on the correct way to cut a king cake

Two ovens, and what that means

Each oven holds twenty-eight Court rings or twelve Balcony rings per bake. A bake takes fifty minutes including the turn. That is the entire arithmetic of the business: the number of cakes that can leave the door on any given day is fixed by the ovens, not by demand. During the last week of Carnival the list closes early, and every year somebody is angry about it. We would rather be honest about capacity than sell a cake that was rushed.

The bakery does not ship. A king cake is a bread, and bread in a box for three days is not the thing people are nostalgic for. Pickup is at the window on Magazine, or delivery within five miles by bicycle on the morning of the parade.

The baby, and other rules

The plastic baby goes in after icing, through the bottom of the ring, wrapped in food-safe paper. It is not baked; a melted baby is a lawsuit and a ruined cake. Finding the baby makes you king for the day and obliges you to bring the next cake. This is not our rule, but we enforce it with the paper crown.

We do not write names on cakes, we do not do novelty shapes, and we do not make a "healthy" version. There is a bakery two blocks down that will happily do all three, and we send people there with a straight face.

After the storm

In 2005 the bakery flooded to the height of the front counter. The ovens were rebuilt, the sign was dried on a porch, and the family baked in a borrowed kitchen in Baton Rouge for a season. The first cake after reopening was a cinnamon Court ring, cut on the sidewalk and handed out in squares because nobody could find a knife. It tasted, according to three people who were there, exactly right.

Who is in the kitchen

Clémence still braids on Tuesdays. Her daughter Inès runs the order book and the ovens, and can tell from the sound of the proofing cabinet whether the yeast is unhappy. Two bakers, one counter person, and a rotating cast of nephews on parade mornings. That is the whole court.

What we do not do

No shipping. No names on cakes. No novelty shapes. No gluten-free ring (the brioche is the point). No orders held past 4 pm.

We will happily tell you where to find any of those things nearby.
