The work, in three entries

From the kitchen book

Not a gallery — a ledger. Each entry is written the way the kitchen works: what was asked, what was decided, what came of it, with the figures left in. The first entry is a bad year. It is placed first on purpose.

Entry 1 — The Light Year, Told Straight

Kitchen book · Autumn, the current season

The ask

The September figures came in from Sicily: 7–11 kg of in-shell pistachios a tree, against 18–24 kg the year before. Alternate bearing — the trees rest the year after a heavy crop, and no contract, however written, argues with it. The kernel crop would be 420–660 kg for the whole season: enough for 1,400–2,200 classic trays, not the 3,600–4,800 of a heavy year. The Eid book was due to open in six weeks.

The decisions

Three, all printed before a single order was taken. The tray price rose from £38 to £44, with the crop figures published beside it — the rise explained itself or it didn't happen. The filling stayed at 300 g a tray: thinning the filling to hold the price was the one option refused outright, because a customer who has eaten the heavy-year tray would taste the difference and never be told. And where the book ran past the crop, a waitlist replaced a substitution — no cheaper nut under the same name, ever.

The outcome

The festival book sold at the printed price, and the waitlist cleared on the second bake of the festival week. One wholesale buyer renewed on the strength of the letter alone — her words are on the home page, attached to the tray they describe. The light year cost the kitchen volume. It did not cost it a single quiet recipe change, and the heavy-year book is already open at £38.

Entry 2 — Five Batches of Praline

Kitchen book · New line development

The ask

A third line for the festival table: pistachio praline — kernels caramelised and ground to a paste, layered like the classic — that would stand beside the two established trays without borrowing their reputations. Six weeks were booked for development before any tray could be sold.

The decisions

Five test batches, each one tasted blind against the classic and written up the same day. Batch 1 was praline at full sweetness against the standard syrup — cloying by the third piece. Batch 2 cut the praline's sugar and lost the caramel note entirely. Batch 3 found the fault in the syrup instead: the honey came down from 120 g to 80 g and the sugar with it, and the praline was allowed to be the sweetest thing in the tray. Batch 4 held the flavour but slumped — the paste layer went on warm and soaked the filo; it is now layered cold. Batch 5 changed nothing. That is the batch that went on sale.

The outcome

A festival batch of 60 trays at £46 sold out in two days, and the line joins the register permanently next season — at the season's pistachio price, like everything else here. The batch book, including the two failures, is shown to any buyer who asks. A line that needed five attempts is not embarrassed by the first four.

Entry 3 — The Standing Order

Kitchen book · Wholesale, three seasons

The ask

A Stoke Newington deli wanted baklava on its counter every week of the year — six trays a week, no gaps for festivals, no surprises at the till. The difficulty was never the baking. It was the price: how does a standing order survive a crop that halves?

The decisions

The standing order was written the way the rate card is written. Twenty per cent under the season's price, whatever the season's price turns out to be — and the season's price agreed in writing, a full season ahead, from the published harvest figures, so the deli plans its shelf price from the same numbers the kitchen plans its book. In the light year the deli's tray went from £30.40 to £35.20 (the wholesale line under £38 and £44 respectively), and it was agreed the previous spring, not announced the previous week.

The outcome

Three seasons, zero missed weeks — including the festival week, when the deli's trays are baked first, before the Eid book's. The order has been renewed twice. The arrangement works because neither side is ever surprised: the harvest is published, the formula is fixed, and the only thing that changes is the number both parties already know how to read.
