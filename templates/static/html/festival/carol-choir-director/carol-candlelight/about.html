Maeve O’Donnell

A choir should be able to tune without the organ

Maeve O’Donnell has directed the Candlelight Choir since 2019. She teaches sight-singing at the parish school, plays the organ badly, and insists the choir rehearse in the dark at least once before Advent.

I grew up in Dorchester in a house where the radio was always on and the piano was mostly furniture. The first choir I sang in was at school, because the alternative was detention. I was an alto who could not read music and learned everything by ear, which is why I now make everyone sight-read for twenty minutes at the start of rehearsal.

I studied music education at Boston Conservatory, spent six years teaching middle school general music, and came to St Brigid’s in 2019 because the previous director left in September and the service was in December. The choir had eleven singers and a filing cabinet of music nobody had sorted since 1994. We now have twenty-four singers and the same filing cabinet.

A congregation does not hear the notes. It hears whether the room believes what it is singing.

My work is mostly not conducting. It is choosing two carols that a tired choir can sing beautifully instead of six it can survive; it is noticing that the tenors go flat after the third verse; it is standing in the empty church on a Tuesday and working out where the sopranos need to stand so the descant reaches the back pew without shouting. Conducting is the last ten per cent.

I use the voicing desk on the front page with the section leaders because it shows the things a score hides: the distance between the alto and the tenor, whether the root is doubled, where a pair of parts will move in parallel. It is not a substitute for listening. It is a way of not wasting the choir’s time on a chord I could have checked at my desk.

The choir is unpaid and auditions are gentle. I ask for a hymn verse, a short sight-reading exercise, and a conversation about Wednesday evenings. The ability to read is useful. The ability to listen is essential.

A rehearsal, in order

Twenty minutes of sight-reading. Ten minutes of breathing and tuning to a held A. The hardest passage of the week, slow, in four parts. A break with tea. Then the run, standing as we will stand on the night.

We rehearse in the dark once, in the last week, so nobody is surprised by how little they can see.
