How we build

The frame first, and the argument before it.

A pandal is a building with a five-day life and a fifty-thousand-person evening. Everything about the way we work follows from those two facts: the frame has to be honest, the skin has to be replaceable, and the light has to be on its own switch.

The yard

My father Haripada rented nine hundred square feet behind a printing press in 1989 and built three pandals that first season. The yard is now ten times that, with a tin roof over the loft where the shola and paper work happens, and a concrete pad at the back where a frame can be stood up and walked around before it goes to site. Every committee that commissions us is invited to that pad before we start.

The yard works from July. Before that it is a storeroom of frames, cane bundles and the previous season's panels, sorted and labelled by committee. Committees often ask why we do not keep photographs on the walls. We keep drawings and frames instead; a photograph tells you what a pandal looked like, a frame tells you whether it will stand.

“A pandal is a building that has to be beautiful on the fourth evening and gone by the sixth morning.”

The frame

Bamboo comes from Barasat, in bundles of twenty to twenty-four culms, and cane from the same growers. We use bamboo for the compression members, the posts and the arched ribs, and cane for the ties, because cane bends where bamboo splits. Every joint is a diagonal lashing in jute twine, eight turns, with a cane peg through the culm where the load is vertical. A frame is only as good as its lashings: a pandal that leans in the third evening leaned because someone tied a joint in a hurry.

The frame is designed to be taken apart. Posts are cut to standard lengths of six and eight feet, so that a committee's frame can be stored, re-used and re-dressed rather than thrown away. We have one frame in the yard that has been out eleven times since 2014.

The skin

Cloth first, then the panels. Cloth is stretched over the frame and sized with a starch paste so that it does not flap in the wind between the panels; the panels themselves carry the theme. Shola pith is cut in the loft with a small knife, one petal at a time, and dyed in three vats. Jute is woven flat and mirror work is stitched on. Terracotta tiles are baked in a kiln near Bishnupur and wired to cane backing. Paper is the cheapest and the fastest to repair.

Nothing on the skin is structural. If a panel tears on Saptami, we can take it down, replace it and re-hang it in an hour without touching the frame. That rule has saved more evenings than any drawing.

The light

Light is the last thing and the first thing the crowd notices. The approach lights are on their own circuit from the pandal lights, and the pandal lights are split into at least four zones so that a single failed strip does not black out the doorway. Everything is earthed, everything is on an RCCB, and the whole installation is signed off by a licensed electrician before the first evening. The certificate goes to the committee and a copy stays in the yard.

We use warm bulbs for the arch and cooler LED strip where the light is meant to graze a texture rather than illuminate. On a brightly lit pandal, less light is usually better: the crowd looks at what is lit, and if everything is lit, they look at nothing.

Safety, which is the whole job

Every cloth and paper surface is treated with a fire-retardant solution, and we can show the invoice for it. Exits are marked and kept clear of the frame; the crowd route is walked by the committee and by us on the evening before Puja. Structural bracing goes in at every corner and at the springing of every arch. This is not the part of the job that wins a prize, but it is the part that lets a committee sleep.

The people

Thirty-four artisans in the yard last season, most of them from Baranagar and Kamarhati, and eleven of them with us for more than a decade. A crew is four or five people: a frame man, a skin man, a light man, a painter and a helper who does the fetching and the tea. Apprentices start in the loft and on the lashings, and the ones who stay learn the whole build. Two of our foremen started as apprentices in 2012.

  • Frame and lashing: 14 artisans, led by Swapan Pal.
  • Shola and paper: 9 artisans in the loft, led by Rekha Das.
  • Cloth, dye and panels: 7 artisans, led by Anil Ghosh.
  • Light and electrical: 4 licensed hands, contracted for the season.
