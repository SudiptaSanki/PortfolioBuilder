TrainingKalamandalam · 2009–2020

Eleven years, and the first two were only feet.

I joined Kerala Kalamandalam at seventeen, after a childhood of temple festivals in the courtyard of the Koodalmanikyam temple. I had watched Kathakali from the third row for a decade and understood almost nothing. The training began by taking away everything I thought I knew about the form: the walk, the stance, the way a hand rises — all of it had a correct order, and I had it wrong.

I

The years, in the order they happened

Kalamandalam still trains in the old sequence. A student is not given a character until the body has stopped arguing with the rhythm.

  1. 2009

    Chuvadu

    Two years of basic steps on the packed earth of the kalari. Morning and evening, six days a week. The first lesson is that a step is not a movement from one place to another; it is a shape held long enough to be read from the last row of a courtyard.

  2. 2011

    Hastas and the eye

    Twenty-four hand gestures, then the eye exercises. Twenty minutes a day tracking a lamp wick from corner to corner, then in a slow circle, then while the head stays perfectly still. My teacher watched from a stool and said nothing for weeks.

  3. 2013

    First attakatha

    Kalyanasougandhikam, as one of the forest attendants. Six lines, no name, and four hours in make-up. I have never been more nervous on a stage, including every night since.

  4. 2016

    Pacha

    The first hero role: Nala, in a student production at the Koothambalam. The green took three hours and forty minutes to build, and my hands shook through the second pass of chutti.

  5. 2019

    Kathi

    Duryodhana. A teacher told me the role would teach me patience because the character has none. He was right, and it took a year to understand why.

  6. 2020

    Vidyarambham

    The final year: the full repertoire assessed over three nights, then the departure ceremony. I left with fourteen attakathas, two crowns, and a set of brushes that had been my teacher's.

Why the eyes come last

Visitors to the green room always ask about the chutti, the white ridge that turns the jaw into a mask. It is the most photogenic part of the make-up and the least interesting. The chutti is a frame. The performance happens in two places the frame points at: the eyes and the hands.

Kathakali eyes are trained before they are painted. The exercise is simple and brutal. A wick is lit and placed at one corner of the room; you track it with your eyes while the head stays still, then follow it in a circle, then in a figure of eight. After a month the eyes move independently of the neck. After a year they can express a mood before the hands begin.

The reddening comes at the very end, twenty minutes before the curtain, because the chundappu seed and castor oil sting and the eyes water. An actor who applies it too early spends the first half-hour of the performance blinking. It is one of the small, unglamorous disciplines that separate a good night from a great one.

Hands are the other half. A hasta is not a sign for a word; it is a sentence with a beginning, a hold and an end, and the hold carries the meaning. Audiences that do not speak Malayalam still follow a Kathakali scene because the gesture is allowed to sit in the air long enough to be understood. Rushing a hasta is the most common mistake in student performances, mine included.

കൈ ഉയർത്തി നിർത്തുന്നിടത്താണ് കഥ തുടങ്ങുന്നത്.

“The story begins where the hand stops rising.” — a note in my first-year notebook.
II

What travels in the trunk

A performer's kit is small, personal and repaired constantly. Almost none of it can be bought ready-made.

  • 01

    Chutti paste

    Rice flour, lime and a little gum, mixed fresh for each performance. It dries hard and comes off only with warm water and patience.

  • 02

    Two crowns

    One pacha mudi with the tall gold tier, one flatter kathi crown. Both carved in Thrissur, both repaired by the same man for twenty years.

  • 03

    Brushes

    Four squirrel-hair brushes and one flat brush for the base. My teacher's set, re-handled twice.

  • 04

    The mirror

    A hand mirror with a brass back. Held by someone else during chutti, because the ridge continues where you cannot see.

  • 05

    Red seed

    Chundappu, kept in a small tin with castor oil. Enough for a season; the tin has been refilled since 2014.

  • 06

    Thick cotton thread

    For the chutti border and for tying the crown. Never wire; wire catches the light and shows from the third row.
