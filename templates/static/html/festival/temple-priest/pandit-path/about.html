The priest

Trained in a gurukula, and still reciting the same verses.

I have been the priest at Aruloli Amman since 1994. Before that I was a boy in a gurukula twelve kilometres from here, learning the verses I now say at weddings. The verses have not changed. My understanding of them has.

Born 1966, Vadipatti Ordained 1991 Tamil · Sanskrit · English

The temple

Aruloli Amman is a small Devi temple on the Vadipatti–Usilampatti road, with a Murugan shrine at the northern corner and a tank that fills in a good monsoon. It is not a famous temple. About three hundred families treat it as their family temple, and perhaps forty of them come on an ordinary Friday. The temple's income is the hundi and a small endowment for lamps and rice; the annual account is read out in the mandap each Thai.

My grandfather was a priest here before the endowment existed, and my father after him. I did not inherit the post so much as grow into it: by the time I was ten I could light the lamps and by fourteen I could recite the evening sequence without the book. The temple committee confirmed me in 1994 after my father's health failed, and I have kept the same hours since.

The training

At eight I went to a gurukula near Usilampatti and stayed for twelve years. We rose before five, recited for two hours, ate, and recited again. I learned the Saiva Agama for temple ritual, the portions of the Vedas used at weddings and funerals, and enough Sanskrit grammar to know when a word is being used in the wrong case. I also learned to cook for forty people, which has been more useful than the grammar.

The training did not make me a scholar. It made me reliable: I can perform the same rite correctly on a Tuesday when I am tired, and that is most of the work. What I have learned since is how to explain it. A family that understands why the thread is tied will remember the rite; a family that is only watching will remember being bored.

“The mantra is fixed. The explanation is the part that has to be alive.”

The office

Bookings are handled from the house beside the temple, between seven and eleven in the morning and five and eight in the evening. My daughter Kavitha answers the phone and keeps the register; my son Madhan is in his third year of training and assists at weddings. The register has every rite since 1994: family, date, thithi, and which priest performed it. Families who have lost their own records come back for that book, and it is the most useful thing in the house.

I meet every family once before a wedding, an upanayanam or a griha pravesham. It is not a formality. In an hour I learn whether the grandmother will be offended by the seating, whether the house has a fire-safe corner, and whether the family actually wants the ceremony or is doing it for a relative. All three change how the day is run.

Languages

The mantras are in Sanskrit, because that is what the rite requires. The explanation is in Tamil. If there are children or guests who follow English better, I will explain in English as well, and I will not make anyone feel small for asking. A rite conducted in a language the family cannot follow is a performance, and I am not a performer.

What I will not do

  • Fix a muhurtam without the family's panchangam, or from an app.
  • Perform a wedding for a family I have not met, unless the date is fixed by a death in the family and the office has spoken to them.
  • Promise a particular outcome from a homam. The rite is the duty; the outcome is not mine to sell.
  • Take a booking during the temple's puja hours. The temple comes first; the office will offer the hour after.

The house beside the temple

There is a small veranda where families wait, a shelf of registers, and a brass lamp that my grandfather lit. If you come for the first meeting, come in the evening rather than the morning; the temple is quieter and there is time to sit.
