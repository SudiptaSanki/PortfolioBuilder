The two seasons

In a tropical vineyard the vine can be made to fruit twice. The October pruning gives the main crop, picked in February and March; the April pruning gives a smaller reserve crop in September. Most of what you drink from Nashik is the first.

October pruning

The cane is cut back hard in the second week of October, after the rains. Flowering comes in November, fruit set in December, and the block is ready somewhere between the middle of February and the end of March. This is the crop that carries the winery, so it gets the better rows, the thinner crop and the pickers who have done it before.

Picking is at night or before nine. By noon the fruit is at 32 °C and the fermentation starts before you have decided what you wanted it to be.

April pruning

A second, lighter pruning in April gives a reserve crop in September — useful for a sparkling base, for a rosé, and for keeping a cellar staff employed. The acid is higher and the sugar lower, which is not a fault if you are making the right thing.

Roughly a third of the yield of the main crop, and a third of the fuss.

What “ripe” means here

Not “sweet”. A grape is picked when the sugar, the acid and the tannin are in the proportion the wine needs — which is decided in the cellar before the season, not in the vineyard on the morning. I carry a refractometer and a notebook, and I taste the berries, because a number will tell you when to start looking and not when to pick.

Sauvignon Blanc for a steel tank: 21.5 to 22.5 °Brix. Shiraz for the barrel: 23.5 to 24.5. Anything above 25 and you are making something hot and jammy and calling it a style.

Why the acid is written down

Because in this heat it is the first thing to go and the last thing anyone notices. A wine at 14 per cent alcohol with 4.6 grams of acid tastes flat and people blame the variety. The same wine with 5.6 tastes alive. Acid is adjusted in the cellar only when it has to be, and never to hide a pick that was three days late.
