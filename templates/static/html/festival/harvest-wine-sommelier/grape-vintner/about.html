Twelve acres on a hot slope, and a notebook that decides the year.

Dindori is thirty kilometres north of Nashik town, on the road to Vinchur. The soil is black cotton over murum, the rain comes all at once in July and August, and from December the vine lives on a drip line and a well.

The ground

Eleven blocks, twelve acres, planted between 2009 and 2014. The lower blocks hold water longer and give the whites; the ridge is thin, hot and stony, and gives Cabernet that tastes like it came from somewhere. The rows run north to south so the afternoon sun hits the fruit evenly, which sounds like a detail and is in fact the difference between a ripe block and a cooked one.

We irrigate with drip from a borewell and one open well that has never gone dry in my father-in-law’s memory. From December the water is rationed by block — a stressed vine makes smaller berries and better wine, and a comfortable vine makes a lot of pleasant nothing.

Two prunings, two harvests

The first cut is in October, after the rain has gone. The vine pushes new canes, flowers in November, sets fruit in December, and by the middle of February the first blocks are ready. The second cut, in April, is lighter; it gives a small crop in September that we use for a sparkling base and a rosé.

“The calendar in a Nashik vineyard is not the European one. February is our September, and the heat is the vintage.”

That second crop is the reason the cellar keeps working through the year, and the reason a September pick can taste like a different wine from the same row six months earlier. Higher acid, lower sugar, less colour — which is either a problem or a tool, depending on what you do with it.

How I came to it

I studied microbiology in Pune and came here in 2011 for a season in the lab, measuring yeast populations and washing tanks. I stayed because the vineyard was more interesting than the lab, and because the man who was making the wine at the time let me taste every barrel and told me the truth about half of them.

Since 2017 I have run the harvest: the picking decisions, the picker crews, the fruit coming in at night, and the first three days of every fermentation. In 2023 I spent a vintage in South Australia, which taught me that our heat is not their heat and that our acid problem is real. I came back with a refractometer I already had and a stronger opinion about picking early.

Six vintages, briefly

What the year did, not what the wine scored. The scores change with the taster; the rain does not.

The years

  1. 2026

    A dry January and a hot February. Picking started on 18 February, ten days earlier than 2025, and the whites came in at 6.4 g/L acid — the best balance since 2019.

  2. 2025

    Rain in the first week of March, right on the Shiraz. We picked half the block early and lost the rest to rot. The Cabernet saved the year.

  3. 2024

    Cool December, slow ripening, everything two weeks late. The Chenin is the wine I would still open for someone who thinks Indian white cannot age.

  4. 2023

    My first year running the harvest. I picked the Sauvignon three days too late and spent the rest of the season learning why acid matters more than flavour.

  5. 2022

    A big crop, a wet September, and a reserve harvest that never dried out. Most of it went to a bulk buyer and I am not sorry.

  6. 2021

    The first year we kept a separate barrel of Shiraz from the ridge. Six barrels, sixty bottles a barrel, all of it gone by 2024.

What we do in the cellar

Whites in stainless at 14 °C, reds in open-top fermenters with two punch-downs a day, then into French oak — a third new, never more. Malolactic only on the reds. Nothing is fined unless it refuses to clear, and nothing is filtered except the rosé, because a cloudy rosé in a Nashik summer is a bottle that will not survive the drive to Mumbai.

Who picks

The same twelve families from the village for nine years, plus whoever is available in February. They are paid by the day, fed twice, and given the fruit they pick for their own houses — a crate each at the end of the block, which is the arrangement everyone is actually happy with.

“Pick at night, press by ten, and never let the fruit sit in the sun while you finish your coffee.”

Aditi Kulkarni, note to the picking crew, 18 February 2026
