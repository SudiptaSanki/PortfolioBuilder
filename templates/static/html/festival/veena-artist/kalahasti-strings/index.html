The instrument

Older than the player, and it knows it

A Thanjavur veena is a single piece of jackwood, two gourds, twenty-four fixed brass frets and four strings. It is heavy, it does not forgive a cold morning, and it makes a sound no synthesiser has managed to copy.

It was made in 1894 in a workshop near the Big Temple and came to my guru in 1961, already old, already repaired twice. When he handed it to me in 2014 he said the veena would outlast both of us and that I should stop worrying about the scratches.

The strings are tuned to the tonic the hall can carry. In a small room I sit at C; in a large sabha, D, because a higher tonic reaches the back rows without anyone turning a microphone up.

What the four strings do

  • The first three carry the melody: Sa, Pa, Sa above, tuned in fifths and octaves.
  • The fourth is the drone string, tuned to Pa, plucked now and then to keep the tonic in the room's ear.
  • The frets are fixed, so every gamaka has to come from the fingers, not the fretboard.
  • The gourd is not decoration. It is the reason a single pluck can fill a courtyard.

I play in the Thanjavur bani: long phrases, kampita on the important notes, and no ornament that exists only to show off. The raga decides the tempo. Most of the time it asks for slower than the player wants.

Where it is heard

Four rooms, four different veenas

The same instrument behaves differently in a sabha with a wooden roof, a stone temple mandapam, a treated studio and a living room with a low ceiling. I tune for the room, not for the tuner.
