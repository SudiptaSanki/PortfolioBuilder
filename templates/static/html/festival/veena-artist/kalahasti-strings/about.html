About

Twenty-two years, and still the same first exercise

I began with sarali varisai in a front room in Srikalahasti at eight, and I still play it every morning. Everything else I know sits on top of those seven notes.

The training

A guru, a wooden bench, and no shortcuts

4 hrsPractice a day, before anything else
25Concerts in a normal year
14Students, from nine years up

My grandmother sang in the temple at Srikalahasti, and the first music I heard was not a recording but a voice in a stone corridor. I was eight when my father took me to K. Subrahmanyam's house with a notebook and a bag of oranges, and asked whether he would teach me.

He taught for ten years before he let me play a full kriti in front of anyone. The first four years were sarali varisai, jantai varisai and alankaras, and nothing else. When I complained, he said the veena has twenty-four frets and a lifetime, and the two should not be in a hurry with each other.

In 2014 he handed me the 1894 veena he had played since 1961, and told me not to have it re-fretted until it truly needed it. I waited five years. The new frets went in on a wax bed in 2019, and the instrument came back louder and slightly strange for a month, which he would have found funny.

The bani

I play in the Thanjavur bani, which means long phrases, a preference for kampita over fast brighas, and a habit of letting a note sit until the room has heard it. It is not the showiest style and I have never wanted it to be.

My guru died in 2016. The first concert after that was in the same temple courtyard where my grandmother sang, and I played Kalyani badly for the first ten minutes, and then the instrument took over, which is the only reason I finished.

Teaching

Fourteen students now, most of them on weekday evenings, two of them online because they live in Bangalore and Muscat. I teach the way I was taught: exercises first, kritis when the fingers can carry them, and no recording of the lesson until the student can hear their own mistake without being told.

The years

What happened, in order

  1. 2004
    First lesson

    Sarali varisai in the front room of K. Subrahmanyam's house, with a notebook and no instrument of my own for the first year.

  2. 2009
    First small concert

    Twenty minutes in the temple mandapam at Srikalahasti, in front of an audience that was mostly relatives and one very patient priest.

  3. 2012
    First sabha

    A morning slot in Tirupati, with a mridangam player who had been playing since before I was born.

  4. 2014
    The 1894 veena

    Handed over on a Tuesday with the instruction to stop worrying about the scratches.

  5. 2016
    Guru passes

    K. Subrahmanyam dies in March. The first concert without him is in the temple courtyard that June.

  6. 2018
    All India Radio

    Graded artist at AIR Tirupati, which means a certain number of recordings a year and a producer who tells you to play shorter.

  7. 2024
    First film session

    Two days in a Chennai studio recording veena for a documentary score, reading to picture for the first time.

The morning

What happens before any concert

None of this is ritual for its own sake. The instrument is wood and gut and brass, and it needs an hour of attention before it agrees to be played.

01

Warm the hands

Ten minutes of stretching and slow sarali varisai in the lowest octave, with the strings tuned a little flat so nothing is strained.

Before sunrise, most days

02

Tune to the room

Sa at C in a small room, D in a hall. The drone string is checked against the first string until the beating disappears.

Twenty minutes before the hall fills

03

Choose the raga

The hour and the season decide it. Kalyani in the evening, Hindolam when the audience is tired, Mohanam when the hall is cold.

Never announced in advance

04

Play the first phrase

Slow, unaccompanied, and long enough that the room stops talking. Everything after that is easier.

The only part I still get nervous about

If there is a room and a date, I will bring the veena
