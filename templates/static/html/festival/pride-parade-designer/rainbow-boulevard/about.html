The shop · Lalbaug

A mandal shed,
two seasons a yearEleven months of the year it smells of plaster and Ganesh paint. In December it fills with plywood, bamboo and bolts of vinyl, and the floats begin.

The workshop belongs to the Lalbaug Sarvajanik Utsav Mandal. I rent it from them from the first week of December to the end of February, and I sweep it before I leave — which they have told me twice is not necessary, and which I do anyway.

Jules Ferreira

Born in Assagao, Goa, 1987. Trained as a set painter at Film City, then spent four years building Ganesh mandal sets in Lalbaug before anyone asked for a parade float.

They/them. Hindi, Marathi, Konkani, English.

How I got here

My first float was a mistake. In 2017 a friend who helped organise Mumbai's march asked whether I could make "something small" for a group of trans women who had been told they could not walk at the front with their banner because the banner was too wide. I had been painting sets for eleven years. I said yes before I asked what small meant.

Small meant a hand-pulled cart with a canopy of sewn saris, a bench for four, and a crown that had to come off to get through a gate in Byculla. We built it in nine days, in this shed, with four people and a borrowed staple gun. It came apart twice on the route. We got it to the end anyway, and the photograph of those four women on that cart is the reason I still do this.

Since then it has been 62 floats across 14 parades — big city marches and first-time marches in towns where the permission letter took longer than the build. The work is the same in both places: measure the street, draw to the street, and build the thing so it can be repaired by someone who is not me, at the roadside, in the heat.

A float is not a stage set. A stage set has to look right from the audience. A float has to look right from the pavement, at walking pace, through a crowd, for four hours, and still be safe when one of the wheels drops into a drain.

Three rules the shop works by

One. The route decides, not the drawing.

I have never regretted walking a route twice. I have regretted trusting a client's estimate of a bridge height. The drawings in this portfolio are elevations because that is the view that tells you whether the thing will physically pass down the road.

Two. Repairable beats beautiful.

Every panel is bolted, not glued. Every cloth edge is sewn twice. The paint is tractor enamel because it dries in an hour and can be touched up with a brush in a moving crowd. If your float needs a workshop to survive the march, it is the wrong float.

Three. The community group is the client, not the sponsor.

Where a brand is paying, the brand gets a logo on the tow vehicle and a line in the programme. It does not get to redesign the message, choose who stands on the deck, or put its name on the canopy instead of the group's. That has cost me two jobs. Both times the group found the money elsewhere.

What I will not build

  • A float for a company that has no queer staff in the room where the float is being decided. The float is not the hire.
  • Anything that puts people in costume on an open deck above 2.4 m without a rail at 1.1 m and a harness point. No exceptions for photographs.
  • A "surprise" float for a march the organisers have not been told about. The traffic branch finds out either way, and it is the organisers who pay.
  • Sound above 96 dB at the float, or amplified sound before 09:00 in a residential stretch. I bring the meter and I write the quiet hour into the permit.

Parades and credits

  1. 2025

    Namma Pride, Bengaluru — two floatsFloat 57 for Sangama's walking contingent; Float 58, the 9.4 m trailer with the unbolting crown. Built in 16 days in a rented shed in Yeshwanthpur.

  2. 2024

    Mumbai Queer Azadi March — lead floatA 7.2 m flatbed with a 26 m hand-sewn cloth banner carried by 40 walkers. The banner now hangs in the office of a community legal aid trust.

  3. 2023

    Kochi Queer Pride — first floatHand-pulled cart, three and a half metres, built in a garage in Fort Kochi. The tightest route I have ever worked: a 5.6 m carriageway and a 5.4 m turn at Princess Street.

  4. 2022

    Nashik and Thrissur — first marches in both citiesOne cart each, built to fit a tempo van, assembled on the morning. Both marched. Both had their permission letter delayed until the previous evening.

  5. 2019

    Set painting, Film CityFour years of sets, then two years of Ganesh mandal work in Lalbaug. Everything I know about building light and fast comes from here.

Working with the shop

Bring the date, the street and the number of people

Everything else — the shape, the cloth, the tow — follows from those three things. If you do not know them yet, bring what you do know and I will tell you what to find out.
