ROUTE

I studied fine art in Pune, which taught me to draw a figure and nothing about deadlines. In 2014 I took a job at a mobile studio in Baner drawing backgrounds for a match-three game — flowers, mostly, and a castle that had to look expensive at 480 pixels wide. I drew that castle for three years.

The job was not wasted. A background artist learns composition and value before detail, because nobody looks at a background for more than a second. That is exactly the discipline a 320×180 screen demands. When I left in 2017, I could already make a room read at a glance; I just had to learn to do it with sixteen colours and no gradients.

The first game I finished on my own was a jam entry in 2019: a 64×64 room where a woman waits for a letter that never comes. It took nine days and taught me that a game is a clock, not a painting. I have been building small clocks since.

WHY THE EPICS

I grew up with two picture traditions: the Amar Chitra Katha panels my mother kept in a steel trunk, and the 16-bit games I played at a cousin's house on a cartridge that had to be blown into. Both of them tell a story in a grid. Both use flat colour, a strong outline and a fixed palette. The overlap is not a coincidence; it is a technology.

The Mahabharata and the Ramayana have been drawn a thousand times, but almost never at the scale where a single gesture has to carry the scene. A 32×32 sprite cannot show you a face; it can show you a decision. I find that honest.

Reference shelf: the BORI critical edition of the Mahabharata (translations by M. A. Mehendale and others), K. G. Subramanyan's writings on Indian visual narrative, and a folder of photographs of Kalighat pat paintings taken at the Indian Museum.

THE CONSTRAINT

Sixteen colours, two of them for the interface. Heroes at 32×32, enemies at 24×24, tiles at 16×16. One screen per room. No parallax unless the room is outside. No text that cannot be read at 3× scale. A palette that survives being photographed off a CRT.

I break one constraint per project, deliberately, and write down why. On Vanara it was a vertical scrolling room — a climb that is taller than the screen. On Karna it is a wheel that rotates on a fixed 8-frame loop, which means the chariot never turns smoothly and that is the point: the wheel is the character.

WHAT I WILL NOT DRAW

The epics are living texts. People argue about them in kitchens and in courtrooms. So: no caste or community drawn as a cartoon, no divine figure used as a boss monster for shock, no “ancient mystery” framing that turns a practice into set dressing. If a scene depends on a real ritual, I ask someone who performs it and I credit them. If I cannot find that person, the scene does not go in.

Consulted on Vanara: Dr. Shubha Kulkarni (Sanskrit, SPPU) and the Kulkarni family in Saswad, who have pulled the Ganesh chariot for four generations.
