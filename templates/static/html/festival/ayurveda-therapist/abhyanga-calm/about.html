About the practice

Oil, wood, steam
and a borrowed clock.

The room is a converted godown behind Kunnumpuram, with a red laterite floor and a ceiling high enough that the steam goes somewhere. Everything else in this practice is an argument about time.

01How I came to it

I did not grow up wanting to be a vaidya

My mother ran a school for Kathakali make-up in Tripunithura, and the first oils I knew were the ones in her box — burnt coconut shell, red lead, a paste that took three soap nuts to remove. I went to Madras to study physiotherapy, worked two years in a sports clinic in Chennai, and kept meeting the same patient: a healthy person in his forties with one shoulder, one knee or one sleep that had quietly stopped working.

So I went back to Thaikkattussery, near Ollur, and did the five-and-a-half-year BAMS course at the Vaidyaratnam college, then the clinical residency in panchakarma. That is the formal part. The part I use every morning came from my teacher's mother-in-law, who had no certificates at all and could tell from the way a person sat down whether the oil should be sesame or coconut before she ever took a pulse.

I have practised in Fort Kochi since 2009. For the first six years I shared a room with a dentist; the drill was a useful reminder that a clinic is a workshop, not a temple. Since 2016 the practice has been mine, two treatment slots a day, five days a week.

“Abhyanga is not a massage with better branding. It is a measured preparation of the body for something else — steam, purge, rest. If nothing follows it, you have had a pleasant hour, not a treatment.”

02Training & registration

The paper trail

  1. 2000–04
    B.Sc. Physiotherapy, Sri Ramachandra, Chennai

    Two years in a sports injury clinic afterwards. I still use the orthopaedic assessment every day; I stopped using the ultrasound machine in 2011.

  2. 2005–10
    BAMS, Vaidyaratnam Ayurveda College, Thaikkattussery

    Five and a half years, six of them on the panchakarma ward under Dr K. R. Nambisan, where the treatments were done at scale and the failures were as instructive as the successes.

  3. 2011–15
    House surgeon and junior vaidya, Ollur

    Amavata, gridhrasi, and a great many post-partum cases. I learned to write down what I had actually done, not what the textbook said.

  4. 2016
    Practice opened at Kunnumpuram

    Two rooms, one steam box, the dentist's old chair sold to a barber in Mattancherry.

  5. 2019
    Registered practitioner, Kerala Board of Indian Medicine

    Registration no. 18422. Renewed every five years. Certificates are on the wall by the door, next to the fire extinguisher.

  6. 2022
    Certificate in marma chikitsa, Kottakkal

    One hundred and seven points, six months, and a humbling amount of anatomy I thought I already knew.

03The materials

What is actually in the room

i. Wood

The droni is jackwood, 2.1 metres, with a channel cut down the middle so the oil leaves rather than pooling under your back. It is wiped with a dry cloth after every treatment and oiled with coconut once a year. No vinyl, no foam, no heated table.

ii. Cloth

Cotton, white, boiled after each use. Four towels per treatment: one under the head, one for the oil, one for the steam, one warm and dry at the end. If you see a paper sheet in a Kerala treatment room, ask what happened to the laundry.

iii. Oil

Pressed at a mill in Vadanappally, decanted into amber glass, and warmed in a steel bowl set in hot water — never in a microwave, which heats unevenly and smells of the last thing cooked in it.

What this practice will not do: it will not treat a suspected fracture, a fever above 38.5 °C, an unexplained lump, chest pain or a sudden weakness on one side. It will not sell you a course of ten treatments before it has seen how your body answers two. And it will not tell you to stop a medicine your physician prescribed — I will write to the physician instead.

04A working day

Two patients, four hours, one pot of kanji

This is not a spa schedule. The gaps are as deliberate as the appointments.

  1. 05:45

    The room warms

    Steam box on, oil bowls set in water, windows open for twenty minutes before the fans go on. I sweep the laterite myself; it tells me whether the floor is damp.

  2. 07:00

    First patient

    Ninety minutes. If it is a first visit, forty of those are conversation and the pulse.

  3. 09:30

    The gap

    Laundry, notes, a second pot of water. Notes are written by hand while the oil is still in my memory of the shoulder; the digital copy is typed at four.

  4. 12:00

    Second patient

    Usually a course visit — day four of seven, or a post-partum week.

  5. 15:00

    Consultations and letters

    New enquiries, replies to physicians, and the pharmacy order. Nothing with oil is booked after four, because the body needs daylight left to come back to itself.

  6. 17:00

    Out

    Door locked. Wednesdays I cook; the rest of the week I walk the beach road and am asleep by ten.
