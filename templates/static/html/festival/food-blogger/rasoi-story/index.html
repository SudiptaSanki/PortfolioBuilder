The everyday, not the festive

I cook for four people,
most nights, in a kitchen
with one window.

This is a record of what actually gets made in a Matunga flat: amti on a Tuesday, bisi bele bath when the rice is old, poha on the morning after a late shift. No restaurant tricks, no ingredient you have to order from a website, and the family notes in the margins kept where they belong.

Amtithe Tuesday dal

  1. 180 g toor dal, soaked 30 min
  2. 30 g tamarind pulp
  3. 25 g jaggery, grated
  4. 2 tsp goda masala
  5. ½ tsp turmeric
  6. 15 g ghee

Boil the dal with turmeric until it collapses, then add tamarind and jaggery and cook another ten minutes. The goda masala goes in last, off the heat — my mother's only firm rule.

22 minutes · one pressure cooker · ₹48 for four

From the notebook

On not washing poha

Written 9 October 2026, after a second cup of tea

The first thing anyone tells you about poha is to wash it. This is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that ruins breakfast: the flakes take water at the edges, go soft in patches, and then break when you stir them. What you want is a colander, a light hand, and a sprinkling — a quarter cup of water for two hundred grams of poha, tossed through and left to stand while the onions fry.

My grandmother kept her poha in a steel tin with a piece of dried red chilli at the bottom, which she claimed kept the weevils out and which I have never tested because I have never had weevils. What I have had is poha that was too thin, which is a different problem: the thick variety from the Kannada shop near King's Circle, not the papery one in the plastic packet.

There is a version of this recipe in every household on my street and none of them agree on the peanuts. My neighbour fries them first and keeps them aside; I fry them in the same oil after the mustard and let them soften, which is considered lazy. Both are correct. The thing that is not correct is the sugar — a teaspoon, no more, and only if your lemon is sharp.

— A.
