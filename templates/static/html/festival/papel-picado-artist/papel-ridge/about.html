The craft

The cut is the drawing, and the stack is the trick.

Papel picado is not paper with a picture printed on it. It is a sheet with holes cut through it, forty at a time, so that the light and the wall behind become part of the design. Everything else in this page follows from that fact.

The town

San Salvador Huixcolotla is a small town east of Puebla, and it has been the centre of Mexican papel picado for a hundred years. My great-grandfather sold paper cut by hand at the plaza; my grandfather, Aurelio, was one of the men who turned it into a trade with a workshop and a pattern book. In the 1960s there were forty families cutting on this street. There are six now, and three of them are on our block.

What the town has is not a secret technique but a habit: everyone here has held a chisel since they were children, and the patterns are passed by photocopy and by eye. I learned on a stack of newspaper at seven, cutting the same diamond over and over until my hand stopped shaking.

The stack

A banner is cut from a stack of forty 50 by 35 centimetre sheets, folded together and fastened at the corners. The design is drawn on the top sheet in pencil, then cut through the whole stack with a chisel and a hammer: one strike, forty identical holes. The chisels are short, sharpened on a stone every morning, and come in about a dozen profiles — round, square, diamond, heart, and three sizes of straight blade for the borders.

The stack is the reason a handmade banner is affordable and the reason no two are exactly alike. The sheets shift by a fraction of a millimetre under the chisel, so the holes breathe from pennant to pennant. If you look closely at a run, you can see the hand in it. A machine can do it more exactly; it cannot do it better.

“The paper is the drawing. What you take out of it is the picture.”

The motifs

The traditional vocabulary is small and it is enough: calaveras, cempasúchil, doves, stars, milagros, nopales, and the names of the people the banner is for. I draw a new pattern for each commission and keep a copy in the pattern book, which now has 214 designs, the oldest from my grandfather's hand in 1963.

Letters are the hardest. A name cut into a border has to be legible at a distance while still holding the paper together: cut too much and the border tears in the wind, cut too little and the letters close up. I usually cut names at 60 millimetres tall and leave a solid band of paper above and below.

The paper

Chinese tissue, 40 gram, in the standard colours: rosa mexicano, naranja, morado, verde, azul and amarillo. For outdoor runs we use 80 gram, and for the rain, a thin plastic sheet that cuts like paper but lasts a season. The paper comes from a mill in San Luis Potosí in reams of 500; in October the taller gets through eleven reams a week.

Nothing is wasted. Offcuts become small stars for the workshop table, and the failed cuts become firelighters for the comal. My mother would not have allowed anything else.

The taller today

There are four of us at the table: me, my brother Beto, my aunt Rosa, and Lucía, who came as a student in 2019 and stayed. We cut on Monday to Friday and teach on Saturday. In season we work to a list on the wall, one commission at a time, because a stack cannot be left half cut overnight without the sheets shifting.

The shop front is still the front room of the house, with the table by the window and the chisels on a rail above it. Visitors are welcome on Saturdays between ten and two, which is also when the workshop runs. Come and cut a star; it takes ten minutes and it will change how you look at a banner.

What we will not do

  • Print a photograph on paper and call it papel picado.
  • Send a design to a laser cutter and pass it off as hand-cut.
  • Promise a colour that the paper mill does not make that season.
  • Take an ofrenda order after 20 October. The stack is full, and the dead do not wait for a late delivery.
