Full concert · January 2026

Marwa into the small hours — Dover Lane Music Conference

The ask. The conference's late slot, the one that starts when the day's other stages have emptied, and a single raga with no interval. The organisers wanted a programme that would hold the serious listeners — the ones who come to Dover Lane precisely for the last concert of the night.

The playing. Marwa, ninety-five minutes: a forty-minute alap in near-darkness, jor and jhala as the hall settled, and a gat in a slow twelve-beat ektal that stretched the final tihai across three full cycles. Tabla and tanpura from the regular bench; two microphones, kept low, at Arjun's insistence — agreed with the hall's engineer before the season opened, not argued about on the night.

The outcome. The hall stayed full past midnight, the conference has booked the same slot for the coming January, and the recording the organisers made for their archive is the one they now send to artists they are considering for late slots — as a warning as much as an invitation.

“He held a full hall with one raga and no theatrics. The silence before the last tihai was the loudest thing I heard all week.”
— Samarjit Bose, programme secretary, Dover Lane Music Conference

Baithak · November 2025

Forty chairs, no microphones — a Jodhpur Park baithak

The ask. A private baithak series in a Jodhpur Park home, running for eleven years on one rule: the music is never amplified, and there are never more listeners than the room can seat on the floor. The host asked for two ragas across the sunset, and for the audience to be close enough to hear the instrument breathe.

The playing. Bhimpalasi as the light went, Bageshri after the lamps — seventy-five minutes in total, seated on the floor among the listeners, tanpura only for the first half and tabla joining quietly for the gat. No stage, no backdrop, a window open to the street sounds of the para, which the alap simply played around.

The outcome. The series rebooked for the following winter before the evening ended, and three of the forty listeners wrote afterwards to ask about lessons — two are now among the six students.

“You could hear the wire of the mizrab on the string. My father, who hosted baithaks in this house in the seventies, said it was the first one since then that sounded like his.”
— Rina Chatterjee, host, Jodhpur Park baithak series

Festival set · December 2025

Yaman in the open air — Poush Mela, Santiniketan

The ask. A forty-minute festival set on the open-air stage at Poush Mela, timed to begin at dusk — the organisers wanted the raga of the first night played as night actually fell, to an audience of fair-goers rather than concert-goers.

The playing. Yaman, compressed by the festival proportions — a short alap, the gat carrying the raga early — to roughly two thousand people standing in winter dust. Twelve minutes in, a main playing string snapped in the cold; the set continued on the second instrument while the first was restrung side-stage, and the gat, when it came, was clapped into time by a crowd that had decided to stay.

The outcome. The organisers moved the slot earlier in the programme for the next year — not shorter, earlier, so the set ends in full dark — and the restrung-in-public moment is now part of how the instrument-care talk on the About page gets told to students: a string change is part of the craft, not an accident outside it.

“Most artists would have stopped. He changed the instrument like a man changing a tyre — calmly, in front of everyone — and the crowd loved him for it.”
— Pradip Hansda, stage manager, Poush Mela open-air stage

Lecture-demonstration · September 2025

The anatomy of an alap — Bengaluru

The ask. A music school in Bengaluru asked for the opposite of a concert: ninety minutes in which one alap is taken apart in public — every phrase named, every decision explained, students allowed to interrupt with questions at any point.

The playing. Puriya, slowed down to teaching speed. The meend demonstrated first as a slide and then as the grammar it actually is; the school's advanced students took turns holding the tanpura, and the final twenty minutes were given to a student playing her own alap while Arjun commented — kindly, and only where it mattered.

The outcome. The school has made it an annual return, and the session's structure — demonstrate, name, hand over the instrument — is now how the first month of lessons runs for Arjun's own six students in Kolkata.

“Our students had heard alaps for years. That morning, for the first time, they could see one being built — and two of them went home and rebuilt their own practice around it.”
— K. V. Anand, head of the music school, Bengaluru
