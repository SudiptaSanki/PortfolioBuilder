Sitar · Kolkata · Evening ragas

The sitar at the hour it was made for.

I am a sitar player in Kolkata, and I play the evening ragas — Marwa as the light goes, Yaman just after, Malkauns when a baithak runs past midnight. Every January I play the Dover Lane Music Conference, the stage I grew up listening to from the cheap seats; the rest of the year is baithaks, halls and festival sets, each one played whole.

A twenty-minute Yaman is a trailer, not a film. I would rather play one raga properly than four of them politely.

Repertoire

Six ragas, each at its hour

Tradition assigns every raga an hour of the day, and I keep to the assignment — not from superstition, but because listeners feel the difference even when they cannot name it. These six are the core of the current season; a full concert draws on one, a baithak sometimes on two.

Marwa

Dusk

Restless and sunless — it leaves out the fifth entirely and leans on a sharpened fourth, so the melody never quite sits down. Played as the light goes, before the lamps come up.

Puriya

Early evening

Grave and inward, built on the same skeleton as Marwa but with a different gait. The raga I open full concerts with when the hall is a listening hall.

Yaman

First of the night

Bright, open, architectural — the first raga most students learn and the one most often butchered by abbreviation. Given its full alap, it is a complete evening by itself.

Bhimpalasi

Late afternoon

The afternoon leaning toward evening — tender, unhurried, a raga for rooms with windows. A baithak favourite in winter, when it ends almost exactly at sunset.

Bageshri

Late evening

A raga of longing that resolves late and quietly. Often the second half of a baithak, when the room has settled and nobody is checking the time.

Malkauns

Midnight

Five notes, no shortcuts, played only when the night is genuinely deep. If a baithak ends before eleven, Malkauns stays in the case.

How an evening is built

Alap, jor, jhala, gat

A concert is not a set list. It is one raga unfolded in four movements, and the proportions below are the ones printed in the planner — the same numbers your evening will be costed and timed by.

Alap

40%

The raga introduced note by note, unmetered, the sitar alone against the tanpura. This is where the audience learns the raga's grammar — rush it and the rest is decoration.

Jor

20%

A pulse enters — a steady, walking rhythm in the playing itself, still with no drum. The melody starts to travel instead of hover.

Jhala

10%

The bright, fast close of the unmetered half: the melody string struck against the shimmering drone strings. Short, because it is a crest, not a plateau.

Gat

30%

A fixed composition set in a tala cycle, where the tabla joins and the improvisation turns rhythmic — question and answer, acceleration, and a landing both players agree on.

Festival sets run tighter — alap 25%, jor 15%, jhala 10%, gat 50% — because a festival audience arrives mid-stream and the gat has to carry the raga sooner.

Recent concerts

Three evenings, as they happened

Dover Lane Music Conference · Kolkata · January 2026

Marwa into the small hours

The conference asked for the late slot and a single raga. Marwa, ninety-five minutes, with the gat in a slow twelve-beat cycle — and a hall that stayed past midnight to hear it land.

“He held a full hall with one raga and no theatrics. The silence before the last tihai was the loudest thing I heard all week.”
— Samarjit Bose, programme secretary, Dover Lane Music Conference

Jodhpur Park baithak series · Kolkata · November 2025

Forty chairs, no microphones

A private series in a Jodhpur Park home asked for a baithak the old way: floor seating, no amplification, two ragas. Bhimpalasi into Bageshri, seventy-five minutes, close enough to hear the mizrab on the string.

Poush Mela · Santiniketan · December 2025

Yaman in the open air

An open-air festival stage at dusk — wind, dust, two thousand people and one string that gave up mid-alap. The set finished on a restrung instrument and a gat the crowd clapped into time.
