The room

Recorded in a quarter
with a tin roof,
between 1 and 5 a.m.

Whisper Reel is made by one person in one room in Ballygunge. The room used to be the servant's quarter of a house built in 1938; it has a tin roof, one window, and a ceiling fan that I switch off before every take.

My name is Ira Bose. For eleven years I recorded sound for documentary films — long interviews in bad rooms, monsoon streets, a factory floor in Howrah at four in the morning. The part of the job nobody asks about is the waiting: you sit still with headphones on and you listen to a building be empty. That is the skill this podcast is made of.

In 2021 I started recording the noises in my own house, because I had begun to hear them as a story. The tap in the courtyard that runs for three minutes at 2 a.m. The neighbour's gate that opens twice but only one person walks. My mother said I was being foolish and then told me about the landing at her childhood house in Shyambazar, which is now episode three.

Every episode is built from real recordings and a written story. I never use a scream as a jump scare, and I never add a sound that a listener could not have heard in the same room. If a story is a family's private grief, I ask before recording it, and I change the names even when they say not to.

What is in the room

One condenser microphone on a stand borrowed from a friend who does location sound. A laptop with a fan that I have to put a cushion over. The Nagra in the photograph on the front page does not belong to me; it belongs to the friend, and it only appears when the roof noise is too much for the condenser.

The tin roof is the most useful thing in the studio. In June it gives me rain without a rain file. In December it contracts at about 4 a.m. with a sound like a chair being pulled out, which I have used in four episodes and which is real every time.

Why there is no music

Music tells you how to feel, and the moment it arrives the listener stops listening for the room. The podcast works because you are not sure which sound is the story and which is the house. Put a drone under it and the house stops mattering.

The exception is the cue deck on the front page. That is not scoring — it is the actual desk, with the actual channels, so you can hear how a scene is built from nothing but room tone and a floorboard. Nothing in it is a recording of anything: it is all synthesised in the browser, which is why it waits for you to arm it.

Two rules I do not break. No real person is named without permission, even when the story is fifty years old. And no episode ends with an explanation — if I have explained it, I have cut the wrong ending.

The rules of the room

Record the silence first

Eight seconds of the empty room at the head of every file. It becomes the bed under the whole episode at minus fourteen decibels. Cut it and the story sounds pasted onto nothing.

One impossible thing

The story is allowed one detail that cannot be explained. A second one turns it into a monster film. I take the second one out in the edit even when the person telling it insists.

Never scare the listener twice

One sudden sound per episode, at most, and it is always something that was already in the room. The rest of the fear has to come from what is being described, not from the mix.

I don't listen for the ghost any more. I listen for the room. That is what these episodes taught me — that a house is a machine for making noise, and some of the noises have not been accounted for.
