The bench in Gali no. 4

Three generations, one flame, and a shop that has not moved fifty metres in fifty years. The front is the counter; the back is where the lac is warmed.

It started with a cart outside the Charminar

Mohammed Yousuf came to Hyderabad from Alwar in 1969 with a wooden box of lac sticks and a mandrel. He sold bangles from a cart near the Charminar for five years, and in 1974 took the lease on the shop in Gali no. 4 — a room the width of two arms, with a counter that still has his burn marks on it.

His daughter Zohra took over the flame in 1998. She had been winding bangles since she was eleven, sitting on the floor beside him after school, and she is the one who introduced the mirror inlay that the shop is now known for. Her son Sameer joined in 2016 and runs the counter, the orders and the courier.

The shop has never had a second branch. It sells wholesale to four shops in the old city and retail to anyone who walks in, and those two things are the same bangle at two prices.

Six people at the bench, two of them apprentices, and a flame that is lit at eleven and put out at half past eight.

The apprentices are taught in the order the work happens: winding for a year, joining for a year, colour after that. Nobody touches a bridal order in their first two years.

A DAY AT THE BENCH

Eleven to half past eight

The counter is open all day, but the good work happens in the morning when the air is cool and the lac behaves.

  1. 11.00

    The flame is lit

    The first hour is repairs and re-polishing for customers who came yesterday. Nothing new is started until the bench is clear.

  2. 12.30

    Winding

    Zohra winds and joins. Two apprentices sit beside her, rolling seams with a palm full of oil. The radio is on and nobody talks much.

  3. 15.00

    The counter fills

    Laad Bazaar is busiest in the afternoon. Sameer handles the shop while the bench keeps working; brides come with photographs, mothers come with old bangles to match.

  4. 17.30

    Inlay

    Mirror and foil work, which needs the steadiest hands of the day and the brightest light. The shop door is propped open.

  5. 20.00

    Sizing and closing

    The gauge comes out for tomorrow's orders. The flame is put out, the lac put away in its box, and the floor swept — lac dust is swept every night because it never sets.

What we make, and what we refuse

Everyday stacks of five to seven, bridal sets of nine to twenty-one, single kadas, and colour matching for a specific outfit. We also repair chips and re-polish any bangle bought here, free in the first year and for a small charge after.

We do not make plastic, glass or metal bangles under this name, and we do not copy another workshop's design from a photograph. If a bride brings a picture of a stack made elsewhere, we will read the colours and make our own version of them, and we will say that is what we are doing.

Caring for lac

Lac softens at about 50°C, which is cooler than a car dashboard in May. Keep bangles away from direct sun, hot water, the stove and the hair dryer, and take them off before washing your hands if you can be bothered. A bangle that has softened and lost its shape can be re-wound once; a bangle that has cracked cannot be glued back to anything worth wearing.

The sizing gauge

The gauge is a wooden cone with five marks on it, and it is the most important object in the shop. A bangle that is a millimetre too small will go on and not come off; one that is a millimetre too large will turn on the wrist and drive the wearer mad within an hour.

We measure the hand across the knuckles with the fingers together, then add two millimetres for the seam and the polish. The size written on the bill is the one the gauge says, not the one the customer asks for — and if a bride insists on a 2.4 when the hand measures 2.6, Zohra will wind it, watch her try it on, and wind it again.

Trade, and the price of a bangle

An everyday bangle is ₹650. A five-bangle stack, made and fitted, is ₹3,250 to ₹4,500 depending on the inlay. A bridal stack of twenty-one begins at ₹18,000 and rarely stops there, because colour matching to a Benarasi border takes time and the silk has to be seen in daylight.

Wholesale is a different trade and a different price: four shops in the old city buy by the dozen, and their bangles leave the shop unpolished and unsized. We do not sell wholesale online and we do not drop-ship.

“The colour is the easy part. The fit is the work.”

A bangle is tried on the gauge three times: after winding, after colour, and after polish. If it fails the third time it is re-wound, not sold at a discount.
