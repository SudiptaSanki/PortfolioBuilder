Method · eight steps

Bread is a schedule before it is a recipe.

Challah is a lean-enough dough with a rich enough crumb that everything shows: an under-weighed flour, a warm retard, a braid pulled too tight. The method below is the one I teach, written for a home oven and a Thursday evening. It makes one 1.2 kg loaf or two smaller ones.

Where it started

My grandmother braided on the kitchen table, not a bench.

My grandmother, Rivka, came to Montreal from Casablanca in 1961 and baked in a fourth-floor apartment on Van Horne. Her table was small, so she braided in two halves and joined them with a pinch she never bothered to hide. The loaves were uneven and they were the best I have eaten.

I started baking them again in 2012, after she died, from a recipe card in her handwriting that lists flour in coffee cups. It took me four years to work out that her cup was 140 g, not 120, which is why my first attempts were dry. The card now hangs above the mixer. I still use it, with a scale.

The bakery opened at the door on avenue du Parc in 2021: one deck oven, one mixer, one bench and a window that fogs every Thursday night. The work is still the same three things — flour, time and hands — and the parts that matter are the parts you can measure.

The bake · Thursday into Friday

Eight steps, from the scale to the first slice

Weigh everything, including the eggs

20:20 · ten minutes

Bread flour at 12.7% protein, water at 60% of the flour, whole eggs at 10%, sunflower oil at 6%, sugar at 4%, fine salt at 1.8% and instant yeast at 0.8%. Weigh the eggs out of the shell; a large egg is 50 g of liquid, not 60. Every number is a percentage of the flour, so the formula scales without arithmetic.

Autolyse before the salt

20:30 · twenty minutes

Flour, water, eggs and oil sit together for twenty minutes. The flour hydrates, the gluten begins to organise itself, and the dough needs less kneading later. Salt and yeast go in after. Salt early tightens the dough; yeast early and warm starts a ferment I am not ready for.

Knead to a window, not a clock

20:50 · eight minutes

Eight minutes on the mixer's second speed, or twelve by hand with a bench scraper. The dough is ready when a walnut-sized piece stretches thin enough to let light through without tearing. Challah is enriched, so it takes longer than a plain dough and will feel tacky until the last minute.

Bulk with three folds

21:00 · ninety minutes

Ninety minutes at 22 °C, folding at thirty and sixty. Folds build strength without beating the dough down. In a cold kitchen, give it another twenty minutes; in July, start checking at seventy.

Divide, then chill overnight

22:30 · overnight at 4 °C

Divide into strands while the dough is soft, roll each under a cupped hand, then set them on a tray and refrigerate. Cold dough is firm and braids cleanly; warm dough stretches, sags and welds its strands into one lump. The retard also gives the loaf its flavour, which is the part that cannot be hurried.

Braid at six in the morning

06:00 · twenty minutes for six loaves

Six strands, joined at one end and counted left to right. The rule is simple: the outside strand goes over two, under one, and into the middle. Pull each strand snug but never tight — the braid has to have room to rise. A 54 cm strand makes a 39 cm loaf.

Proof, wash twice, bake on stone

06:20 · seventy minutes, then thirty-one in the oven

Seventy minutes at 22 °C under a linen cloth. Egg wash once at the start of the proof and once ten minutes before the oven, with a light hand: too much wash pools in the crevices and browns before the crumb sets. Bake at 190 °C on a stone with four minutes of steam, then dry heat. The loaf is done at 88 °C in the centre.

Cool before you cut

08:10 · forty minutes

A challah cut hot steams out and turns gummy by the time it reaches the table. Forty minutes on a rack, then wrap it in linen, not plastic. It keeps to Saturday lunch and makes acceptable toast on Sunday, which is more than most bread can say.

Taking challah

When the batch is large enough, I separate a piece of dough the size of an olive, say the blessing and burn it in the oven. This is hafrashat challah, and it is the one part of the bake that is not about the bread at all. On a home batch under 1.2 kg of flour the portion is taken without a blessing; I keep a note on the wall so I do not have to remember which batch is which.

What I do not do

  • No same-day orders. The dough needs the retard and I need the sleep.
  • No fondant, no glitter, no shaped animals. The braid is the decoration.
  • No shipping. Bread that spends a day in a box is not the bread I make.
  • No dairy in the dough, so a loaf can sit on a table with meat. The kitchen is not certified kosher; if that matters to you, treat it as a home kitchen rather than a supervised one.

Flour and weather

Quebec flour changes with the wheat year. The bag will say 12.7% protein and the dough will still feel different in February than in September. I keep the hydration at 60% and adjust with a tablespoon of water at a time rather than changing the formula. In a humid week the dough takes two minutes longer to come together; in February it takes ten.
