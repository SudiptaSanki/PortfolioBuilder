How the horn came first

The first instrument I was handed was not a cornet. It was a snare drum with a split head, and I was nine, and I was told to keep time while the older kids played. That is how most of us start: at the back, counting, watching the horns to know where the phrase turns around.

My uncle played trombone in a social aid and pleasure club band. He brought me to a rehearsal at a hall on North Robertson in the spring of 1998 and sat me on a folding chair beside the bass drum. By the second hour I had stopped watching the drummer and started watching the cornet, because the cornet was the only instrument that had to be right on every single beat and still sound like it was having fun.

He lent me a student cornet that summer. I played it badly for two years, then less badly for another three. The first time I was allowed on the front line for a full parade I was nineteen, and I made it four miles before my lip quit. The lesson from that day is still the lesson: a parade is not a performance, it is a distance.

Play loud enough to be found. Play clean enough to be followed.

Written on the inside of my case lid, in pencil, in 2003

What a season actually costs

People see the costume and the parade and assume the work is the hour on the street. The hour is the smallest part. A season runs from Twelfth Night to Ash Wednesday, and inside it there are section rehearsals on Tuesday nights, fittings, float walk-throughs, and the long argument about keys that happens every January because the saxophones want to be comfortable.

I keep a written record of every parade I play: the route, the weather, the horn I used, and the moment the band locked. There are 61 entries for last season. The best one is three lines long and describes a turn onto St. Charles at dusk, when the snare line changed the feel without anyone saying a word.

Why I still play the small rooms

A ballroom holds two hundred people and no reverberation. You can hear every mistake and every good decision in the same second. Playing there keeps the parade honest, because a horn that leans on echo to sound full will fall apart the moment the echo is gone.

I take four to six ball dates a season and three or four workshop weeks in schools. The workshops pay less and matter more. Half the kids who try a cornet will quit inside a year; the ones who stay are the reason the line still exists in twenty years.
