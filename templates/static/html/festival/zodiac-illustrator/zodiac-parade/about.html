The illustrator · 陈美

Twelve animals, drawn small enough to fit in a pocket

Mei Tan has drawn the same twelve animals for six years, once a year, at the size of a postcard — and then watched them become twelve metres of street.

At a glance Born 1992, Penang
Singapore since 2010
Riso press, 2017
First parade banner, 2021

01Penang

A grandmother's shop, and a wall of calendars

The shop sold dried goods and, every January, calendars. Her grandmother hung twelve of them along the back wall, one for each supplier, and Mei spent the school holidays copying the animals out of them with a ballpoint pen. The rat from the medicine calendar. The dragon from the hardware one. None of the drawings matched, and she noticed before she could explain why it mattered.

She came to Singapore at eighteen for a diploma in illustration, stayed for the presses, and has been in Jalan Besar since. The studio is one room above a bak kut teh shop, with a risograph, a drying rack, a guillotine older than she is, and a wall of failed proofs she refuses to throw away.

“A line has to be decided. A gradient can hide a mistake for ever.”

02The press

Two inks, and a machine that jams on Tuesdays

The risograph is a 1997 model bought secondhand from a church printer in Geylang. It prints one colour at a time through a stencil, which means every poster passes through the machine twice and every registration is slightly, physically imperfect. She has stopped fighting this. The blue plate is printed first and the red second, and the half-millimetre shift is left where it lands.

Two inks also force a decision that a computer does not: what happens where they overlap. Red over blue gives a purple that is nearly black and never quite; blue over red is a different kind of dark. She plans the drawings around that third colour rather than treating it as an accident.

  • Fluorescent orange and teal blue on 160 gsm cream stock for the parade series.
  • Black and yellow for the calendar sets, because they are meant to be written on.
  • Gold foil for the hongbao, pressed by a neighbour with a letterpress in Kallang.
03The banner

How a postcard becomes a street

In 2021 the Chinatown parade committee asked for a banner and gave her a brief of two lines. She drew the ox at ten centimetres, scanned it at 1200 dpi, and sent the file to a large-format printer in Ubi who printed it on scrim — a mesh-reinforced vinyl that survives a monsoon and does not flap itself to pieces in a crosswind.

The drawing changed on the way up. Lines that read as confident at ten centimetres became uncertain at twelve metres, and two whiskers had to be redrawn because they disappeared against the sky. She now proofs every banner at A3 first, pinned to the wall, and stands at the far end of the corridor to look at it.

The animals are drawn as a single set, one weight, no fills, so that any of the twelve can be enlarged without redrawing. That constraint is the whole style. It is also why the set has not changed much in six years: improving one animal would mean redrawing all twelve.

05On the tradition

Borrowing, carefully

The zodiac is not hers. It is a calendar system that a few billion people grew up with, and the animals carry meanings — the rat's cleverness, the ox's patience, the horse's restlessness — that were not invented for a poster. She reads the older woodblock prints before drawing, and she keeps the branch characters accurate even when the drawing is loose.

What she adds is the printing: the misregistration, the fluorescent ink, the halftone. That is a Singapore printing tradition as much as a Chinese one, and it is the part she can honestly call her own.

She also does not let a machine draw the animals. The line is hers, drawn by hand at postcard size, scanned, and kept. Twelve animals, six years, one weight of line.
