The studio

A desk, two bottles of ink, and a tide table on the wall.

I draw comics in a rented room above a hardware shop in Fort Kochi. The room is hot for four months of the year, which is probably why so much of my work takes place in water.

I came to comics from printmaking. At art school in Baroda I was making large woodcuts and hating how little they could say in sequence. The first comic I finished was seven pages about a bus conductor, drawn in a single week with a brush that was already splitting. It was the first time an image of mine had a before and an after, and I have not really stopped since.

Everything begins with a walk. For Kayal I spent five weeks travelling the backwaters with a fish workers' collective, filling three notebooks with what people said while they mended nets. Almost none of it is quoted directly in the book. It is there in how long a character takes to answer.

The drawing itself is simple in materials and hard in discipline: a brush for wash, a steel nib for line, a single sheet of 300 gsm paper per page. I cut the risk out of the work by keeping the tools boring.

I keep one rule about tools: if a tool needs explaining at a workshop, it is the wrong tool for a workshop. The students I teach at the Kochi comics residency get a brush, a nib, and a stack of cheap paper. The expensive paper comes after they have ruined a hundred sheets.

How a page gets made

  1. Week 1 — listening

    Walk and record

    Notebooks, a voice recorder, and no drawings at all for the first four days. The material is the way people pause.

  2. Week 2 — thumbnails

    Sixty pages of nothing

    The whole book as rectangles the size of a stamp. Panels that cannot be understood at that size are cut here, not later.

  3. Weeks 3–9 — ink

    One page a day

    Pencil, then nib, then wash. A page that takes two days is a page whose panel count is wrong, so the thumbnails get a second look.

  4. Week 10 — print

    Two passes at the riso

    Black first, then the spot. Registration is never perfect and that is part of the look; the misregistration becomes a texture the reader feels rather than sees.

01 / Materials

Brush and nib

A No. 4 sable for wash, a Hunt 102 for line, and Chinese ink that dries matte. Nothing digital until the lettering stage.

02 / Pace

Nine panels or two

Density is the only real grammar I trust. A quiet page earns the loud one that follows it.

03 / Print

Riso in Kochi

Two passes at a small press in Kaloor, 180 copies a run. The machine is older than I am and jams on humid days.

04 / Teaching

The residency

Six artists a year, ten weeks, no fees, one shared riso. Applications open each January.

Things I am reading

The Arrival, Shaun Tan — for how much a silent page can carry. Bhimayana, Subhash Vyam and Srividya Natarajan — for the courage of pattern as narrative. Palestine, Joe Sacco — for the notebook as a form.

Also: the tide tables of Kochi harbour, a photocopied manual on outboard motors, and every Malayalam film title card from 1978 to 1986, which is research for something I have not started yet.
