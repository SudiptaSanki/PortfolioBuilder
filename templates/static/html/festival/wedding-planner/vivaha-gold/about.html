About the practice

A planner who arrives
with a notebook

I am the second person in my family to be paid for standing near a wedding kitchen. My grandmother cooked for forty years on Kutchery Road; I carry her ledger numbers in my head and her rule in my manners — never let a guest see you hurry.

My first wedding was a cousin's, in 2014, with a borrowed clipboard and no insurance, after nine years producing events for a hotel group in Chennai that shall stay unnamed because I left in a hurry. What I learned there was unglamorous and useful: a wedding is a logistics problem wearing silk.

The first thing families ask me is what a wedding costs. The second is whether the muhurtham can move. The answer to the second is almost always no, and the answer to the first is the reason I built the mandapam plan on the front page — because guest count, footprint and leaf count decide eighty percent of the budget before a decorator has opened his mouth.

I take two weddings a month, sometimes three in Aadi when the hall rates drop. I do not do destination weddings to temples I have not walked, and I do not take a wedding I cannot be present at from the janavasam through the last leaf. If your date is taken, I will say so in the first line of my reply and give you two names I trust.

How the four months go

  1. Month oneGuest list, budget band, three hall visits, the muhurtham fixed against the panchangam.
  2. Month twoCaterer tasting at my table, decor drawings to scale, photographer chosen for the light in your hall, not their Instagram.
  3. Month threeInvitations proofed twice, saris and jewellery listed, the run sheet drafted vendor by vendor, advances paid and dated in the ledger.
  4. Month fourSite recce on the same hour as the muhurtham, a dry run of the procession route, the family's jobs written down so nobody asks you anything on the day.

The same people, wedding after wedding

Who is actually there on the day

I work with a small set of people I have used for years. You are not buying a vendor list; you are buying that these five will answer the phone in March at 3 a.m.

  • VR

    Vaani Raghavan planning, the clock, the ledger

    Walks the hall with the priest the day before. Holds the only copy of the run sheet that matters and the cash box for the morning.

  • SK

    Sundaram Kitchen catering

    My grandmother's head cook, now with his own forty-person team. Twenty-two item sadhya, leaf order written by hand, payasam timed to the last round of photographs.

  • ML

    Mallikai Strand flowers

    Three women on the floor of a house on Veeraperumal Koil Street who string a thousand metres of jasmine in a night and keep it in wet cotton until it is hung.

  • AR

    Arul Nadaswaram music

    A pair of nadaswaram and thavil, a playlist that knows when to stop, and a second pair on call for the janavasam route in case the first pair's bus is late.

  • PN

    Prema Nair photography

    Available-light work, no flash during the muhurtham, and a printed album at your door before the first anniversary. She has photographed eighteen of my weddings.

House rules, written on the inside of the notebook

01The muhurtham time is not a suggestion. Everything else in the run sheet is negotiable.

02No vendor is confirmed until the advance is dated in the ledger. A WhatsApp yes is not a booking.

03The family's list of jobs is short and written. If someone is idle, they will start rearranging chairs.

04Feed the crew before the guests. A hungry lighting team at 4 a.m. is a safety problem.

05One person — me — has the final word on the floor. Even over the eldest uncle. Especially over the eldest uncle.

06At the end, the bills are settled, the hall is empty, and the family is at breakfast. I leave when the last chair is stacked.
