Weddings, with the budgets left in

Most planners show you photographs and hide the arithmetic. Mine is the other way round: what the family asked for, what I did, what came of it, and the budget going in and coming out. One of these three went over. It is printed too, with the reason.

The 9:42 muhurat

Hyderabad · December 2025 · Telugu wedding
620Guests
3Days of events
₹52,00,000Budget planned
₹50,64,000Budget spent
₹1,36,000Returned with the accounts

The ask. A muhurat fixed at 9:42 in the morning by the family priest, in a hotel ballroom across the city — and the bride's family arriving from Vijayawada on a train due in at 8:10 the same morning. The groom's family wanted a baraat that crossed the bride's route to the hall.

What I did. The baraat was re-routed to the hotel's lawn gate, so the two parties never blocked each other. Fourteen cars were placed at the station from 7:30, because that train is late more often than it is on time in December. Breakfast was served in two sittings so the hall could be emptied, dressed and closed by 9:20.

The outcome. The train came in fifty minutes late. The bride walked in at 9:31, the muhurat was met at 9:42, and the only people who knew there had been a problem were the ones who had planned for it. The wedding came in under budget because the lawn venue for day one was negotiated in August, before the season's rates rose.

“Kavita told us in October that the Vijayawada train would be late on the wedding morning. It was, by fifty minutes. Her cars were still at the station, and my daughter walked in at 9:31 for a 9:42 muhurat.”
— K. Lakshmi, the bride's mother

The reception stage that evening — the same hall, turned around in four hours.

Two families, one fort

Jaipur · January 2026 · Marwari–Bengali wedding
380 → 412Guests, printed → actual
4Days of events
₹68,00,000Budget planned
₹69,12,000Budget spent
₹1,12,000Over — reason below

The mandap in the fort courtyard — built over two nights, struck in one.

The ask. A Marwari family from Jaipur and a Bengali family from Kolkata, who had met twice, marrying at a heritage fort property: two full ritual calendars — Bengali rituals in the mornings, the sangeet and wedding in the evenings — and one shared roof for four days.

What I did. The mornings ran in parallel, in two courtyards, with a Bengali priest brought from Kolkata and a shared sweets counter where the families actually met. Menus were split by day and by family, with one joint dinner on day three. Every ritual was timed against the other family's, so nobody's ceremony waited on anybody's.

The outcome — and the overrun. The guest list grew from 380 printed invitations to 412 people who came. The extra thirty-two plates, rooms and cars were paid first from the contingency line, as it exists to be used; the remaining ₹1,12,000 went beyond it, and it is printed here because it happened. The family knew the figure the same evening, not at the final accounts.

“The guest list grew after the cards were printed — our doing, entirely. She fed everyone, housed everyone, and showed us the exact line where the money went. There was no argument, because there was nothing hidden.”
— R. Banerjee, the groom's father

The small one

Secunderabad · February 2026 · Garden wedding
140Guests
2Days of events
₹18,50,000Budget planned
₹17,86,000Budget spent
₹64,000Returned with the accounts

The ask. A couple who wanted no stage, no receiving line and no evening function: a ceremony at eleven under the trees of a Secunderabad garden, one long lunch, and everyone home by four. Three other planners had tried to talk them out of it.

What I did. The mandap was kept low and open so the trees stayed the decoration. Lunch was one sitting, served at the tables, with the couple eating with their guests instead of posing above them. A veena player — the bride's uncle — played through the ceremony; there was no DJ, and the generator was hired anyway, because gardens and December afternoons are not a promise.

The outcome. Home by four, as asked. The saving came from the honest places: one venue day instead of two, flowers cut to what the garden did not already provide, and a lunch menu priced on 140 covers plus the true-number margin — 158 plates cooked, 149 eaten.

“We asked for lunch under trees and home by four. She was the only planner who did not try to sell us a stage.”
— Sruthi & Karthik, the couple

A lawn ceremony set for 140 — the trees did most of the decorating.
