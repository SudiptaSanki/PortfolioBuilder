The turner · bench no. 2

A lathe, a chair, and one stubborn top.

I am not a rabbi, a historian or a toymaker. I am a turner with a small bench, a rack of dry boards, and a wife whose father asked for a dreidel that would outlast the plastic ones in the drawer.

A garage in San Antonio

My grandfather turned chair legs in a garage off Zarzamora Street for thirty years, first for a furniture shop and then, after it closed, for anyone who asked. He was Japanese, born in California, and he had a way of squaring a board that looked like a magic trick. I was eight when he put a gouge in my hand and told me to keep my knuckles on the tool rest. I still do.

I did not become a turner straight away. I studied industrial design in San Antonio, worked six years drawing fixtures for a shop that made restaurant kitchens, and turned on weekends. In 2014 my grandfather died and left me the lathe — a 1948 Delta with a sag in the bed and a switch I have replaced twice. I moved it to Austin in a borrowed truck and put it against the wall of a rented room on East 6th.

The request

My wife's father, David, collected dreidels. Not valuable ones; he collected the ones his children had grown up with, a drawer of plastic tops and one carved from olive wood that had lost its handle. At a Hanukkah dinner in 2017 he said the thing that changed my work: “A plastic dreidel is a game. A wooden one is a memory. Make me one that my grandchildren will fight over.”

I made him one from a scrap of walnut. It was too light, it wobbled, and it landed on its side twice. He kept it anyway. The third one was better. By the sixth I had learned to put a brass pin in the tip and to hollow the body so the weight sits low — the same principle as a weeble, though I did not know that then. He died in 2021. The dreidel sits on a shelf in our house, and his grandchildren do argue over it.

Wood remembers the hand that turned it. My job is to make sure it remembers a steady one.

How a dreidel is turned

  • Rough out green. A blank is cut oversize and turned to a cylinder, then left to move for six weeks in the rack above the bench.
  • Finish-turn. Back on the lathe, true to the final width, with the body tapered a degree or two so the top leans into its spin instead of away from it.
  • Hollow and weight. A 22 mm bore, a brass pin set in the point, then the cavity filled to bring the centre of gravity down to the bottom third.
  • Carve the letters. A 2 mm veining gouge, one face at a time, checked with a square. The paint goes in after the oil has cured, not before.
  • Spin test. Twenty spins on the bench. Anything under eight seconds, or anything that walks more than a hand's width, is re-tipped or rejected.

What I will not do

I do not carve letters with a laser. I do not use a decal, a stencil or a CNC. I do not sell a top I have not spun myself, and I do not take an order I cannot finish before the holiday it is meant for. If that means a run of two hundred in November becomes a run of one hundred and sixty, the customer hears it in October, not December.

The lathe

1948 Delta, 12-inch swing

Inherited 2014. Bed with a sag of about 0.4 mm over its length, which is why every blank is turned between centres and checked with calipers rather than trusted to the tailstock.

The bench

1204 East 6th Street

A rented back room behind a print shop. No shopfront, no sign. Visits by appointment, usually on Saturday mornings, when the print shop is quiet and the coffee is on.

On the wall

David's six dreidels

The first six are in a frame above the lathe: too light, too heavy, one with a crack I filled with brass dust and glue. They are the honest record of the learning.

Also on the bench

Not only dreidels

Mezuzah cases, seder plates and the occasional chess piece. The dreidels pay the rent; the rest keeps the gouges sharp.

The bench, year by year

  • 2014The Delta comes to Austin. First year is chair legs and salad bowls for friends; no dreidels.
  • 2017David asks for one that will last. First dreidel, walnut, too light, kept anyway.
  • 2019Brass pins and a hollowed body. The first top to spin past twelve seconds.
  • 2021David dies. His six dreidels go on the wall; the shop starts taking orders from his synagogue's gift shop.
  • 2024A run of 200 maple tops for a museum shop in Houston. Twelve rejected; the museum asks for the rejects as a display.
  • 2026Nine woods on the rack, 1,840 tops turned, and a waiting list that starts in September.
