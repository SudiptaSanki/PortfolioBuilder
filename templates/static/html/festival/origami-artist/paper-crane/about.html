Method

The drawing is the hard part. The folding is the proof.

I fold paper, one sheet at a time, without cuts or glue. Before anything is folded there is a crease pattern on graph paper and a calculation of whether the sheet is large enough to carry the shape. Most of the work happens before the paper is touched.

B. 1988, Kyoto Studio since 2016 Washi, tant, kozo

The sheet

Paper is not a neutral material. It has a direction, a grain, a memory. A sheet folded against the grain tears at the crease; folded with it, the crease holds for years. I buy washi from a workshop in Echizen and tant from a supplier in Tokyo, and I keep each run in a drawer with a note of how it folded. Twenty sheets from the same ream can behave like twenty different papers.

For a piece that has to hold a curve, I wet-fold: dampen the paper, fold it, and let it dry in shape. Kozo takes this well because its fibres are long. Tant does not; it goes limp and then marks. Knowing which paper refuses a fold is half the design.

The crease pattern

Every piece begins as a crease pattern: a square with the mountain and valley folds drawn in. The pattern is a map of where the paper will be, not a set of instructions. I draw it in pencil, copy it onto a sheet, and fold a maquette in cheap paper to see whether the map is true. Most patterns are wrong the first time, in a way that only the paper can show.

The crane's pattern is 32 creases and has not changed in my studio since 2014, when I re-derived it from my grandmother's folding rather than from a diagram. It is the piece I teach first because it contains almost every fold a beginner needs: valley, mountain, petal, reverse.

“Paper will do what you ask once. Ask wrongly and it remembers.”

No cuts, no glue

The rule is not a purist gesture; it is a design constraint that makes the work honest. If I can solve a shape by cutting, I have not solved it by folding. The rule forces the pattern to carry the structure, and it means a piece can be unfolded back into a sheet, which is how I check it. Every work in the archive can be flattened without damage, except the wet-folded ones, which would crack.

Scale

The largest single sheet I can usefully fold is about 1.6 metres; beyond that the paper's own weight pulls the creases out and the shape sags. For bigger pieces I join sheets along creases, which is the one place glue is allowed, because the joint is structural and hidden. The 4 by 6 metre wall in Osaka is 240 sheets joined this way, and the seams run inside the pattern.

Teaching

Thursday evenings, six people, two hours. We start with the crane and end with a tessellation, and nobody leaves without a finished fold, even if it is a small one. I teach the crease pattern as well as the steps, because a student who understands the pattern can fix a fold that has gone wrong instead of starting again.

The studio also takes two apprentices a year, from April, for nine months. They cut paper, fold maquettes and sweep the floor. The ones who stay are the ones who can sit with a wrong fold for an hour without getting angry.

What I will not do

  • Cut a shape and call it origami.
  • Copy another folder's published pattern for a commercial commission.
  • Ship a piece without a box that can survive a courier.
  • Promise a paper colour the mill is not making this year.

“The bench is by the window because the paper tells you more in daylight.”
