Work & Commissions

Four commissions, told the way they happened.

Each piece below began as a letter or a visit. I have written them the way I would tell them in the studio: what was asked, what I did, and where the painting lives now. The rate card and the planner further down this page are the same figures I quote from in person.

The Kohbar of the Two Rivers

The ask. A family in Madhubani town was marrying their daughter to a boy from across the Kamala river. They wanted the kohbar on her bedroom wall to carry both families' rivers, so the groom's people would find their own water waiting in the painting when they arrived.

What I did. The wall was washed and limed a week ahead. The great lotus sits at the centre, as it must. Beneath it run two rivers in indigo and one in green — the third is the pond by the house, which the bride insisted on, because she learned to swim there. The bride's two aunts filled the border hatching with me, the way it has always been done: the wall belongs to the house, and the house helps paint it.

The outcome. Finished three days before the first guests arrived. The groom's mother, seeing the painting, asked which river was hers — and was told, correctly, the one with the extra fish.

“Sunaina asked my daughter what the wall should remember. Nobody had asked her that. The painting is the first thing every guest asks about, before the food, before the gifts.”

— Phulo Devi, the bride's mother, on The Kohbar of the Two Rivers

Madhushravani: Five Panels for the Storytelling

The ask. A cultural society in Patna holds the fifteen evenings of Madhushravani storytelling every Shravan. Their old printed backdrop had torn. They asked for painted panels of the snake goddess story that could be read from the back of a hall — and stored flat for the other fifty weeks of the year.

What I did. Five medium panels, one for each turn of the story, in bharni — the filled style reads at a distance, where kachni would have vanished past the third row. I painted the serpent large and calm. In the story she is not a villain; she is a wronged goddess, and the painting should say so before the storyteller says a word.

The outcome. The set hung for all fifteen evenings, then was framed and now lives in the society's hall. They have ordered a second set — the wedding of the goddess — for next year.

“Our storyteller said the panels did half her work. The children sat down before she began, because the snake was already looking at them.”

— Ranjana Sinha, programme secretary, on the Madhushravani set

A Jitiya Panel That Folds Away

The ask. A mother in Darbhanga paints her puja room fresh every Jitiya and was tired of the work washing away. She asked for the fish of the festival on cloth — something she could hang for the fast, then fold into the family trunk with the good saris.

What I did. Kachni, in lampblack alone, on washed cotton. Line work folds without cracking, where a thick bharni fill would suffer at the creases. The fish is drawn at the size of a hand-span and a half, the measure her grandmother used for the wall version.

The outcome. It hung for the festival, was photographed for the family, and folded away with a sheet of plain cotton over the painted side — the storage method I wrote out for her on the order card.
