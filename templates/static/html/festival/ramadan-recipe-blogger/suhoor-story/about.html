This week The recipes About Amina Write to me

I did not set out to write about food.

Amina Rahman is a schoolteacher in Hyderabad, the second of four daughters, and the person her family telephones when the dough will not come together. She has been writing Suhoor Story since 2021.

In the Ramadan of 2021 my mother was in her last month and none of us slept properly. I was awake at three anyway, so I started cooking, and because I could not concentrate on anything longer than a page, I kept a notebook beside the stove: what I made, how long it took, what my father ate and what he pushed to the side of the plate. Thirty days of it. It was not grief-writing and it was not a recipe book. It was a list of things that worked.

My eldest sister read it in the summer and typed it up, and then a friend of hers asked for the paratha method, and then a woman in Bangalore asked for the keema, and at some point it became this. I still write it the same way. If a thing did not work at four in the morning in my kitchen, it does not go on the site, however good it looks in the afternoon.

What I actually do

I teach history at a girls' school in Malakpet, standard nine and ten. I am at the school by 8:15, home by 4:30, and I cook for six people most evenings because my father lives with us and my youngest sister has not moved out and may never. The blog is written between nine and eleven at night, which is why the sentences are short.

I am not a chef. I have never worked in a restaurant. I have never been paid to cook anything. What I have is thirty-eight years of eating the same food in the same house as my mother and grandmother, and a stubbornness about doing things properly that my mother would have called, with affection, a talent for making life difficult.

Why suhoor and not iftar

Iftar is easy to write about and easy to photograph: dates, a table full of dishes, people coming in at sunset, the noise. Suhoor is the opposite. It happens when everyone is tired and slightly cross, it is eaten fast, and nobody takes a picture of it. But it is the meal that decides the day. A bad suhoor makes sixteen hours into a punishment; a good one makes them ordinary.

So this is a small site about an unglamorous meal, written for people who are going to read it at an hour when they do not want an essay. I try to keep the essays above the recipes, so you can scroll past them.

If a recipe needs a second pan, it had better be worth the washing up. At four in the morning, most things are not.

The rules the recipes follow

No more than eight ingredients, not counting salt and water. Nothing that needs constant stirring for more than five minutes. Nothing that has to be fried to order unless it is under five minutes. Nothing that cannot be reheated or eaten cold, because half of you are going back to bed. And nothing — nothing — that requires an ingredient you would have to go out and buy at ten at night.

One more thing

I write as a Muslim, and Ramadan is not a food festival, and I am aware of the difference. The point of these recipes is that the meal should take as little as possible from the month — twenty minutes at the stove, not two hours — so that the rest of the time can go where it is supposed to go.
