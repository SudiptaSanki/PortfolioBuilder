The family

One cart,
three generations,
one corner

My grandfather bought the cart in 1978 from a fabricator in Kamezawa. It has been pushed to the same corner of Kaminarimon-dori almost every night since, except for two months in 1995 when the axle broke and a week in 2020 when there was no festival at all.

My name is Hiroshi Kondo. My grandfather Tetsuo started with a single pan and a hand-painted noren; my father Kenji added the flat plate for yakisoba in 1986 and the second pan in 1999. I took over the gas bottle in 2008, when my father's knees stopped agreeing with standing for six hours.

The cart is the original. The body has been re-welded twice, the wheels replaced four times, the wooden counter sanded down so far that you can see the grain of the second board he ever fitted. The noren is new every third year, painted by the same woman in Katsushika who has done it since 1991.

We have been offered money to move to a permanent shop three times. Twice by landlords, once by a company that wanted to put the cart in a food hall in Shinjuku. My father's answer is the one I use: a yatai is not a shop that has not found a building. It is a stall that arrives, and that is the whole of it.

What we buy

The octopus comes from a supplier near Toyosu, twice a week, and it is the one thing we do not economise on — cut into pieces that fit the hole exactly, boiled the night before. The flour is a blend of two, the dashi is kombu and bonito made in the shop, and the nagaimo is grated to order because it goes grey if you do it in the morning.

The sauce is my grandfather's recipe: Worcestershire, tomato, dates, a little soy, simmered and left for two days. I will not write the quantities down here because my mother would find out.

The rules of the cart

No one works two jobs after seven. The first batch of every night is given away to whoever is standing nearest. Money goes in the tin, never in a pocket, and the tin is counted at home with the television off. If the queue reaches the corner, we stop taking orders for a minute and let it shorten, because a queue that does not move is how a stall loses a street.

And the lantern goes up before the pan is hot. My grandfather said a stall with food and no light is a stall that is closing. A stall with light and no food is one that is about to open. Both are better than the other way round.

Behind the counter

Who you will meet

  1. Hiroshi

    The pan

    Turns every ball by hand with a steel pick, twice, and never two pans at once. If you ask him how many he has made in his life he will say "not enough" and mean it politely.

  2. Michiko

    The money

    His wife. Takes the cash, remembers every regular's order, and is the reason the queue moves. She also decides when the lantern comes down, which is not a decision Hiroshi is allowed to make.

  3. Sota

    The plate

    Their son, nineteen, on the yakisoba from six o'clock. He is faster than his father and less careful, and the two of them discuss this every Sunday.

  4. Kenji

    The bench

    His father, seventy-eight, who comes at eight, sits on the bench that belongs to the building, and tells customers not to order too much. He still checks the gas connection every night.

“The cart has been on that corner longer than most of the buildings around it. When the street was re-paved in 2011, the workers put the new stones around it rather than ask it to move.”

A neighbour on Kaminarimon-dori · 2025
