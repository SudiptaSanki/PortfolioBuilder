Nine people, one furnace, and a lot of filing

My grandfather repaired kettles here in 1962. The trade has changed: the kettles are plastic now, the lamps are still brass, and the furnace runs on a gas line instead of a borrowed blowpipe. The filing has not changed at all.

I am the third generation at this bench, though my father would say the fourth, counting the uncle who poured for two years before I was born. I started at fourteen, not with a lamp but with a bucket of lamp parts and a file, smoothing the gates off cast bases until my wrists ached. That is still how I judge a new apprentice: give them forty bases and see whether the fortieth is as clean as the first.

Brass is a forgiving metal and a dishonest one. It looks solid when it is thin, it polishes to gold when it is full of pin holes, and it hides a bad seam under a beautiful surface. So every reservoir goes into the water tank before it goes to the lathe, and every finished lamp is burned with the oil the buyer will use. That is not a marketing promise; it is the only way I know to find out whether the thing works.

A lamp that goes out at three in the morning is worse than a lamp that was never lit. Somebody has to get up.

The shop is on the ground floor of the house, open to the lane on one side so the air moves. The furnace is at the back, the lathe by the window, and the burn tests happen on a steel tray near the door where the draught is worst — because the draught is what will happen in the customer’s house. In summer we start at six and stop by two. In winter the brass is cold enough that the polishing paste takes twice as long.

I do not sell through a website. People write or come to the lane, and we talk about the room the lamp is going into: the height of the table, whether there is a fan overhead, whether the flame will be left alone or watched. Half the orders change after that conversation. A temple committee that came for a jhoomar left with two samais, and the veranda got a plain dish lamp instead.

What I will not do: lacquer a working lamp, put a bulb inside a brass shade and call it a lamp, or copy a design from a photograph without telling the person whose design it is. If you send me a picture, I will tell you what it is and whether it can be made honestly at the price you have in mind.

The order of work

Four stages, and the test that decides whether any of it mattered

  1. 01 · Melt

    Sheet into shape

    Cartridge brass is sorted by gauge, cut, annealed and raised over a stake. A reservoir takes two hours of hammering and one hour of annealing in between, or the metal cracks at the rim.

  2. 02 · Cast

    Base and stem

    The base and any solid ornament are sand-cast. Sprue and runners are cut off, weighed, and go back into the next melt, which is why the 12% loss never leaves the shop.

  3. 03 · File

    Seam, joint, surface

    The seam is soldered from the inside, then filed flush. Three grades of emery, then tamarind and brick dust. No lacquer on anything that will hold a flame.

  4. 04 · Burn

    Twelve hours, minimum

    Every reservoir is filled with the buyer’s oil and burned on the draughty side of the door. If it goes out before twelve hours, it does not leave — it goes back to the bench.
