What leaves the workshop

Three kinds of order, one furnace

01

Temple sets

A pair of samais for the sanctum door, a panchmukhi for the offering table, and a spare reservoir ring, all from the same melt so the patina ages together. We pour in pairs because a single lamp beside its twin looks like a mistake.

02

Household akhand jyot

For a vow, a birth, or the eleven nights after a death. These are smaller and plainer than temple work, with a lip wide enough to refill from a spoon without a funnel, because whoever lights it at four in the morning is half asleep.

03

Repairs and re-wicking

Grandmother’s lamp with a split seam, a jhoomar whose chain has been re-soldered three times. I keep the old dents. A lamp that has been in a family for eighty years should not come back looking as if it was bought last week.

Material notes

Why the brass is never quite one colour

Cartridge sheet arrives in bundles from a dealer in Bareilly, already a hundred shades of yellow because it has lived another life. I sort it by thickness under a lamp, not by eye alone: a 0.6 mm sheet rings higher when flicked. The gauge decides how the rim behaves when it is spun on the lathe, and how much the lamp weighs in the hand, which is the first thing a customer notices.

A cast lamp is not a quiet object. It takes the fire, then the file, then three grades of emery, then a paste of tamarind and brick dust that turns the skin of the metal the colour of weak tea. Left alone, it darkens; rubbed with oil once a month, it goes back to gold. I do not lacquer the working lamps, because lacquer burns at the rim and then the whole surface has to be stripped.
