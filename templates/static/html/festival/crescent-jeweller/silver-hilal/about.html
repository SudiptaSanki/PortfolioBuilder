The workshop · Zincirli Han

A bench, a mandrel and a saw that is older than I am.

The workshop is twelve square metres on the first floor of the Zincirli Han, with one window over the courtyard and a door that does not quite close. I have worked here since 1998, when I took over the bench from Hasan Usta, who took it from his father.

My family came to Istanbul from Kayseri in 1972, and my father was a jeweller's errand boy in the bazaar before he was a jeweller. I was not a good student. At fifteen I was sent to Hasan Usta to learn a trade, and for the first year I swept the floor and polished other people's work. He let me cut a band in my second year and said nothing about it for three days. Then he said the ends did not meet, and to do it again.

Hasan Usta died in 2011. His saw hangs on the wall above my bench, and I still use it for crescent plates because the blade is thin and the frame is light. When people ask what makes the work different, I tell them it is that saw, which is true and also a way of not answering.

What the crescent means, and does not

The crescent is older than any of the faiths that use it. It appears on coins in the Greek world, on Sassanid seals, and on the standards of cities that had never heard of the Prophet. The Ottomans put it on the flag after 1453, and from there it became a symbol people now read as Islamic. I make it because it is a beautiful shape with two radii and a gap, and because the customers who ask for it come from every kind of household in the city.

I do not claim the ring has a blessing in it. If you want a blessing, ask someone who is entitled to give one. I can promise that the crescent will be cut evenly and the stone will stay in.

How the metal behaves

Sterling, not fine silver

Sterling is 92.5% silver with copper for hardness. Fine silver is softer and will mark at the edge of a table; sterling will last a lifetime and can be re-polished. Every piece is assayed and struck with the 925 mark and my maker's mark, as the law requires.

Gold and platinum

White gold is 18k, alloyed with palladium rather than nickel, so it does not provoke the allergy that older white gold sometimes did. Platinum is 950 and heavier; it wears differently, keeping a soft sheen rather than a mirror polish. Both are cast to my drawings rather than forged, because the alloys are harder to work cold.

Setting

A stone is set with four beads, raised from the metal around it with a graver. On an eclipse ring the stone sits inside the closing horns and is held only by the arc, so it must be a cabochon — a brilliant would be lost. I say no to that request more often than any other.

How to find a ring size

  • Measure the inside of a ring that fits, in millimetres, and divide by 3.1416 for the diameter.
  • Or wrap a strip of paper around the finger, mark it, and measure the length in millimetres; that number is the European size.
  • Measure at the end of the day, when the finger is at its largest. A ring that fits at nine in the morning will be tight at six.
  • Wide bands fit tighter; add one size for a band over 8 mm.

If you are in Istanbul, come and be measured on the brass ring gauge. It takes a minute and saves a remake.

Care, plainly

  • Silver tarnishes. Wear it, and it tarnishes less; store it dry and closed.
  • A silver cloth is enough for the surface. Do not dip a piece with a stone in silver cleaner.
  • Take the ring off before swimming, gardening or lifting weights. Chlorine and abrasion do more damage than time.
  • If a stone loosens, stop wearing it and bring it in. A tightened bead is a ten-minute job; a lost stone is a new stone.
  • Re-polishing is free for the life of the piece. Post it back, or come to the han.
