About the Dancer

Twelve years under one guru

I began at seven, in a practice room in Thrissur with a cracked mirror and a teacher who believed slowness was a form of respect. Everything on this page — the training, the grammar, the costume — is what stands behind the ninety-three minutes of a full margam.

The Training

One teacher, from seven to nineteen

My guru, Smt. Saraswathy Amma, took me at seven years old and kept me for twelve. There was no syllabus printed anywhere. There was her stick on the floor for the talam, her singing for the music, and her rule that no student touches an item until the adavus are in the body the way walking is in the body.

I made my arangettam at sixteen, began assisting her classes at eighteen, and danced my first full solo margam — the same six items I perform today — at nineteen. She still sits in the front row when I dance in Thrissur. I still glance at her before the varnam. Some habits are not technique; they are gratitude.

When organisers ask for my credentials, this is the honest list: one guru, twelve years, 214 performances, eleven students presented. Mohiniyattam has no certificates worth the paper. It has lineages, and mine is a short, straight line I am proud of.

The Road

Year by year

  • Age 7

    First class. Adavus only — taganam, dhaganam, jaganam, sammisram — for the first two years. My guru's position was that a child who cannot hold a posture cannot hold a story.

  • Age 12

    First items. Cholkettu and a keerthanam, danced at a school youth festival in Thrissur. I forgot the second half of the keerthanam and improvised; my guru noticed, the judges did not. We worked on memory for a year.

  • Age 16

    Arangettam. My debut — a full margam before two hundred people, with my guru compering. The lamp, the new chilanka, my mother's tears in the front row: the template of every debut I now present for my own students.

  • Age 19

    First solo margam. Booked in my own name at a temple festival in Guruvayur. I have performed the full margam every season since.

  • Age 24

    Teaching begins. My guru handed me her Saturday beginners. I have taught every week since — thirty-eight students so far, eleven of them to arangettam.

  • Today

    Thrissur, and wherever the lamps are lit. Temple festivals, cultural programmes, college stages and family functions across Kerala, with my regular musicians. The Pooram closing item is the fixed point of my year.

The Dance, Plainly

What you are actually watching

Mohiniyattam is Kerala's solo dance of the enchantress — Mohini, the female form of Vishnu. Its nature is lasya: grace, sway and softness, built on movements that rise and fall like paddy in wind. Four ideas explain nearly everything on stage.

Andolika — the sway
The torso moves in a slow pendulum arc, side to side, like a palm in sea wind. It is the first thing a student learns and the last thing she perfects; the whole dance hangs from it.
Chuzhippu — the whirl
Turning movements that spiral the body through space. In Mohiniyattam they are rounded and unhurried — a whirlpool, not a spinning top.
Adavu — the steps
The alphabet of the dance, in four families: taganam, dhaganam, jaganam and sammisram. Every item ever choreographed is these steps, combined and recombined, the way sentences are made of letters.
Abhinaya — the telling
Story carried by the face, eyes and hands. A padam may hold a single verse for fifteen minutes while the dancer's expression turns it over — longing, doubt, teasing, surrender — like a stone in a stream.

The Costume & The Musicians

White, gold, and four people to my right

The kasavu sari
Off-white cotton or silk with a woven gold border, worn as a settu mundu with the pleated fan opened at the front. The gold is real zari in a good sari and catches the lamplight — the costume was designed for lamp-lit temple courtyards, and it still works best there.
Jasmine and gold
The hair is drawn into a bun at the side, ringed with fresh jasmine — in Thrissur we string it the same morning. Temple jewellery at the throat and ears, and chilanka, bells, at the ankles: the dancer's own percussion.
The vocalist
Leads the music and carries the words of every item. In Mohiniyattam the singer is the dancer's second voice — I choose items with my vocalist, never without her.
Mridangam, edakka, veena
The mridangam holds the talam; the edakka — Kerala's own hourglass drum — answers it; the veena threads melody under the voice. Flute joins us for larger halls. All four are booked as one unit in the fee table, because they rehearse as one unit.
