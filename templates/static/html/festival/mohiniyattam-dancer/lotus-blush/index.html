The Repertoire

Six items, in the order they are danced

A Mohiniyattam evening — the margam — follows a set path: salutation, the great varnam, slow story, song, play, farewell. These are the items I perform, with the durations I actually take. Nothing here is padded and nothing is rushed.

ItemWhat it isRagam · TalamMinutes
Cholkettu The opening salutation — pure dance built on chollu, spoken rhythmic syllables, as the dancer greets the stage, the lamps and the audience. Chollu · Adi 12
Varnam The centre of the evening. A heroine speaks to her absent lord; story and pure dance alternate, verse by verse, for over half an hour. Shankarabharanam · Adi 35
Padam Slow abhinaya — one feeling held and turned in the light. A woman waiting at dusk, counting the lamps, deciding whether she is angry or only tired. Khamas · Misra Chapu 15
Keerthanam A devotional song — Krishna as the child of Ambadi, stealing butter and hearts in equal measure. Narrative, tender, unhurried. Begada · Adi 10
Javali The light item. Playful and teasing, quicker in the feet — the smile before the close of the evening. Kapi · Adi 9
Tillana The farewell — pure dance again, brisk and patterned, ending with a salutation to the audience that raised the dancer. Dhanashree · Adi 12

The full margam is 93 minutes of dance. With a 15-minute interval the evening runs 108 minutes — most temple and festival slots hold it comfortably, and the planner below checks yours exactly.

How an Evening Begins

First, the lamps

Every performance I give begins the same way, whether the stage is a temple courtyard in Thrissur or a black-box theatre in Bengaluru: the brass lamp is placed at the front of the stage, lit, and the musicians take their places to the right — vocalist at the centre, mridangam and edakka beside, veena where the light catches it.

It is not decoration. The lamp is the first witness of the dance, older than any hall I will ever perform in. Organisers sometimes ask if we can skip it for time. We cannot — but it takes four minutes, and I have counted them into every running time on this page.

The closing item at the Pooram

The askThe festival committee wanted a full margam as the closing item of the cultural programme — after a day of percussion ensembles loud enough to shake the temple roof tiles.

What I did · The outcomeI danced all six items as the lamps were lit around the grounds, and held the padam at its full length even when the programme ran forty minutes late. Three thousand people who had come for drums stayed, and went quiet. The committee has booked the same slot for next year.

"The grounds were loud all day. She made three thousand people quiet with one raised eyebrow — that is the Pooram performance we will measure the others against."

— K. R. Venugopal, programme convener, Thrissur Pooram cultural committee

The forty-minute wedding eve

The askA family wanted Mohiniyattam at their daughter's wedding eve — but the hall gave us exactly forty minutes between functions, not a minute more.

What I did · The outcomeWe did not hurry anything. I dropped three items and danced cholkettu, padam and tillana whole — 39 minutes, rehearsed to the minute with my musicians. The bride's grandmother, who had danced in her youth, wept through the padam and blessed the musicians after. The family wrote to say it was the part of the wedding their guests still mention.

Presenting my student's arangettam

The askDevika, my student of six years, was ready for her arangettam — her debut as a solo dancer. Her family wanted it done properly, but modestly.

Teaching

Classes in Thrissur

I teach the way I was taught: one guru, small classes, the adavus before the items, and no stage until the basics are in the body. Beginners start with the four families of adavu — taganam, dhaganam, jaganam, sammisram — and a great deal of patience.

Group classes: ₹1,500 a month, Saturday and Sunday mornings, Thrissur.
Private lessons: ₹800 an hour, at my practice room or your home within the town.
Arangettam preparation: a two-year path, priced from ₹60,000 all-in — see the fee table above.

Children may start at seven. Adults may start whenever they stop saying they are too old — my oldest beginner was sixty-one, and she danced her first cholkettu at sixty-three.
