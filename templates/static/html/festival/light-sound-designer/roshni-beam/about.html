Lakshmi Das · light & sound · Kolkata

I started in a black box theatre with eight lamps and a borrowed desk

Twelve years later the rooms are bigger and the desks are better, but the job is the same: decide where the audience looks, and make the sound arrive at the right moment.

Physics, then theatre, then clubs

I studied physics at Jadavpur because I liked optics, and spent every evening in a theatre group in Jadavpur that had eighty seats and a lighting rig held together with gaffer tape. I was the person who offered to fix the dimmer, and then the person who was asked to light the next play, and then the person who stopped going to physics lectures.

The first show I lit had fourteen cues and one of them was wrong. The audience never knew; the director did, and told me afterwards, kindly, that a cue is a promise. I have written every cue down since.

Theatre taught me darkness and timing. Clubs taught me speed and how to work with a room full of people who are not watching the stage. Festivals taught me to design for daylight, dust, generators and a rigging crew I have just met. All three are the same job with different constraints.

Lighting is not about making the stage bright. It is about deciding, second by second, where the audience is allowed to look.

How a show is made

I read the script before I meet anyone. Then I watch a rehearsal with the set, not with the actors — I am looking for where the light will fall and where the darkness needs to be. The design starts as a plot on graph paper: fixture positions, angles, colours, and the six or seven moments that will need a cue.

In the tech, I focus every fixture myself with a ladder and a torch, because a focus is a decision and cannot be delegated to a number. The cue stack is written during the dress rehearsal, in the dark, with one finger on the GO button and a pencil in my mouth. After the first preview I cut about a fifth of the cues. There are always too many.

What I will not do

  • Run a strobe without a warning at the door and in the programme, and never above 10 Hz.
  • Blind the front row to make a photograph look good.
  • Put haze in a room with no ventilation, whatever the promoter says.
  • Hang a fixture without a safety bond, even for one night.
  • Operate a show from a position where I cannot see the stage.
On hearingI mix at levels I would sit through myself, and I keep earplugs in the case for the crew. A club night that leaves the staff deaf by 2 a.m. is a badly designed night, not a loud one.
Practice file

Twelve years, in order

The rooms got bigger, the budgets did not always follow, and the cue count went down as I got better.

  • 2009 – 2013

    Jadavpur, physics and a black box

    Eighty-seat theatre, eight lamps, a borrowed two-scene desk. Learned to focus from a ladder and to write a cue sheet by hand.

  • 2013 – 2015

    Assistant LD, theatre circuit

    Academy Theatre, Padatik, Gyan Manch. Carried cable, plotted, and learned how a good LD talks to a director.

  • 2014

    First club rig

    A basement on Park Street with a low ceiling and a resident DJ. Four movers and a lesson in heat.

  • 2017

    First festival main stage

    Kolkata International Festival, daylight until the third act. Designed two shows: one for the sun and one for the dark.

  • 2021

    Pandemic, and projection

    Streamed theatre with no audience. Learned projection mapping and how to light for a camera instead of a room.

  • 2024 – 2026

    Bonbibi, and a studio

    A two-hour play with 46 cues, a club residency, and a small studio in Lansdowne where the desks live between shows.

The kit, and why it is small

I travel with a desk, a laptop, an interface and two small movers. Everything else is hired in the city where the show is, because a flight case is a tax on a design and local hire keeps the money in the local crew.

The plot is drawn in Vectorworks and the cue stack is written in the desk, but the working document is a printed cue sheet and a pencil. Paper does not need a firmware update in the middle of a show.

Teaching

Two workshops a year for young technicians in Kolkata, run through a theatre group in Jadavpur. Free, six people, one week, and the last exercise is always the same: light a monologue with four lamps and no colour.
