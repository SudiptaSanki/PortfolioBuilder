Sunday kitchen · Mylapore · since 2016

Seva is not a feeling.
It is a schedule.

At 3:40 on a Sunday morning the lights in the Mylapore kitchen come on, the first pot of water goes on the fire, and by seven there are a thousand plates on the tank steps. Nobody asks who is hungry. The rule is simpler than that: if the food is ready, the plate is served.

I am Urvi Krishnan. I keep the roster, the ledger, and the peace between the onion cutter and the sambar cook. The cooking is everyone else’s.

01

The morning,
in order

The roster is written for a thousand plates. On a festival Sunday we double it and start at two.

  1. 03:40

    The lights, then the water

    Two volunteers open the shed and fill the big urli. The first thing on is water, because everything else waits on it. Filtered water is bought in cans on Saturday night; the tap is only for washing.

  2. 04:05

    Vegetables on the floor

    Sixty kilos of onions, forty of tomatoes, and whatever the Koyambedu market sent that week. Four people cut, one weighs, one keeps the floor dry — a wet floor at five in the morning is how people get hurt.

  3. 04:50

    Rice, rinsed three times

    Ninety kilos of ponni rice, rinsed until the water runs clear, then set to soak. The vessel is marked inside with a line of white paint: water to here, rice to there. Nobody has to guess.

  4. 05:30

    Sambar, in the big pot

    Toor dal pressure-cooked in four batches, tamarind from a block, sambar powder ground the week before by two of the older women who will not let anyone else do it. Salt is tasted by two people, never one.

  5. 06:15

    Packing, in threes

    An assembly line of three: rice, sambar, poriyal. The leaf is banana on festival days, steel otherwise. Plates are counted in tens and stacked where the queue will bend, not where it begins.

  6. 06:45

    The first ladle

    Older people and children first, then the queue, then the volunteers. Everyone eats the same food. There is no separate vessel for the kitchen, and that is deliberate.

  7. 08:30

    Washing up, and the ledger

    Plates washed, shed swept, and the day written into the book: how many plates, how much spent, who came, who did not, what ran short. The book is the only thing here that is not shared out.

03

What a plate
actually costs

The fourteen rupees do not include the shed, the gas connection, or anyone’s time. Those are given.

Rice and dal are bought once a month from a wholesaler in Koyambedu who has supplied the kitchen since the first Sunday. The vegetables are bought fresh at four in the morning, by whoever is on market duty, with a fixed ₹6,000 in an envelope.

The sweet is the only line that moves. On a ordinary Sunday it is semiya payasam; on Pongal it is sakkarai pongal cooked in the same urli after the rice, which is why the sweet tastes faintly of the meal before it. Nobody has ever complained.

04

Five rules of the shed

  1. 1

    Nobody is asked why they are hungry. The queue is not a form. If a person comes back for a second plate, they are served a second plate.

  2. 2

    The kitchen eats last, and the same food. No separate vessel, no extra ghee. If it is not good enough for the volunteers it is not good enough for the queue.

  3. 3

    Two people taste the salt. One tongue is a habit; two tongues are a decision. This rule has saved more Sundays than any recipe.

  4. 4

    Count everything, hide nothing. The ledger is open on the shelf and anyone can read it — plates, kilos, rupees. Trust in a kitchen is built out of arithmetic.

  5. 5

    Leave the shed better than you found it. The last volunteer out sweeps, and the first one in makes the coffee. There is no rank in between.

“A kitchen is a promise you keep every week, not a feeling you have once.”

05

Come for one Sunday.

You do not need to cook, and you do not need to bring anything. There is always a job that suits a pair of hands: cutting, packing, carrying, washing, or standing at the end of the queue to say good morning. Most people start with the washing up, because that is where the conversation is.
