The people

A kitchen is a
roster, not a hero.

Forty-six people sign up for a Sunday. Six of them have been coming for more than five years, and they are the reason the rest of us can be useful. This page is mostly about them, and about the shed we all share.

How it started

In 2016 my father died on a Thursday. On the following Sunday my mother cooked for the lane, as families here do, and about sixty people came. The cooking was finished by nine and there was still rice in the pot. My mother looked at the pot and said the sentence that started this: “This should not wait for a death.”

I had spent nine years in logistics, scheduling trucks for a cold-chain company. I knew how to build a roster, how to cost a plate, and how to keep a cold store at four degrees. What I did not know was how to cook for sixty, let alone a thousand. Meenakshi Ammal taught me that, mostly by taking the ladle out of my hand and telling me to watch the salt.

We began with sixty plates on the tank steps, a borrowed gas stove, and a ledger in a school notebook. Ten years later the notebook is four ledgers, the stove is three, and the queue is a thousand people long on an ordinary Sunday. The sentence has not changed.

I still work four days a week, from Tuesday to Friday, because the kitchen should not depend on one person’s salary or one person’s energy. The roster exists so that any Sunday can run without me. It has been tested twice: once by a wedding in the family, once by dengue. Both Sundays happened.

01

The six who
come anyway

Core crew are the people who hold a key to the shed. Everyone else on the roster is a guest, and that is meant as a compliment.

  • MA

    Meenakshi Ammal sambar, since 2016

    Seventy-one, retired from the Hindu Mission Hospital canteen. Grinds the sambar powder at home on Thursdays, refuses help, and has never once let the pot catch. She tastes the salt with the back of a spoon, not the front.

  • SV

    Selvam market duty, since 2016

    At Koyambedu by four, back by five-fifteen with the vegetables and a story about the price of tomatoes. Keeps the ₹6,000 envelope and returns it with the bill folded inside, always in the same order.

  • FB

    Fathima Begum rice and washing, since 2017

    Runs the three-rinse rule and the painted water line on the rice vessel. Also runs the washing-up, which is the job nobody asks for and the one that decides whether the shed is usable next week.

  • AK

    Arjun Kumar gas, weights, lifting, since 2018

    A gym trainer who arrives at four and leaves at nine, having carried more than anyone. He checks both cylinders on Saturday night and keeps a spare regulator in the boot of his scooter.

  • LR

    Lakshmi Rajan packing line, since 2019

    A schoolteacher who turned the packing table into a system of three: rice, sambar, poriyal. She counts in tens aloud, which is why the queue never gets a plate without a spoon.

  • DN

    Devi Nagarajan the ledger, since 2020

    An accountant who took over the books during the pandemic and made them honest to the rupee. She reconciles the envelope, the bills, and the plate count every Sunday before she eats.

02

The shed on
Kutchery Lane

It is 18 feet by 26, with a serving window that opens onto the lane. The layout is the whole trick: a thousand plates move in one direction, from the vegetable floor at the back to the window at the front, and never cross. When we moved in, the first thing we did was paint arrows on the floor.

The urli sits at the centre because everything passes it. The rice vessels are on the left, the sambar pot on the right, and the packing table faces the window so the volunteers can see the queue and pace themselves. The washing area is outside, under a corrugated roof, which is unpleasant in June and the reason the queue moves at all.

We rent the shed from the temple for ₹8,000 a month, paid from donations, never from the food budget. If you are in Mylapore on a Saturday, the door is usually open and someone is cutting something.

The last line is the one I am proudest of. It is not luck; it is the plate count at six-fifteen and the decision to cook the last ten kilos only if the queue is still long at seven.

04

The door is open
at three forty.

You can come and watch before you commit. Stand at the end of the queue, take a plate to someone, or just drink the coffee and see whether the morning suits you. Nobody will put you on a list, and nobody will ask you to sign anything at the door.
