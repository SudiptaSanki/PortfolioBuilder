Cellar Records · Kept since 2021

Three entries from the book.

A cellar book is not a trophy cabinet. It records what was asked, what was decided, and what came out — including the brew that went down the drain. These are the three entries people ask about.

The house bitter, decided blind

Record 01 · Stillage Bitter · January–March 2025

The ask

When the plant went in, the pub needed a house bitter — the beer the regulars would judge everything else against. I could have brewed my own preference and put my name on the pump clip. Instead the ask was put to the people who would drink three firkins of it a week: decide it yourselves, blind.

What was decided

Four versions were brewed across five months and tasted blind, twice, by a panel of fourteen regulars. Version 1 came in at 38 IBU and was called “all elbow”. Version 2 dropped the bitterness but lost the finish. At version 3 the yeast was changed, which cleaned up the middle of the beer; version 4 settled at 32 IBU with the new yeast, and won both tastings. The losing versions were not hidden — their gravities and bitterness are on the same brew sheet as the winner's.

The outcome

Stillage Bitter — OG 1.040, FG 1.010, 3.9% by the formula on the brew sheet — has been on the bar since March 2025 and sells about three firkins a week: 216 pints, out of the 1,296 a brew yields. The panel still meets quarterly, and it still tastes blind. Its secretary's account of the process is on the brew sheet page.

The October festival, run off the cellar book

Record 02 · Three days · 9–11 October 2026

The ask

A three-day festival in the yard: nine guest casks from breweries in the valley alongside our own, long tables, and no ticket — the tariff stayed the tariff. The risk in any festival is not the beer you sell; it is the beer you stillage for hope and pour away on Monday.

What was decided

The stillage was planned from the calculator on the brew sheet page, not from optimism: 24 firkins in total — 24 × 72 = 1,728 pints of capacity — matched against the house's festival trade from the two previous years, with every cask vented in sequence so that no more than eight were ever open inside their three-day window at once. The cellar held 11–13 °C across all three days, logged morning and evening.

The outcome

1,611 pints were sold. Twenty-one firkins were finished clean (21 × 72 = 1,512 pints); the remaining 99 pints came out of the last three casks, and 117 pints went back down the cellar steps and were written off — 1,728 − 1,611 = 117, printed here because the book prints losses the same as sales. The festival covered its costs on the beer with that loss included, not by leaving it out.

The pale ale we poured away

Record 03 · Copperline Pale, batch 14 · June 2026

The ask

There was no client and no event — just a brew of Copperline Pale that would not clear. Cask ale is fined and given time, and by day ten batch 14 was still holding a haze that no amount of cellar patience was going to drop bright.

What was decided

The cause was in the brew sheet, once I read it honestly: a protein rest skipped on a busy brew day, and the finings added too early in compensation — two small shortcuts, one cloudy beer. The decision was the one the book exists for: none of it was sold. A hazy pint explained away as “unfiltered style” would have been a lie with a pump clip on it; the beer was a pale ale, and pale ale pours bright or it does not pour.

The outcome

Sixteen firkins went down the drain and two were never racked — about £540 of malt, hops and energy, written in the loss column in the same hand as everything else. The protein rest is back on the sheet with a box that has to be ticked, and the finings timing is chalked on the cellar wall where the racking happens. Batch 15 poured bright on day six.
