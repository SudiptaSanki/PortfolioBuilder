Mira Sen · owner, buyer, tier of bows

I have bought roses at four in the morning for thirteen years

The shop is on Sarat Bose Road, between a tailor and a stationer. I opened it in 2013 with one bucket, one pair of scissors and a phone that took messages.

Ballygunge, a hotel, and then a bucket of my own

I grew up in Ballygunge, in a flat where my father ran a small printing press and my mother kept a garden on the roof. There were always flowers in the house, but they were marigolds and tuberoses, not roses. Roses were for other people's weddings.

I studied commerce because it was sensible, and then took a job in the flower department of a five-star hotel because it was not. For six years I made arrangements for lobby tables and banquets, learned to work at two in the morning before a wedding, and learned that a rose with a bent neck cannot be saved by any amount of wire.

In 2013 the shop on Sarat Bose Road came free — a stationer was retiring — and I took it with a loan from my mother and a great deal of confidence. The first month I sold eleven bunches. The second month a hotel asked me to do three hundred rooms for Valentine's, and I have not had an empty weekend since.

A rose is a perishable product with a romantic use. You have to respect both facts, in that order.

Where the roses come from

Three growers in Mallickpur, across the river in Howrah, where the soil and the humidity suit roses and the market is close enough to cut in the evening and sell in the morning. I have bought from the same families for a decade. They know I want stems of sixty centimetres with the guard petals on, and they know I will refuse a box that has been sitting in the sun.

The van leaves at half past three. At Mullick Ghat I open the bunches myself, check the heads for botrytis and the stems for crunch, and pay cash. By six the buckets are in the shop and the water is cold. There is no cold room — a cold room would cost more than the roses and would tempt me to keep them too long.

What I will not do

  • Dye a rose blue, or paint the edges gold. It kills the stem and looks like a birthday card.
  • Use glitter, plastic grass or cellophane with printed hearts.
  • Sell a bunch I would not put on my own table.
  • Promise a delivery time on the fourteenth that I cannot keep. I would rather lose the order.
  • Deliver roses in a box without water for more than an hour. They are cut flowers, not biscuits.

Thirteen years, in numbers

The shop is small on purpose. Two people can tie a hundred bunches in a day, and a hundred is about the most that can be done without the bows going crooked.

How to keep a bunch alive

Cut two centimetres off the stems at an angle when you get home, in a basin of water, not in the air. Strip any leaf that would sit below the waterline. Change the water every second day and cut again on the third. Keep the vase out of direct sun and away from a ripening fruit bowl — the gas from fruit ages a rose faster than heat does.

If a head droops, do not throw it away yet. Wrap the stem straight in newspaper, stand it in deep cool water for four hours, and it will often come back. This works about half the time and is worth trying.

The seven-day promiseIf a bunch I sold you collapses before a week, bring back the stems and I will replace it. It has happened eleven times in thirteen years, and each time it was my fault at the market.
