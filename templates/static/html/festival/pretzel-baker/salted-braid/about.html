The baker · third generation

Three Kerns, one bench, seventy-two years.

My grandfather opened this shop with one oven and a bicycle. The oven has changed once. The recipe has not changed at all.

I am Jonas Kern.

I started at this bench when I was nine, not baking but counting: sixty brezeln onto a tray, twelve by five, until my hands knew the grid better than my eyes. At fifteen I was allowed to shape. At nineteen I went to bakery school in Ulm and came back with a certificate and a strong opinion about dough temperature. My father let me keep the certificate.

We are a small bakery, which is a polite way of saying that four of us do everything. Anna runs the counter and the coffee. Mehmet shapes faster than anyone I have met, including my father. Elias does the Friday rye and the deliveries. I mix at 03:40, bake at 06:15, and by ten I am usually at the counter explaining why the butterbrezel is finished.

A brezel is not a lifestyle. It is breakfast. It should cost a coin, fit in a coat pocket, and make the walk to work better. Jonas Kern

What the sign does not say

The sign says Bäckerei. It does not say artisan, traditional, handcrafted or sourdough. Those words are true, but they are also what every bakery says now, and a word everyone uses stops meaning anything. The bread has to say it.

What we do say, plainly: the flour comes from a mill in Aalen, the butter is from a dairy in the Allgäu, and the salt is coarse rock salt from the Berchtesgaden mine. Nothing arrives frozen. Nothing is baked twice to look fresh. If we run out at noon, we run out at noon.

The hour before the town wakes

People ask whether baking at four is hard. The getting up is hard. The hour itself is the best part of the day: the oven ticking as it comes up to temperature, the first tray going in, the smell reaching the street before the door is unlocked. By the time the first customer arrives, the work is mostly done and the shop is warm. There is a particular quiet at 06:10 that I would not trade for an extra two hours in bed.

Then and now

  • 1954

    Ernst Kern opens the shop

    One room, one wood-fired oven, a bicycle for deliveries. The first day's takings are 11 marks and 40 pfennig. The ledger is in the drawer by the till.

  • 1978

    The deck oven arrives

    My father Matthias takes over and buys the three-deck stone oven that is still the heart of the bakery. It is serviced every February and has never missed a Saturday.

  • 1996

    Five tables next door

    The neighbouring shoe repair closes. We knock through, add five tables and a coffee machine, and learn that people will sit down if you give them a chair.

  • 2016

    I come home

    After bakery school in Ulm and a year in a Munich bakery that baked 4,000 pieces a day, I come back to 610. My father retires from the oven but keeps the accounts and the opinions.

  • 2024

    Seventy years

    We bake the anniversary batch with the 1954 recipe, weigh it on the 1954 scale, and sell it for 1.40 — the same as any other Tuesday.

House rules

Four things we will not bend.

  • 01

    Weigh everything

    Not just the dough. The salt, the yeast, the lye. A brezel that is three grams heavy has a belly that bakes unevenly, and you can taste the laziness.

  • 02

    Lye is not a shortcut

    It is the whole point. We buy food-grade sodium hydroxide, store it locked, and treat it with the respect it demands. There is no substitute that gives the same colour, and we have tried.

  • 03

    Sell out, do not bake twice

    A second bake in the afternoon makes yesterday's method look efficient and today's bread worse. When the board is empty, it is empty.

  • 04

    Free brezel under 1.20 m

    Every child who comes in with a grown-up gets one, no purchase needed. My grandfather started it; we are not going to be the generation that stops.
