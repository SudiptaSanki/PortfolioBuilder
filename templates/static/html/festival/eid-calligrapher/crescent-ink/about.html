The hand

Taught by three
people and one
stubborn reed.

I am Ayaan Qureshi. I write Arabic and Urdu calligraphy in a third-floor room above Moti Mahal lane, where the light comes in from the east and the traffic below is loud enough that I have learned to work through it.

My grandfather sold attar and paper on this lane. The paper came in bundles wrapped in newspaper, and the leftover sheets were mine to ruin. I ruined a great many of them before anyone thought to teach me the alphabet properly.

The first teacher was Ustad Rafiq Ahmed, who ran a sign-painting workshop in Koti and had no patience for ink that was not ground on the stone. He gave me a reed and told me to draw the same alif for a month. I did it for two, because the third week is where the hand stops arguing.

In 2014 I went to Istanbul for eleven months to study thuluth with Hoca Selma Aydın, who taught a room of six students by taking the pen out of our hands and writing the letter herself, once, and then saying nothing. What I learned there was not a style. It was a way of looking at a stroke until you can see what is wrong with it.

Nastaliq came later, and from a harder teacher: the signboards of the old city. A board has to be read from a rickshaw in the rain, so it forgives nothing. I still take one sign-painting job a month to keep the hand honest.

The studio has been in this room since 2016. There is a cupboard of trial sheets going back to 2011, arranged by year, and a drawer of failed commissions that I keep because the failure is usually more instructive than the sheet that left.

A letter is not drawn. It is placed, and then you spend a year learning why it was placed there. Hoca Selma Aydın, Istanbul, 2014

The chain

Three teachers, in the order they arrived

1996 – 2004

Ustad Rafiq Ahmed

Sign painter, Koti. Taught me that a reed is a tool with a grain, and that a bad pen is not an excuse but a lesson in sharpening.

2005 – 2013

Ustad Mirza Baqar

Nastaliq, Hyderabad. Ran the correction sessions at the Idara-e-Adabiyat. Made me copy a page of Ghalib's divan every week and mark my own faults in red.

2014 – 2015

Hoca Selma Aydın

Thuluth and naskh, Istanbul. Eleven months, six students, one letter a week, and a great deal of silence while she looked at the paper.

How the studio works

Rules I keep

These are not a philosophy. They are the things I have learned cost me a sheet when I ignored them.

  1. i

    One sheet, one sitting

    A nikkah nama written across two evenings has a join in it that the couple will see on their wall for the rest of their lives.

  2. ii

    The Arabic is checked first

    If I do not know the source of a line, it does not go on the paper. A misspelt blessing is not a small mistake.

  3. iii

    No copies of copies

    Every commission is drawn for its own wall and its own words. I will not resize a previous piece and call it new.

  4. iv

    Nine a season

    The limit is real. It is the number of sheets I can write without the hand getting tired and the eye getting lazy.
