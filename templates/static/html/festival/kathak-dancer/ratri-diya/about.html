The dancer · Lucknow

Sixteen years,
one room,
five in the morningI was taught in a room above a sweets shop in Aminabad. The tabla player arrived at five; the milkman at seven; the lesson ended when the shop opened.

Ananya Mishra, born 1997 in Lucknow. Trained by Vidushi Kamla Devi from the age of six. Now performs, teaches two classes a week, and still practises at five.

Lineage

Lucknow gharana, through Kamla Devi, who learned from Bindadin Maharaj's line. The gharana's insistence is on nazakat — delicacy — and on abhinaya being understood before it is admired.

Kathak is a living practice, not a replica. Where a teacher and a text disagree, I say so in the programme note.

The room in Aminabad

The room was three metres by four, with a ceiling fan that turned too slowly and a mirror that had been broken and re-silvered twice. There was no spring floor. We danced on cement with a cotton mat, and my guru sat on a stool by the window with a brass glass of water she did not drink from until the lesson was over.

Kamla Devi taught the taal before she taught a single turn. For the first two years I did tatkar — the footwork — for forty minutes at the start of every lesson, with the tabla keeping Teen Taal, and nothing else. I was nine and I thought this was a punishment. It was the whole training.

“If your foot lands on the matra, the audience forgives the rest. If it lands late, nothing else you do will be believed.”

— Kamla Devi, in the Aminabad room, 2011

What the training was, exactly

Six days a week for eleven years, with three breaks for examinations and one for a broken ankle. Two hours in the morning, one in the evening. Tuesdays were for abhinaya; Fridays for the tarana; Sundays for the all-night listening, when she would play recordings and make me count the matras out loud.

I learned seven taals to performance standard, and I use three of them. The rest stay in the practice room, which is not the same as being forgotten: a dancer who cannot count Jhap Taal cannot find the khali in a thumri either.

What I will not do

  • Shorten the nritta to make room for a Bollywood finale. If the evening needs a finale, book a different dancer.
  • Dance on a stage smaller than five by four metres, however good the acoustics. The chakkar needs the floor.
  • Perform without a live tabla player unless the venue has no other option and the programme is announced as recorded.
  • Explain the piece while it is happening. A four-minute spoken introduction at the start, and then the audience is trusted.

Classes

Two classes a week, six students each

Tuesdays and Fridays at five, in the studio in Nakkhas. Beginners start with the taal and the tatkar, and do not learn a turn until the foot is true.
