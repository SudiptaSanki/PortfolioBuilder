The programme

Three parts, in this order, always

Act I

Nrittaनृत्त

Pure movement: the theka in the tabla, the footwork answering it, the chakkars. No meaning to carry, only time to keep. This is where the audience decides whether to trust the rest.

Teen Taal · 16 matras · 9 minutes

Act II

Nrityaनृत्य

Movement with a text: a thumri or a bhajan, the bol in the hands before the feet. The ankle bells become quieter here, which is the hardest thing to teach a young dancer.

Rupak or Dadra · with harmonium and vocal

Act III

Abhinayaअभिनय

The face and the eyes, mostly still. One line of a song held for two minutes. If the hall is quiet at the end of it, the programme is finished; if it is not, I do not add anything.

One padam · one lamp · no percussion

The gharana

Lucknow, and the things it insists on

I trained under Vidushi Kamla Devi of the Lucknow gharana from the age of six, in a room above a sweets shop in Aminabad, with a tabla player who came at five in the morning and left when the milkman came. The gharana's insistence is on nazakat — delicacy — and on the abhinaya being understood before it is admired.

That means a programme built slowly: the footwork first, because it earns the right to the stillness later. It also means the theka is never decoration. If the tabla is playing Teen Taal, my foot is answering all sixteen matras, including the khali, where the sound thins and the audience is supposed to notice.

“The bol is not a syllable to say. It is a place for the foot to land.”

— Kamla Devi, in the Aminabad room, 2011
