The attic, seventh floor, no lift

Fifteen Decembers, and a room that smells of paper and pine

I am Isabella Kovacs. I wrap gifts for a living, which is a strange thing to say at parties, and a very ordinary thing to do for eight hours a day in a room above Neubaugasse.

I learned to wrap in a bookshop, badly

I grew up in Budapest, in a flat where every present was wrapped in newspaper and tied with string saved from the butcher. In 2004 I moved to Vienna to study art history, and took a job in a bookshop on Währinger Straße because it was warm and the staff discount was good. At Christmas the manager handed me a roll of paper and a pair of scissors and said the queue was out of the door.

The first fifty were terrible. The fifty-first was acceptable. By the end of that December I had worked out that a corner is not a fold but a decision, and that a book wrapped in the wrong paper looks like an apology. I have been chasing that feeling ever since: the small, quiet moment when someone turns a parcel over in their hands before they open it.

“The paper matters less than the moment the tape comes off. Everything else is preparation for that.”

How the attic works

The attic is on the seventh floor of a building with no lift, which keeps the rent low and the customers committed. It has a slanted skylight, a long oak table from a school in Favoriten, and a wall of shelves that hold about forty rolls of paper and twelve ribbons. In summer it is unbearable. In December it is the best room in Vienna.

Paper comes from a mill in Lower Austria that has been making kraft since 1912. I buy it in 150-metre rolls, unbleached, 90 gsm, and I pay extra for the batch that has not been re-bleached. Ribbon is bought by the hundred metres from two suppliers in the city; the velvet comes from a passementerie in Ottakring that also repairs theatre costumes. Everything that can be paper is paper. The only plastic in the room is the tape inside the dispenser, and even that is paper tape now, which took three suppliers to find.

Offcuts go into a box by the door. Once a month they go to a primary school in Favoriten where the children use them for collages. It costs me nothing and it saves a bin bag a week, which is the sort of arithmetic that keeps a small business honest.

The room in numbers

I have been asked to open a second studio three times and said no three times. Two rooms would mean two sets of hands I have not trained.

A year upstairs

The work has a season, and the season is short

December is about half the year's income. The rest of the year is shop windows, weddings and the quiet business of wrapping things people could wrap themselves but would rather not.

  1. January – February

    Repair the year: rewind ribbon, mend the table, write to shops about spring windows. The attic is cold and I am mostly alone in it, which I like.

  2. March – May

    Wedding season begins. Favours, table cards, ribbon matched to a fabric swatch the couple brings in a plastic bag. Somebody always cries at the sample.

  3. June – August

    Shop windows and hampers for summer launches. The attic reaches 34 °C. Paper behaves badly in the heat, so I work early and keep the skylight open.

  4. September – October

    Corporate orders are designed and sampled. Every year someone asks for four hundred boxes by 1 December, and every year I write the date in the book and say a small prayer.

  5. November

    The run. Paper by the pallet, ribbon by the kilometre, two quiet helpers on Thursdays. The order book closes in the first week if it has to.

  6. December

    The attic opens to the public on Tuesdays to Saturdays. I wrap until the light goes, then wrap under the lamp. On the 24th I close at noon and sleep for two days.

What I will not do

  • Wrap in plastic film, even when a client asks for it for a courier. I will use a paper bag and a box instead.
  • Print a logo onto ribbon. It ruins the ribbon and the ribbon gets thrown away.
  • Use a bow that cannot be untied. A gift should not have to be cut open.
  • Promise a date I cannot keep in the last week of December. I would rather lose the order.
  • Wrap anything alive, leaking, or still warm from the oven.
If you are late There is a bell at the street door and a box outside the attic door for finished parcels. Ring twice; the lift does not come this high.
