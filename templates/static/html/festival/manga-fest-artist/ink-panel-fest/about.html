Interview聞き書き

“The festival is not a subject. It is a deadline.”

Mio Tachibana draws festivals in ink, on site, in a B6 sketchbook, then turns the notes into pages at a desk above a print shop in Nishikujō. This interview was recorded in her studio in February 2026, over two pots of tea.

Mio Tachibana
b. 1991, Kyoto
Kyoto Seika University, manga, 2014
2,140 pages drawn
Studio: Nishikujō
Represented by herself
Q

Why festivals, of all the things to draw?

My grandmother sold grilled mochi at a stall near the Nishikujō market. Every July she took me to Gion because she said a child should see the floats before they understood how much they cost. I was six. What I remember is not the float — it is the men pulling the rope, and how they were all shouting at once and none of them was in charge.

When I started drawing seriously I tried to make stories about people in rooms. They were fine. But I kept going back to the street, because a festival gives you something a room does not: a fixed deadline. It will happen, and then it will be over, and you cannot draw it later from memory.

Q

You draw standing up, in a crowd. How does that work?

Badly, at first. Now it is a method. I use a brush pen because a pencil makes people stop and ask what you are doing; a brush looks like a tourist thing and nobody cares. I draw the shape of the movement, not the person. A yamaboko being pulled has a particular lean, and if you get the lean the float draws itself.

The sketchbook is B6, soft cover, so it bends in a pocket. Forty minutes in one place is the limit before someone asks me to move. I write the sound in the margin: the wooden clappers, the whistle, the specific way the crowd goes quiet when the float turns.

“You cannot draw the festival. You can draw what you noticed before it moved on, and hope that is enough for the reader.”
Q

Why still ink, when the tone is digital?

The line is the only place where my hand is visible. If the line is a stylus line, the reader sees a line that could have been made by anyone, and the drawing loses its authority. So the contour is a maru pen or a brush, always. Tone and gradation are digital because I am not romantic about cutting screentone with a knife at two in the morning.

I also letter by hand. Balloons are drawn, not typeset. A typeset balloon sits on the page like a label on a jar; a hand-drawn one has a wobble and a direction, and the reader reads it with their eye before they read the words.

Q

What does a festival commission actually involve?

Usually a programme, a free paper, or a publisher who wants a reportage book. I ask for press access, a place to stand that is not in the way, and permission to draw the people whose faces I use. If I cannot get the third thing, I draw them from behind, or not at all.

The pages come back to the office in a week. They get a fact-check from someone who was there — usually the festival office, sometimes a neighbour who has been going for forty years and remembers which lantern goes where. I would rather be corrected by a neighbour than praised by a stranger.

Q

What will you not draw?

Sacred things used as decoration. A festival is not a backdrop; the people in it are not extras. I do not draw the inside of a shrine where the camera is not allowed, and I do not draw a private ritual for a paying client. And I do not draw children in a way that turns them into a symbol of the festival's innocence — they are just people standing in the heat.

Born

1991, Kyoto. Raised in Nishikujō, five minutes from the Katsura river.

Training

Kyoto Seika University, manga course, 2014. Two years as an assistant on a weekly, drawing backgrounds.

Tools

Maru pen, kabura pen, a no. 6 brush pen, B4 manuscript paper, Deleter tone, Clip Studio for the gradients.

Shown

“Yoiyama, 21:40” — group show, Kyoto International Manga Museum, 2025. “Six Fires” — Kyoto Art Center, 2024.

Tea

Two pots. She made the second one after the third question and would not let me leave before it was finished.
