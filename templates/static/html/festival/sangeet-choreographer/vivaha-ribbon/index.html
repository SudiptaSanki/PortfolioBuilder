    Anatomy of a set

    Four minutes is twelve decisions

    Every item I build has the same skeleton. The family decides the song and the story; I decide where the counts sit and who is standing where when the beat drops.

    1. Walk-on 4 counts

      The most under-rehearsed part of any sangeet. Eight people walk on from one wing, find their mark, and stand still. If this is wrong, the audience decides the item is amateur before the music starts.

    2. Hook, first time 16 counts

      Everyone on stage, one simple repeated phrase, no formation change. This is the part the uncles learn in ten minutes and remember on the night. It has to be good enough to carry the item alone.

    3. Couple's verse 12 counts

      Everyone else freezes or sways. The couple gets the centre, the light and the only lift of the evening. Choreographed last, because it depends on what the two of them can actually do in a lehenga and a sherwani.

    4. Formation change 16 counts

      One clean change — a V, a circle, a diagonal — walked twice in every rehearsal. More than two changes in a four-minute item is how families end up colliding at the chorus.

    5. Last chorus & bow to the end

      Full stage, biggest phrase, then the bow on a fixed count so the photographer gets the frame. The bow is printed on the count sheet in bold. Nobody leaves the stage until the last note is over.

    Before the first rehearsal, I ask four things

    1. One song, or a medley of three?Medleys look generous and rehearse like three separate items. I will tell you honestly which one your family can carry in the time you have.
    2. Who absolutely must be on stage?Grandmothers, a brother flying in on the morning, a cousin who refuses. The set is built around the fixed people first.
    3. How does the family feel about being funny?Some families want the laugh, some want the tear. Both work. Mixing them without warning does not.
    4. What is the stage, actually?A photograph of the empty venue with something for scale. Ballroom stages are often four feet narrower than the hotel's floor plan claims.
