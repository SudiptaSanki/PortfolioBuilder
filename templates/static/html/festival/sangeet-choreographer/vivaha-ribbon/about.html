The method

Nobody in a sangeet
is a dancer.
That is the starting point.

I am not a dance teacher who tolerates beginners. Beginners are the whole job — the uncle who counts in his head, the cousin who has never been on a stage, the bride who is being watched by four hundred people and cannot hear the track.

I trained in Bharatanatyam from the age of six to twenty-two, which taught me discipline and very little about staging a family. The staging came later, from nine years of choreographing for film launches and corporate shows in Mumbai, where the client always wanted thirty people on stage and always said yes to a lift.

A sangeet is the opposite problem. Nobody is auditioning, nobody is being paid, and everybody has an opinion. The work is not choreography in the usual sense — it is translation. I take a song the family loves and turn it into a sequence of eight-counts that a fifty-year-old aunt can learn in three evenings and still enjoy on the night.

Counts before choreography

Every item begins with numbers: song length, tempo, eight-counts, phrase length. I write the count sheet before I teach a single step. If a routine has 48 counts and the family can only rehearse four times, the item has to fit 12 counts per rehearsal, or the set gets shorter. I have cut whole songs the week before a wedding because the numbers said so, and the family was relieved.

Video for the people who miss a session

In every family there is one person who is in Hyderabad until the tenth, and two who work nights. After each rehearsal I send a slow reference of the phrase filmed from the front, then the same phrase at tempo filmed from the side. No one has to confess they missed a session. They learn from the video and arrive on Sunday already caught up.

The stage is part of the choreography

The difference between a good sangeet and a chaotic one is usually two feet of stage the hotel did not mention. I ask for a photograph of the empty venue with someone standing on it, and I build the formations around the walk-on wing, the speaker placements and where the couple enters. The set you see on the builder is what I would draw for that stage, not a generic diagram.

I take six to eight sangeets a year, in Mumbai and travelling to Pune, Delhi and Bengaluru. Between November and February, book eight months ahead. For the rest of the year, two weeks is usually enough notice.

What I will not do, politely

  1. Promise thirty people in four rehearsalsIf the numbers do not work, the set gets shorter. Nobody's memory improves because the wedding is in ten days.
  2. Choreograph a lift for a dancer who has not rehearsed it twiceI have seen a groom's shoulder go out at the rehearsal. It is not worth the photograph.
  3. Add a segment the week of the weddingThere is always one more cousin who wants to dance. They can join the final chorus, which is already staged for extra people.
  4. Take over a set someone else choreographedUnless there is time to re-teach it from the count sheet, which there usually is not. I will say no and mean it.
  5. Change the song after the first rehearsalThe family has already learned the hook. Changing the song is not a creative decision at that point, it is a way of losing two weeks.
  6. Let the audience on stageThey will want to. The bow is the ending. After the bow the stage is empty and the bar is open.

Our family had four left feet between us and Rhea never once made anyone feel stupid. She built the set around the people who were terrified, not the three who could dance, and by the last rehearsal the uncles were asking for one more run.
