About

Eleven years of faces that stay faces

I am not a transformation artist. I am the person who makes the girl in the mirror look like the girl her mother knows, on the most photographed day of her life.

Trained at a Chennai salon, then four years assisting a film makeup artist. Neither taught me brides; brides taught me brides.

The first bride I did was my cousin, in 2015, for a muhurtham at six in the morning. I had assisted on film sets for four years and thought I knew what I was doing. I put a foundation on her that was half a shade too light, and in every photograph of the ceremony her face is a different colour from her neck.

She has never complained. I have looked at that photograph before every trial since.

What I believe

A bride should look like herself. Not a better version, not a filtered one — herself, in good light, with the colour the sari asked for. The face must survive a hot room, a garland, a hundred relatives and a photographer who wants one more shot under the mandapam.

That means skin first: hydration, a light correction, a base matched at the jaw and checked at the door. Then colour, and only as much as the face will carry at six in the morning.

What I will not do

  • Skin lightening, or any product sold as “fairness”. If a client asks, I explain why the answer is no.
  • Borrowed portfolio images. Every photograph on this site is a bride who agreed, and none of them is a stock face.
  • More than one wedding a day. A double day is possible for one bride with two ceremonies; it is not possible for two brides.
  • Twenty people in one morning. Four family members is the most I can do properly with one assistant.

The kit, honestly

Two cases: one of colour, one of tools. Cream and liquid foundations in eighteen shades, three powder tones, twelve blushes, forty lips (I use about six), one airbrush that comes out only when the venue is humid. Nothing in the kit is there because a brand paid for it to be.

I buy my own product, I replace mascara every three months, and I will tell you what I am putting on your face and why, in the trial, before it goes anywhere near your eyes.

The years

How it went, roughly in order

There is no dramatic break in this story. There is a lot of early mornings, and a slow accumulation of brides who sent their sisters.

  1. 2011
    Salon floor

    Two years of blow-dries and threading in a Chennai salon, learning to talk to people before touching their faces.

  2. 2013
    Film sets

    Four years assisting a film makeup artist. Learn speed, learn to work under bad light, learn that nobody waits for you.

  3. 2015
    First bride

    A cousin's muhurtham in Mylapore, one shade too light, and a photograph I still keep on the studio wall.

  4. 2018
    The studio

    A room in Alwarpet with north light and a chair that reclines. Trials happen here with the sari, not over the phone.

  5. 2021
    Photograph every trial

    The rule that changed the work: trial in daylight, photographed, looked at together before anything is agreed.

  6. 2024
    Sixty brides

    The year the calendar filled from January to December by August, and I stopped taking bookings I could not do properly.

The studio

North light, one chair, no reception desk

Trials are at 10 am because that is when the light through the north window is honest. Bring the sari, the jewellery, and one person whose opinion you trust.

The trial

Two hours · included in bridal

We try the base, one eye, one lip, and photograph it in daylight and under warm light. Whatever fails, we change before the wedding, not on the morning.

The morning

Four hours · two assistants

I arrive before the light. Hair and family are handled by the assistants; the bride's face is mine from first cleanse to last touch-up at the mandapam.

After

The photographs

I ask for three photographs from the photographer afterwards, because the camera sees things the mirror does not, and the next bride benefits.
