One box, one bass hand, thirty years of Saturday nights.

Mira Kolar learned to play in a parish hall in Cleveland's St. Clair-Superior neighborhood, on a borrowed instrument that had been in the choir loft since the 1960s. She has been returning the favour ever since.

m. 07

How it started

The first box was a two-row Hohner with a cracked bass board and a smell of candle wax. Mira was eleven, the parish was raising money for a new furnace, and the man who usually played had broken his wrist. She knew four tunes and the names of three chords. She played for two hours.

What she remembers is not the music but the mechanics of the room: how the dancers moved closer when the tempo dropped, how the hall went quiet for the waltz before the toast, how a bad microphone could empty a floor faster than a wrong note. That education never really ended. Every hall still teaches it.

The instrument, honestly

A diatonic button accordion is not a small piano accordion. Each row of buttons belongs to one key, and each button carries two notes: one when the bellows close, another when they open. The push side of a C row gives the tonic triad; the pull side gives the rest of the scale. The left hand works the same logic — tonic chord on the push, dominant on the pull — which is why a diatonic box breathes in phrases rather than sitting on a chord.

Mira plays a two-row B/C box with three reed banks, low, middle and high, and twelve folds in the bellows. The B row sits a semitone above the C row, so the two rows together cover the chromatic gaps a single row cannot. The instrument was rebuilt in 2019 by a workshop in Ljubljana: new wax, new valves, the original reeds kept.

What she plays

The book is Cleveland style: Slovenian and Croatian tunes learned from parish musicians, a few Bohemian favourites, and the American swing that crept into the polka halls in the 1950s. The tempos are dance tempos, not concert tempos. A polka that cannot be danced to is a performance, and this is not a performance.

She writes too, mostly tunes named after places: the East Side, Lodge 158, the stretch of the Cuyahoga where the summer festivals used to be held. Two of them have entered the local book, which is the only kind of fame that matters at a Saturday dance.

"Mira plays the room down for the toast, then has every generation on the floor for the last hour."

Joan Mihelič, hall stewardSt. Jerome's, Cleveland

The long way round

  1. 1994

    First parish dance

    Two hours, four tunes, a broken furnace fund. The regular player came back the next month and let her sit in on the second set.

  2. 1998

    Lessons that stuck

    Six months of Saturday mornings with a retired bandleader who taught by making her play the bass hand alone until the bellows stopped guessing.

  3. 2003

    First trip to Slovenia

    Two weeks in Prekmurje and Štajerska, playing dances in village halls and learning how much of the Cleveland style had drifted and how much had not.

  4. 2011

    The trio becomes a working band

    Tuba and tenor sax joined for the big halls. The arrangement rule has not changed since: the accordion carries the melody, the tuba carries the floor, the sax carries the space between.

  5. 2019

    The box is rebuilt

    New wax, valves and straps in Ljubljana. The reeds were kept, including the third button that has been taped since a wedding in 2004.

  6. 2024

    Tuesday button-box class

    Nine students, ages fourteen to seventy-one, in the back room of a Slovenian hall. The first rule: learn the bass hand before the melody, or the box will always sound unsure.

m. 09

How a date works

The band is put together for the room, not the other way round. A parish hall for two hundred does not need a five-piece; a festival tent for eight hundred does.

01

Solo

One box and one microphone. Best for dinners, anniversaries, small halls and the first hour while people arrive.

02

Trio

Accordion, tuba and tenor sax. The standard dance band for parish halls and lodge nights, up to about three hundred people.

03

Five-piece

Adds banjo and a second accordion. For big halls, tent festivals and any night where the floor needs to stay full past midnight.

04

Sets

Two sets of forty-five minutes with a break, or three of thirty for a wedding. The last polka is always the same.

05

Sound

One condenser microphone for the box and a shared vocal microphone for the band. No backing tracks, no click.

06

Learning a tune

Requests need a month and a recording, if one exists. It will be learned in the right key and the right tempo, not busked.

m. 10

Bring the box to your hall

Send the date, the room and roughly how many people will be standing on the floor. The rest can be worked out by phone.
