The practice

I am a musician who learned to stop performing.

Trained in Hindustani vocal and Nada Yoga in Pune and Rishikesh. Fifteen years at the bowl, seven of them in hospital rooms. I work alone, in small rooms, with no recorded music.

Study

  • Nada Yoga, 900-hour, Rishikesh
  • Hindustani vocal, 12 years, Pune
  • Oncology massage foundation, 2022
  • Trauma-informed practice, 2024

I came to the bowl through singing, not through wellness. For eleven years I sang khayal in a small riyaaz room behind Lakshmi Road, and the thing I loved most was not the melody. It was the moment after the last note, when the room kept going without you.

In 2011 I bought a bowl in Patan because I liked the hammer marks. I did not know how to play it. A teacher in Rishikesh, an old man named Bhaskar who had no interest in the word “healing”, showed me that a bowl is struck once and then it is not yours any more. That is the whole technique, and it took me four years to accept it.

What a session is, precisely

A session is a room, a mat, and one person lying down. I place between four and nine bowls — on the sternum, the belly, the thighs, beside the ears, sometimes under the knees. Each is struck with a padded mallet and allowed to decay fully before the next. There is no music playing, no scent, no guided visualisation, and nothing that requires you to believe anything.

The sound does two things that I can defend. It masks the room's small noises, which is why people who live in loud homes find it restful. And its slow decay gives attention something to follow that does not demand a response. A nervous system that has been braced for months can sometimes take that as permission.

What I cannot claim is that it treats a disease. It does not. It is not a substitute for medication, therapy, or a diagnosis, and I will not work with anyone who is using it as one.

“The bowl is not the medicine. The quiet is not the medicine either. What happens is that for forty minutes nobody asks anything of you, and your body remembers that this is allowed.”

Who it suits

Most of the people who come are not unwell in any diagnosable way. They are tired, or they cannot sleep, or they are grieving someone and have run out of ways to say so. Others are in treatment: chemotherapy, palliative care, recovery from surgery. I work alongside their doctors at Sahyadri and at two hospices in Pune, and I stop when a clinician asks me to stop.

It is not suitable for anyone in an acute psychotic episode, anyone who finds sustained low sound distressing, or anyone who cannot lie flat for an hour without pain. First-trimester pregnancy is fine with a doctor's agreement; I avoid placing bowls on the abdomen then.

How touch is handled

Bowls rest on clothing, never on skin. I say before we begin exactly where each will go, and you may refuse any placement without explaining. A session can be done entirely off the body if that is what you prefer — the sound carries through air perfectly well. There is always a bell beside your hand, and if you ring it I stop immediately without discussion.

What training means here

There is no licence for sound healing in India, and I am wary of the ones sold abroad. What I have is 900 hours of Nada Yoga study under Swami Atmananda in Rishikesh, twelve years of vocal training with Vidya Kulkarni in Pune, a foundation certificate in oncology massage from the Pune Cancer Centre, and a trauma-informed practice supervision group that meets monthly. I keep a log of every session, and I refer people to a psychotherapist or a physician when the conversation points there.

I charge for time and not for outcome. A session costs the same whether it helps or not, which is the honest arrangement.

A short, unglamorous chronology

  1. 2002–2013

    Khayal, in a room behind Lakshmi Road

    Twelve years of daily riyaaz with Vidya Kulkarni. Bad posture, good ears, no career plan.

  2. 2011

    The first bowl

    Bought in Patan, Nepal, for the hammer pattern. Learned to strike it properly four years later, from Bhaskar Prasad in Rishikesh.

  3. 2016

    Nada Yoga training

    900 hours with Swami Atmananda, Rishikesh. Overtone chant, nādānusandhāna, and a great deal of sitting still.

  4. 2019

    First hospital work

    Invited into the palliative ward at Sahyadri by Dr. Leena Kurien. Two sessions a week, still free, still the centre of the practice.

  5. 2022–24

    Oncology massage and trauma training

    A foundation certificate at Pune Cancer Centre, then two years of supervision in trauma-informed bodywork. The training that changed how I speak in a session.

  6. 2026

    The listening room

    Studio 3 opens in Model Colony: one room, thirteen bowls, twelve seats, no recordings.
