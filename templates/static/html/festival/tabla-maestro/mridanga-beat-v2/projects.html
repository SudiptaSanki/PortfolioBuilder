Engagements · The work

What was asked. What I played. What came of it.

An accompanist's portfolio cannot be a pile of posters. These are four engagements told the way I would tell them to the next organiser who calls — including the parts that were difficult.

Festival accompaniment

Sawai Gandharva Bhimsen Mahotsav — December 2025

Asked by: the festival, for khayal vocalist Vidushi Shalini Bhide · Talas: teental (vilambit & drut), jhaptal · Rehearsals: two

The ask. Accompany a singer I had met once, in the festival's evening session, through a vilambit teental slow enough that the cycle takes over half a minute to come home. What I played. Mostly restraint: theka like a held hand, the dayan tuned a shade under her sa so the drum sat behind the voice, answers only where a phrase ended with its palm up. In the drut, I let the rela run exactly once — a soloist's sentence in an accompanist's paragraph. The outcome. The organisers' letter mentioned not the fast passage but the slow one: the singer took a twenty-minute vilambit development she had planned to cut, because, she told them, the cycle felt safe to stand on.

“Rohan does the rarest thing an accompanist can do — he makes the stage feel safe at slow tempo. My vilambit had somewhere to stand.”
— Vidushi Shalini Bhide, khayal vocalist
Lecture-demonstration

“Counting Out Loud” — Kothrud Music Society, 2025 season

Asked by: the society's programme committee · Format: four 90-minute sessions · Audience: 60–90 listeners, most not musicians

The ask. A lecture-demonstration series for listeners who love the music and cannot say why a hall full of people gasps when sam lands. What I did. No lecture, at first. Session one is clapping: the whole hall learns teental's clap-and-wave with their own hands before I play a single bol, and by the interval they are arguing about where the khali falls. The later sessions open jhaptal and rupak the same way, then take the audience inside a recorded concert to hear what they now know. The outcome. The society renewed the series for a second season and doubled the school bookings — four schools now take the single-session version, whose entire fee structure is printed on the Console page.

“Our members have listened to this music for forty years. Rohan is the first person who put the cycle in their hands instead of describing it at them.”
— Prakash Limaye, programme secretary, Kothrud Music Society
Dance accompaniment

Kathak recital — with Rituja Patwardhan, February 2026

Asked by: the dancer, for her first full-length solo recital · Tala: teental throughout · Rehearsals: six, over two months

The ask. Hold the lehara — the repeating cycle — for a two-hour Kathak recital, steady enough for improvised footwork, alive enough that the audience never hears a loop. What I played. The theka as a fixed star with the light moving on it: small changes of weight and colour, never of tempo, and my eyes on her feet, not my hands, for the sawal-jawab passages where drum and ghungroo trade sentences. In rehearsal we built three hand signals for the moments a dancer cannot speak. The outcome. The recital ran its full length without a single broken cycle; the review in the Pune arts pages named the “conversation between feet and drum” — which is the only sentence a lehara player ever hopes to read.

“I improvise more bravely with Rohan behind me, because I know exactly where the floor is. He has never once moved it.”
— Rituja Patwardhan, Kathak dancer
Teaching outcome

A student's road to the stage — Advait, 2022–2026

Asked by: his parents, when he was twelve and “only interested in playing fast” · Path: the full ladder, Years 1–4 · Stage: youth music festival, March 2026

The ask. A twelve-year-old with quick hands and no patience, whose previous teacher had let him race. What we did. We started again from the clap — the hardest sell I make, to a boy who could already “play”. Four years on the ladder: keherwa rebuilt at 60 BPM, teental earned, then jhaptal, and in the fourth year his first baithak accompaniment, unrecorded and unrescued, exactly as the ladder promises. The outcome. At the March 2026 youth festival he accompanied a senior vocalist — not a student showcase, a working engagement — and held a vilambit teental that the vocalist afterwards described as “boringly reliable”, which in our trade is a medal.

“Rohan gave our son back his speed — but only after teaching him what it costs. The patience came first; the playing sounds like it.”
— Sneha Joshi, Advait's mother
