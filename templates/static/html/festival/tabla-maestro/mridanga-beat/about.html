The first teacher was not a famous one

My mother enrolled me at a neighbourhood class because I would not stop drumming on the dining table. The teacher was Mrs. Kulkarni, a school music teacher who taught bhajans and kept a single tabla in a cloth bag. For two years I played dha dhin na on a cycle of six and thought I was finished. She was strict about one thing only: the bayan had to be in tune with the dayan, or the whole thing sounded like a bucket.

That is still the first thing I check in a student's playing. Not speed, not repertoire. Whether the two drums agree.

Twelve years with one man

In 1998 a family friend took me to hear Ustad Rahimuddin Baksh play in Dadar. He was sixty-seven, had no recordings in circulation, and played a teentaal kaida for forty minutes without repeating a phrase. Afterwards I asked him to teach me. He said, “Come on Sunday and bring your drum, but do not expect to play it.”

For the first year I sat and listened, and copied bols into a notebook. He would speak a phrase once — dha tirakita dhin na — and I would have to write it, recite it, and only then touch the drum. He believed the hand is the last thing to learn, not the first. The notebook habit stayed; I still write a kaida by hand before I teach it.

“You are not playing fast. You are playing early and calling it fast.”

He said that to me in 2003, at a rehearsal in his flat, and I have repeated it to students every year since. Most speed problems are placement problems. The stroke lands before or after the matra, and the ear hears a mess it cannot name.

What the Ajrada house asks of you

Every gharana has a temperament. Ajrada is known for a clean, straight stroke and an unusual tolerance for space: phrases that stop early, leave a matra empty, and then land so hard on sam that the room flinches. It is not a showy style. It suits players who would rather be understood than admired.

My guru also insisted that a tabla player learn to accompany before learning to solo. For six years I played for singers and dancers on Thursday nights at a hall in Sadashiv Peth — light music, bhajans, the occasional Kathak class. It taught me to watch a shoulder for a cue, which no solo practice can teach.

Why I teach now

In 2007 I took four students because my guru was ill and had stopped teaching. He died in 2011. I have taught continuously since, in a room in Kothrud with a rug on the floor and a photograph of him on the wall. The room holds four students comfortably and six badly, so I stop at four.

I do not take students who want to learn a solo in a year. A kaida is a set of rules for improvising, and you cannot improvise inside a rule you do not yet obey automatically. Twelve years was not excessive. It was, if anything, quick.

02 Chronology

Benchmarks, not achievements

Dates are approximate where memory is. The order is not.

  1. 1992

    First bol at Mrs. Kulkarni's

    Age eight, a borrowed tabla, and a cycle of six that I played for two years without realising it was dadra.

  2. 1998

    Accepted by Ustad Rahimuddin Baksh

    Sunday mornings in Dadar, a notebook, and a year of not touching the drum. My first real lesson in patience was not musical.

  3. 2003

    First solo, Pune

    Teentaal, twenty-two minutes, at a small hall in Sadashiv Peth. My guru sat in the third row and said only that the tihai was early. He was right.

  4. 2007

    Starts teaching

    Four students, Tuesday and Thursday evenings, in the room that is still the studio. First batch included an engineer and a Kathak dancer.

  5. 2014

    First tour outside Maharashtra

    Jaipur, Delhi and Kolkata with a sitar player and a tabla duet in the second half. Learned that a room with a bad PA will expose every unsteady matra.

  6. 2019

    Records the Ajrada notebook

    Eleven kaidas and four relas, recorded in a single afternoon in a Prabhat Road studio with two microphones and no edits.

  7. 2026

    Still four students

    Two of them are preparing solos this year. One has been with me for nine years and still plays the first kaida every Tuesday, which is exactly right.
