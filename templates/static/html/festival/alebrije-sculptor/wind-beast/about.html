A craft that is younger than it looks.

Alebrijes are not an ancient Oaxacan tradition. They were invented in Mexico City in the 1930s, moved to the villages in the 1970s, and only became a village trade when a few families in San Martín Tilcajete found that copal carved beautifully. This workshop is one of those families.

The workshop · 05

Three generations, one yard

Nadia's grandfather Emilio carved wooden masks for the village fiesta and figures for the church crèche, the kind of work that paid in favours and maize. In the 1980s, when the first Oaxacan carvers began selling brightly painted animals to visitors, he was already old enough to be skeptical and curious in equal measure. He cut a jaguar from a piece of copal and gave it to Nadia's mother, Rosa, to paint.

Rosa still paints. Her patterns are the workshop's signature: a base colour laid flat, then rosettes or stripes, then a border of small dots along every edge, then the eyes. Nadia's brother César cuts and dries the trunks. Nadia carves, and draws the animals in the first place. The family has never hired outside painters, which is why the pieces look like they came from one hand even when five people worked on them.

What an alebrije is, and is not

The form began with Pedro Linares in Mexico City, who described a fever dream of a forest full of impossible creatures — a donkey with butterfly wings, a rooster with horns, a lion with a dog's face. He made them from papier-mâché, and the name he used for them, alebrije, was his own invention.

In Oaxaca the idea met wood. Carvers in San Martín Tilcajete and Arrazola adapted it to copal, a soft local wood that takes detail and holds paint. The animals are not nahuales, the spirit companions of older Mesoamerican belief, although the two are often confused in souvenir shops. An alebrije is a made thing: a drawing, a joke, a creature assembled from parts that do not belong together, and then painted as if they always had.

The wood

Copal blanco (Bursera species) grows in the dry hills around the village. The workshop uses wood from planted trees on family land and buys from two neighbours who plant for the same reason. Nobody cuts wild copal: the trees are too slow, and the village has learned what happens when a craft outruns its material.

Green copal is soft enough to dent with a thumbnail. It is split and dried for six to ten weeks, turning hard and pale. A trunk that dries too fast splits along the heart; one that dries too slowly grows a blue mould that has to be cut away. This is why the workshop yard is full of wood at every stage and why a finished piece takes six weeks at the shortest.

Learning

Nadia started carving at twelve, on offcuts, and was allowed to paint a whole animal at fifteen. She studied for two years at a design school in Oaxaca de Juárez before coming back to the yard, which she says taught her to draw better but not to carve better. The carving came from her grandfather's hands and from ruining a lot of wood.

She teaches on Saturday mornings in the workshop, four students at a time, mostly village teenagers and a few visitors who stay for a month. The first exercise is a fish: one curve, one tail, no legs. The second is a rabbit, which is harder than it looks because there is nowhere to hide a mistake.

Materials · 06

What is in the piece

Nothing exotic, nothing secret. A carving is wood, sealer, pigment, wax and a great deal of sanding.

6–12

Weeks per piece

Two to three of those are drying before the carving even starts. A large commission can take a season.

4

Grits of sandpaper

From 80 to 400. The surface has to be smooth enough that the brush never catches on a raised grain.

3

Coats before colour

Sealer, a second sealer cut with thinner, then the base colour. The pigment sits on top instead of sinking in.

40

Pieces a year

Small rabbits and armadillos go faster; a metre-tall jaguar with wings can occupy the bench for three months.

0

Trees cut wild

Copal comes from planted trees on family land or from two neighbours who plant for the workshop.

1

Signature detail

A row of dots along every edge, painted last, the same in every piece since Rosa started doing it in 1991.

The village

San Martín Tilcajete sits about thirty minutes south-east of Oaxaca de Juárez, on the road to Ocotlán. Several families carve; several more paint for the ones who do. The village square has a small market on Sundays and a church that is older than any of the animals sold around it.

The workshop is four blocks from the square. Ask for the house with the drying racks of pale wood in the yard.
