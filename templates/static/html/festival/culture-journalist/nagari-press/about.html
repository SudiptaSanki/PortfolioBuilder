The desk Since 2011

I write about the arts the way a court reporter writes about a trial: dates, names, and what was actually said.

Culture journalism in this city is mostly press releases with a byline. I try to do the other thing — sit in the rehearsal, count the audience, read the lease — and then write it at the length the subject deserves.

How I got here

I started at a Bengali daily in 2011, covering the film industry because nobody senior wanted the beat. It taught me two useful things: how a production actually works, and how little of it appears in the interviews.

In 2016 I moved to independent longform. The first piece was about a jatra troupe whose lead actress had left to work in a call centre. It ran to 4,000 words on a website that paid ₹6 a word and it is still the piece people bring up at parties.

What I cover

Theatre, craft, film, and the institutional life that keeps them possible — leases, grants, archives, unions. I am less interested in opening nights than in what happens on the Tuesday after.

I do not review. A critic serves the audience; a reporter serves the record. When I am asked to do both for the same publication, I decline the review.

How the work is checked

Every figure has a document behind it and every document has a holder. Where a claim rests on a single conversation, the copy says so — “unconfirmed”, “one source”, “the clerk would not put it in writing”. This is not hedging; it is the difference between reporting and rumour.

Corrections run at the foot of the edition with the date and the original wording. I have published four in nine years, two of them my own arithmetic.

Five rules of the desk

  1. 01

    Go twice

    The first visit is for the notebook. The second is for the piece, because by then people have stopped performing for you.

  2. 02

    Count the room yourself

    Attendance figures from organisers are aspirational. I count seats and heads, and I say which I counted.

  3. 03

    Quote the person, not the press note

    If a sentence exists in a press release, it is not a quote. It is a claim by an institution, and it is attributed that way.

  4. 04

    Name the money

    Grants, fees, rents and salaries are the story more often than the art is. Where a figure is confidential, the piece says who refused and why.

  5. 05

    Correct in public

    A correction at the foot of the page, dated, with the original wording intact. Quiet edits are a way of lying later.

Selected recognition
