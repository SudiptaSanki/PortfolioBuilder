The shopKachori Gali

A counter that opens at seven and has never once been early.

My grandfather opened in 1963 with two benches, a hand grinder and a clay pot he kept under the counter. The lane has changed around it: the sweet shops have become bigger, the signs have become brighter, and the ice has become reliable. The grinder is still here, and so is the rule that the paste is made the morning it is sold.

The pot under the counter

In the beginning the shop sold only what could be made in one morning: thandai, lassi, and a thin rice kheer in winter. The clay pot was the refrigerator. It was wrapped in a wet cloth, set in the coolest corner of the floor, and the cloth was changed every two hours by whoever was free.

The pot is still used for the first batch, even though there is a cold room now. It is not nostalgia. Clay pulls a little moisture out of the air and holds the temperature without a fan, and the first two hours of chilling are the ones that decide the texture. After that the batch goes into steel and the cold room takes over.

The other rule was about ice. Until 1978 the shop did not serve ice at all — the Ganga water was considered cold enough. My father added ice in the year the lane got its first electric freezer, and the regulars complained for a month before admitting it was better. Now the crushed ice goes in the glass first, always, because pouring thandai onto ice makes it cloudy and stirring it afterwards makes it thin.

Where it comes from

Four suppliers, all of them older than the shop. The list has not changed because the taste has not.

  • Almonds

    Mandsaur, Madhya Pradesh

    Bought in the shell, cracked in the shop. The California almonds are cheaper and larger; they also taste of nothing once ground.

  • Saffron

    Pampore, Kashmir

    One supplier since 1986. He sends 20 g at a time, in a tin, and the tin is kept closed with a clove against damp.

  • Fennel

    Jodhpur, Rajasthan

    Bold seed, ground the same morning. A week in the shop and the oil has gone; it is the first thing to taste stale in a bad thandai.

  • Milk

    Ramnagar dairy, 14 km

    Full-fat, twice a day, boiled in the shop. We have never used milk powder and the accounts would show it if we did.

The questions we get asked

Most of them are about the same three things: strength, sugar, and the version we do not make.

Is there bhang in it?

No. The shop has never served it and never will. During Holi, groups come in asking, and we say no and offer them a badam thandai with extra pistachio. That is the end of the conversation.

Can it be made without sugar?

Yes, and it is better than most people expect. The almonds and the fennel carry a sweetness of their own; the sugar is there to round the pepper. We make a small unsweetened batch every morning for two regulars.

Why does it separate after an hour?

Because there is no stabiliser in it. Ground nuts settle. Stir the pot once before pouring, not the glass after — stirring the glass breaks the ice and waters it down.

Do you deliver the clay pots?

Within Varanasi, yes, on a deposit of ₹500 for ten pots. They come back within three days or the deposit is kept, which has happened twice in eleven years.

Come before noon
