Attar · deg-bhapka · Kannauj, Uttar Pradesh

The smell of a season, kept in oil.

Rehana Bano distils attar in Kannauj, the north Indian town that has made perfume from flowers for four centuries. Rose in March, jasmine in June, and mitti — the smell of the first rain on baked earth — whenever the monsoon allows. No alcohol, no synthetic top notes, no shortcuts through the garden.

Build a distillation

Attar is flower vapour caught in oil. A charge of petals goes into the copper deg, steam passes through a bamboo pipe into the bhapka, and the sandalwood oil in the receiver holds what the steam carries. Set the charge and the notes; the bench works out the rest.

    LOT027THE STILL
    In the still room · 03

    Three things decide a bottle

    The flower, the fire and the months afterwards. Everything else is a variation on those three.

    01 / THE FLOWER

    Picked before the sun

    Rose is picked between four and seven in the morning, when the oil is still in the petal. A day's picking is distilled the same day; petals left overnight lose the top of the smell and give a flatter attar.

    Rose · March to April
    Jasmine · June to July
    Kewra · May
    02 / THE FIRE

    Wood, not gas

    The deg sits in a brick furnace over a wood fire. The fire is kept low and steady; a hot fire pushes the steam too fast and carries the green, bitter part of the flower with it. A charge runs six to eight hours.

    Copper deg · 60 kg a charge
    Bamboo pipe · bhapka receiver
    Sandalwood oil base
    03 / THE MONTHS

    Left to settle

    The attar rests in leather or glass for weeks before it is sold. The sharp edges soften, the base oil takes the flower properly, and the smell becomes one thing instead of two things next to each other.

    Rest · 3 to 8 weeks
    Glass or leather
    No alcohol, ever

    LOT028METHOD
    The method · 05

    Deg and bhapka, in order

    The same six steps every day of the season. The only thing that changes is what goes into the deg.

    01

    Pick and weigh

    Flowers are picked before sunrise, weighed at the still room door and spread on a cloth to shed the field heat.

    02

    Load the deg

    A charge of about 60 kg goes into the copper deg with water. The deg is sealed with a clay and cotton band, and the bamboo pipe is fitted.

    03

    Set the bhapka

    The receiver sits in a cooling tub of water with the base oil inside. The pipe ends just above the oil, so the vapour passes through it rather than bubbling.

    04

    Fire and watch

    Wood fire under the deg, kept low. The stillman watches the pipe and the water in the cooling tub, and tops up the fire rather than stoking it.

    05

    Change the charge

    After six to eight hours the charge is spent. The deg is emptied, refilled and set again; one lot may run through seven or eight charges.

    06

    Rest and decant

    The oil is decanted into glass or leather and left to settle for weeks. What was flower and oil becomes one smell, and then it is bottled.

    "A rose does not smell like attar. Attar smells like the rose remembered in a warm room, months later."

    Rehana Bano

    The season decides the shelf

    Rose attar is made in March and gone by autumn. If you want a particular lot, write before the harvest rather than after it.
