Home/The still room

Fire, water, flowers and patience.

Kannauj has been distilling flowers for four centuries, and the method has changed less than the town around it. This is one family's still room: two copper degs, a brick furnace, and a shelf of glass bottles that are the whole archive.

The still room · 06

The family

Rehana's great-grandfather opened the still room in 1936, in a lane off the Kannauj perfume market. He had learned the work as a boy in another man's yard, and he kept a ledger of every lot: the flower, the weight, the weather, the hours on the fire, and a line about how it smelled. Four of those ledgers survive. The oldest is from 1938 and records a rose lot that the family still argues about.

Rehana took over in 2009, after eleven years of standing beside her father at the fire. She is the third generation and the first woman in the family to run the still room, a fact she mentions only when someone asks whether she is sure about the temperature.

Why oil, not alcohol

Attar is not perfume. Western perfume is mostly alcohol with a small amount of aromatic oil; attar is the oil itself, usually sandalwood, that has been made to hold a flower. There is no alcohol in the bottle, which is why a drop on the wrist behaves differently: it warms slowly, stays close, and does not throw a cloud across a room.

The base oil matters. Sandalwood is traditional and increasingly expensive; vetiver gives a drier, earthier finish; mitti is not a base at all in the strict sense but a distillation of baked earth into sandalwood, and it is the house's most requested lot.

The season

Rose comes in March and April, picked before sunrise from fields around the town. Jasmine follows in June, kewra in May, and mitti whenever the first proper rain falls on ground that has been baked all summer. The still room runs from dawn to dusk through the season and then goes quiet, which is why the bottles on the shelf are dated by year and month.

Smelling, not reading

There are no gas chromatographs in the still room. Rehana tests a lot by putting a drop on the back of her hand, waiting, and smelling it three times over an hour. The first smell is the top, the second is the heart, the third is what will still be there tomorrow. A lot that fails the third test is blended or sold as a lesser grade, never labelled as the flower it was supposed to be.

The flowers · 07

What each season gives

The same still, the same fire, a different flower. Each one asks for a slightly different hand.

03

Rose

March to April. Picked from four to seven in the morning. The most generous flower and the most easily spoiled by a hot fire.

06

Jasmine

June to July. Picked at night, when the flower opens. The oil is heavier and the distillation slower.

05

Kewra

May. Screwpine, a green and slightly sweet flower that gives a cool edge to the winter blends.

07

Mitti

First rain, usually July. Baked earth discs are distilled into sandalwood oil; the smell is petrichor, caught.

11

Shamama

A winter blend of around twenty herbs, flowers and spices, rested for a year before it is sold.

60

Kilos a charge

The size of one deg charge. A rose lot may run through seven or eight charges before it is finished.

The process · 08

One lot, from field to bottle

01

Field

Flowers are picked by hand before sunrise, in baskets that are weighed at the still room door.

02

Deg

The copper pot is loaded with flowers and water, sealed with clay and cotton, and set over the fire.

03

Bhapka

The receiver, sitting in cooling water, holds the base oil. The bamboo pipe carries the vapour into the oil.

04

Fire

Six to eight hours of low, steady wood heat. The stillman's hand is on the fire more than on the pipe.

05

Rest

The oil is decanted and left for weeks, until the flower and the base have become a single smell.

06

Bottle

Glass or leather, labelled by lot, year and flower. A drop on the wrist is the only test that matters.

"You cannot hurry a flower into oil. You can only keep the fire low and be there when it is ready."

Rehana Bano

Visit during the season

The still room is open to visitors in March and April, in the morning, when the rose is coming in. Come before eight; after that, nobody has time to talk.
