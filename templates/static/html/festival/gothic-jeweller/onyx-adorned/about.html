The jeweller

Mira Vance makes jewellery for the unsentimental kind of grief.

Trained at the Birmingham School of Jewellery. Nine years at a bench in the Midlands, three in Edinburgh. She works alone, answers her own letters, and will tell you when a stone is wrong for the piece.

I make jewellery for the worst week of someone's life, and for the people who simply prefer a darker sort of ornament. Those are not as different as they sound.

I came to this through a box of my grandmother's things: three mourning brooches, a jet cross, and a ring with a lock of hair under glass. She had never worn any of it. It had been her mother's, and her mother's before that, and nobody in the family knew whose hair was in the ring. I was nineteen and I found that the most interesting object I had ever held.

At Birmingham I learned the ordinary things — sawing, filing, soldering, setting — and then spent a dissertation on Victorian mourning jewellery, which is a subject that makes people at parties move away slowly. Jet from Whitby, black enamel, hairwork, the language of forget-me-nots and wheat and broken columns. A whole grammar for a feeling that most people now express with a card.

What I took from it was not nostalgia. It was the idea that an object can carry a feeling so that the person does not have to carry it every hour. A ring is a small, heavy, private thing. It does that better than a photograph on a phone.

“I am not trying to make death pretty. I am trying to make it portable, so a person can put it down for an hour and then pick it up again.”

The materials

Blackened silver is my first metal. I oxidise it with liver of sulphur, then cut it back on the high points so the piece has a patina rather than a uniform black. It will wear further with the hand and become more silver over years; I consider that part of the commission.

The stones are cabochons, not faceted. Onyx from India, obsidian from Mexico, garnet from Rajasthan, moonstone from Sri Lanka. Jet is the exception: Whitby jet is a fossil, not a mineral, and it is getting harder to buy in pieces large enough for a panel. When I cannot get it, I say so and offer onyx instead, which is not the same and I will not pretend it is.

I do not use ivory, coral, or any shell taken live. I do not set human hair any more. I did, early on, and then a client asked me to take her mother's hair out of a brooch and give it back to her in a bag, and I understood that the hair was not mine to handle. I will make a compartment for hair that the client places herself.

How the bench works

A commission begins with an hour in the studio, or a letter if you are not in Edinburgh. I want to know what the piece is for, and I want to see the stone if you have one. Then I draw. The drawing usually takes longer than the making, and you will see it before any metal is cut.

Deposit is forty per cent. The balance is due when the piece is finished and before it ships. I do not take more than six commissions at a time, and I will tell you honestly if the wait is three months rather than three weeks.

Why Edinburgh

The studio is a single room up a close off the Royal Mile, with one window that looks at a wall and a good north light. I came for the stones in the National Museum — the Scottish regalia, the Iron Age torcs, the Victorian mourning collection — and stayed because the city is built out of dark stone and does not apologise for it.

There is no shop. You come by appointment, climb the stairs, and sit at the bench while I open the stone drawer. Tea is assumed. If you bring a child, there is a box of offcuts for them to rummage in.

Four steps, and one of them is waiting

  1. i

    The conversation

    An hour, or a letter. What the piece is for, what it should feel like on the hand, what it must not look like.

  2. ii

    The drawing

    Graphite on tracing paper, scanned and sent. Two revisions are included; the third is charged at £60.

  3. iii

    The bench

    Cutting, filing, setting, engraving. Four to nine weeks depending on the setting and the engraving.

  4. iv

    The hallmark

    Assayed in Edinburgh, struck with my maker's mark, and sent in a lined box with a care note in my own handwriting.
