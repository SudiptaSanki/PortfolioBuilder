Almanac · season 48

Forty-eight Decembers, nine years behind each one

My father planted the first rows in 1978 on land that had been a dairy. The cows are gone; the stone wall, the sugar maples and the cold pocket at the bottom of Plot 3 are not.

Why the trees are here

The farm is 62 acres, of which eleven are in fir. The rest is hay, sugar bush, and a wet corner nobody has ever found a use for. The firs are on the slopes because fir will not tolerate wet feet: the roots rot in a season, and you find out two years later when the tree leans.

Cabot sits in a cold pocket of the Winooski valley. Our last frost is late and our first is early, which is bad for corn and good for fir. The trees put on most of their growth in six weeks of June and July and spend the rest of the year hardening off. A tree we plant in May will be ready to cut in seven years, or nine if the deer find it.

A Christmas tree is a crop with a nine-year lead time. You are always farming for somebody's December that has not happened yet.

We do not buy trees in. What is on the field is what we grew, which is why the tall ones are scarce and why we will sometimes say no to a request for nine feet in a narrow room.

The work, season by season

Shearing is the part people do not see. Without it a fir is a rangy thing with gaps; with it, the tree is dense because every cut makes two new buds.

  1. March

    Correct the leaders

    Walk every row with a knife and a roll of flagging. Take out double tops and the odd branch that will make a hole in the side of the tree. Cold hands, short days.

  2. April

    Mend the fences

    Eight-foot deer fence around the young fields. Deer pressure is the difference between a crop and a buffet. We also trap for porcupine, which girdles a tree in one night.

  3. May

    Plant

    Nine hundred bare-root seedlings from a nursery in Michigan, in the ground within four days of arrival. Planted by hand with a spade, at the same depth they grew.

  4. June

    Shear

    Six weeks with a long shearing knife. Each tree cut by eye to a cone, the top handled last. This is the only skilled work on the farm and the only part I still do all of myself.

  5. July

    Mow and scout

    Mow between rows, watch for spider mite in the dry years, and count what the frost took in the low corner. There is always a low corner.

  6. November

    Open the gate

    Shake, bale, tie to the car, sell wreaths. The busiest five weeks of the year, and the only ones with customers in them.

  7. January

    Sharpen

    Every blade on the bench, the baler stripped and greased, the seedling order written for next spring. Then a month of nothing, which we have earned.

The family, briefly

  • 1978Ray and Margaret Whitcomb plant the first 400 firs on the old dairy pasture. Sold 61 trees that December, at $9 each.
  • 1989The barn goes up, rebuilt from a neighbour's dismantled cow barn. Still unheated, still standing.
  • 1996Wreaths begin, mostly to use the trimmings. Margaret's method, wired on a bench by the stove.
  • 2004Ray dies in February. The family keeps the gate open that December, and every one since.
  • 2011Ellis takes over the shearing. Ellen runs the barn, the wreaths and the books, which is the harder job.
  • 2017Plant 900 a year instead of 600, to keep a crop coming as the older fields are retired.
  • 2022Last year of the old baler. The new one is quieter and we still miss the noise.
  • 2026Season 48. About 560 trees to cut, 240 wreaths, and one dog who has stopped chasing the tractor.

An honest note on spray

We spray twice in a normal year, in May and early June, for adelgid and tip moth, and we do not spray anything after the middle of June. It is a small operation and we are not certified organic; I would rather tell you that than print a word on a sign that is not true.

What we do avoid: colourants, flocking, and trees shipped in from Canada or North Carolina. The needles you smell at our barn were on the tree the day before.

Questions about any of this: ask Ellen at the barn, or write to the address on the visit page.

Come see the rows
