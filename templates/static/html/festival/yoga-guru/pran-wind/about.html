The teacher

I came to yoga to fix my back. I stayed for the silence.

Leela Raghavan. Born in Mysore, taught by a father who recited Sanskrit in the morning and a teacher who barely spoke at all. Twenty-two years of practice, twenty of teaching, and the same six mats.

My father was a Sanskrit teacher at a school in Agrahara. He did not practise yoga; he practised grammar. But he recited in the morning, on the verandah, and the sound of that — long, even, unhurried — is the first thing I remember about breathing.

I was twenty and working in a bank when my back began to hurt in a way that frightened me. A colleague sent me to a class in Lakshmipuram run by a man in his seventies who had studied in the Krishnamacharya line and taught in a room with one bulb. He rarely demonstrated. He would sit on a stool, watch, and say one word every ten minutes. Longer. Or again. I stayed for eleven years.

What he taught me was not a sequence. It was that the postures are a way of asking the body questions, and the answer arrives in the breath. If the breath is ragged, the answer is no. That is the whole of my method, and it took a decade to learn.

In 2004 I began teaching, in the same room, on the same red floor. In 2011 I moved to the third cross in Gokulam, up one flight, with three windows that let in the morning at an angle. The lease is in my name and the fan is older than my students.

I do not teach teacher trainings to make teachers. I teach one a year because people ask, and because it forces me to say out loud what I know, which is a useful discipline. If you want a certificate to hang on a wall, there are faster places.

Chapter 02

Years, in order

1976 – now

  • 1976

    Born in Mysore

    Agrahara, third house from the temple. My father taught Sanskrit; my mother taught me to keep quiet while he did.

  • 1996

    The room with one bulb

    A bank job, a bad back, and a colleague who sent me to a seventy-year-old teacher in Lakshmipuram. Eleven years, three classes a week.

  • 2001

    The back stopped hurting

    It took five years, and it was not the postures that did it. It was learning to breathe out for longer than I breathed in.

  • 2004

    First class of my own

    Four students on the same red floor, borrowed mats, ₹200 each a month. Two of them still come.

  • 2011

    The shala on the third cross

    One flight up, three windows, a fan older than my students. The lease is in my name and the rent is the reason I teach six days.

  • 2017

    Pranayama on Fridays

    Eight people, seated, no asana at all. The class I most look forward to and the one that fills last.

  • 2023

    Fifty thousand breaths

    A year-long count I kept in a notebook, one mark per round. I do not recommend it; I did learn what a year of practice actually looks like.

  • Now

    Six mats, two rooms

    Morning self-practice, evening taught classes, one training a year, and Sundays with the mats hanging over the balcony rail.

Chapter 03

What I was handed

Not a style · a method

  • MethodBreath first

    Every instruction is tested against the breath. If it shortens, the instruction is wrong for that body today.

  • LineageKrishnamacharya line

    Through my teacher in Lakshmipuram, who studied in Mysore in the 1950s and never once called it a brand.

  • RoomSix mats

    Small enough that I can see your jaw. Big enough that you are not performing for the person beside you.

  • RuleAsk before touching

    No adjustment without consent, no correction that requires a surprise. You may always say no.

“Most people do not need a harder practice. They need a quieter one, done more often.”
— Leela Raghavan
