The reader · Fort Kochi

Vasudha NairI was taught to read hands by a woman who read them in the afternoon, after the fish was sold, and who never once told anybody their fortune.

Born 1968 in Fort Kochi. Twenty-two years of sittings, three thousand and more. Malayalam and English. A notebook, a cloth, and a north window.

At a glance

Taught by Mariamma, her mother's aunt, in a house on Rose Street. Before that, twenty years of teaching Malayalam literature at a college in Ernakulam, which is where the reading actually comes from.

No photographs on this site. The hand drawn here is a diagram, not a client's.

The afternoon sittings

Mariamma read palms in the afternoon, on the veranda, after the fish was sold and the house was quiet. She had a cloth she spread on the table, a bottle of coconut oil she never used for reading, and a way of holding a hand as if it were a letter she had been waiting for. She charged what people could pay, which was often nothing, and she refused to read anyone who came to be frightened.

I was eleven when she began teaching me. Not the lines first — the mounts, the shape of the hand, the way a person gives it to you. A hand that comes palm-up and open is a different conversation from a hand held out at arm's length, and she said the reading begins before the first line is looked at.

“The hand is not a fortune. It is a map of where the person has already been, drawn by the person themselves, in a language they cannot read.”

— Mariamma, on the veranda, 1984

What twenty years of teaching literature did

I taught Malayalam poetry and fiction at St. Teresa's for two decades, and it made me a better reader of hands than any book on palmistry. A poem does not tell you what will happen; it tells you how someone is living. The lines are the same kind of evidence — a shape that means something in a tradition, held up against a person who is sitting in front of you and cannot be reduced to it.

It also made me careful with other people's stories. A reading is a conversation with a text that the client wrote without knowing the alphabet. If I take that seriously, I cannot also pretend to know their future.

What I keep and what I do not

I keep a notebook with the date, an initial, and four or five lines. No photographs, no tracings, no copies of anything. If a client comes back years later I can find the page, which people seem to like; if they ask me to destroy it, I do, in front of them.

The note is the only thing that leaves the room. I write it in the last five minutes, in front of the person, and I read it aloud before they take it. If a sentence is not something I would say to their face, it does not go in the note.

What I will not do

  • No health, pregnancy or death readings, ever. A line cannot know, and fear is not a service.
  • No dates for marriage, money or work. A date is a guess dressed as knowledge.
  • No reading a hand that is not in the room. Bring the person or bring nothing.
  • No predictions that require the client to spend money — no gemstones, no rituals, no "remedies" sold at the door.

A short history

  1. 2004

    The room above the spice shopTaken over from a tailor who retired. One north window, two chairs, a table with a cloth. It has not changed since, except the fan.

  2. 2011

    Readings in EnglishFort Kochi filled with visitors and the sittings doubled. I learned to say the same thing in two languages without making either sound like a translation.

  3. 2016

    The notebook starts againBurned the old one, on purpose, after a client asked what would happen to it when I died. Four hundred pages, one match, in the courtyard. The new notebook has the same rule: initials only.

  4. 2021

    Two years of no sittingsThe room was shut. I read nothing and missed it less than I expected, which told me something about how much of the work is the room.

  5. 2025

    Three thousand one hundred and forty sittingsI count them because the notebook counts them. Six a week, Tuesday to Saturday, and no more.

Sittings

Bring the question you actually have, not the one that sounds reasonable. The reading is only as useful as the thing you are willing to say out loud.
