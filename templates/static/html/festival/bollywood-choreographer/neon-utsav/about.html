Method

The count is scaffolding. The wall is the dance.

I trained in kathak from the age of six, then spent four years in the chorus of a film unit learning what a camera does to a body that is not facing it. Both trainings are in every number I make.

What I actually do

Choreography for film is a negotiation with a lens. The step that looks magnificent in the rehearsal mirror can die in a 35 mm frame because the camera cannot see the feet. So I choreograph twice: once for the room, once for the frame — and I keep the two versions close enough that the dancer does not have to relearn their body.

For the stage, the problem inverts. Everyone sees everything, all the time, including the back row and the wings. A formation has to be legible from the cheapest seat, which usually means fewer steps and clearer lines than the room version.

What I refuse

I do not take numbers that ask dancers to be furniture. I do not work with music that has been cleared without the composer being paid. And I do not put a step in a sangeet that a grandmother cannot reach by count three — that rule has cost me two bookings and kept every one of the others.

The rehearsal room is in Andheri West, on the second floor above a printing press. It is hot, the mirrors are old, and the floor is the best sprung floor I could afford. Dancers get water, a break every forty minutes, and the count called out loud.

How a number gets built

  1. Listen once, with no pen

    The first pass is only for the shape of the song: where it breathes, where the percussion takes over, which line the audience will remember. I hum the count out loud before I write anything.

  2. Chart the eight-counts

    Every phrase gets a number and a formation. If the chart needs more than eight eights, the number is probably two numbers, and I say so early.

  3. Choreograph the back row first

    The dancers furthest from the camera or the stage front get the simplest, strongest version. The front row then decorates it. This is the opposite of how most rooms work and it is why the wide shots hold.

  4. Rehearse to the count, then to the breath

    Three days on counts, then the counts come out. By the shoot or the show, the dancers should be able to hold the phrase while talking — that is how I know it has left the page.

  5. Fix it in the room, not the edit

    If a step only works after cutting, it does not work. I would rather shoot the whole phrase twice than save it in post.
