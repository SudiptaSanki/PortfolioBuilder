Case files

The work, counted.

Three productions told the way they happened: a film song led end to end, a sangeet taught to fourteen people who had never performed, and a number that never opened. Roles are stated on every file — lead means the steps were Sana's; nothing here borrows anyone else's credit.

“Platform No. 9” — a hook the crane rebuilt

Production
Station Road Romeo, independent feature · 2025
Role
Lead choreographer
The floor
28 dancers · 11 sessions · 4 weeks
Outcome
Shot in 2 days · hook at 16 counts

The ask

A railway-platform song for a first-time director: one hero, twenty-eight dancers, a platform set that allowed exactly one long crane move. The song is the production's and its composer's; the brief to Sana was the steps — an eight-count hook the hero could carry through the whole number.

What was set

The song was mapped into phrases before anyone danced: 11 phrases of 8 counts, with the hook written as two phrases that lock into one sixteen-count figure. Formations were drawn as floor diagrams (the hook's diamond is on the home page) and taught mirror-first.

What the camera changed

On the first camera rehearsal the crane's pull-back needed four counts of travel the eight-count hook didn't have — the frame emptied before the step landed. The hook was rebuilt that afternoon to sixteen counts: the figure travels as it turns, so the crane and the dancers arrive together. Nobody re-shot the rehearsal footage to hide the first version; the change is the case.

Phrase map, as taught

  • Intro — 2 phrases. The platform walk; the hero finds the beat before the beat finds him.
  • Verse — 4 phrases. Lines and counter-lines; the crowd parts on count 1, every time.
  • Hook — 2 phrases = 16 counts. Danced three times in the song; the diamond forms on the second.
  • Bridge — 3 phrases. The only stillness in the song: one breath, then the last hook.

“She rebuilt the hook in an afternoon because the crane needed four more counts — and the sixteen-count version is the one the audience hums on the way out.”

— Rohan Vardaan, producer, Station Road Romeo (fictional production), 2025

The Bhatia–Fernandes sangeet — fourteen non-dancers, four numbers

Client
Private family · Pune · Nov 2025
Role
Lead choreographer & teacher
The floor
14 non-dancers, ages 9 to 72 · 11 sessions
Outcome
All 14 performed · finished inside the plan

The ask

A two-family sangeet: four numbers — the couple, the bride's side, the groom's side, and everybody — taught to people whose combined stage experience was one school play in 1998. The date was fixed; the hall was booked; the calendar was the whole problem.

The plan, from the printed tables

Non-dancer family rates, four numbers at eight phrases each: phrase teaching 4 × 8 × 35 = 1,120 minutes; formations 4 × 3 × 15 = 180; total 1,300 minutes = 11 sessions of 120 minutes; at three sessions a week, four teaching weeks plus the polish week — five calendar weeks. The family booked six weeks out. That one spare week is why this case is uneventful in the best way.

What actually happened

The venue changed halls in week two and one session moved — absorbed by the spare week, exactly as the planner's rules intend. The arc formation (diagram, right) put the grandparents at the open ends of the arc: last to enter, first to be seen, and the loudest cheer of the night. All fourteen performed all four numbers; nobody was hidden at the back, because the formations were drawn so nobody had to be.

“My father is seventy-two and had never danced in public. Sana put him where the whole hall could see him, gave him four counts he could own, and he has dined out on it since.”

— Sunita Bhatia, mother of the bride, on the Bhatia–Fernandes sangeet, Pune, Nov 2025

The dropped number — “Retro Rani”, cut two days before opening

Production
Silver Screen Nights, touring stage show · 2024
Role
Lead choreographer
The number
9 dancers · 3 weeks of rehearsal
Status
Dropped · replaced by a re-blocked duet

What happened

The medley was built around the show's lead vocalist singing live — the steps answered her phrasing, not a track. Two days before opening she was hospitalised with a throat infection and lost her voice. There was no understudy for a number that existed only in her throat.

The decision

The number was dropped, not re-voiced. Lip-syncing another singer's recording would have broken the show's one rule — everything live — and a replacement vocalist could not have learned the phrasing in two days. In its place, the Act 1 duet was re-blocked into a nine-dancer finale using formations the cast had already been taught: two extra sessions, no new steps invented under panic.

What it cost, in print

  • Three weeks of rehearsal on the dropped number — written off, and counted in the record on the home page.
  • Sana's fee for that number was halved by agreement with the producer: the work was done, the number never opened, and both halves of that sentence are true.
  • The vocalist recovered and rejoined the tour in its third week; the medley was never reinstated — the re-blocked finale had become the better ending.

This file stays in the work because a portfolio that only shows what opened is a poster, not a record. The dropped number is also why every stage contract Sana signs now carries the replacement rule printed on the about page.
