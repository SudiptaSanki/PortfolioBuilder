The practice · since 2012

Sadashiv Peth, Pune. A steel trunk of powders, a long tape, a chalk line, a broom, and a knee pad that my mother made from an old cotton quilt.

Anjali Kulkarni

Born 1984 in Pune. Learned rangoli from her mother, Sunita, on the threshold of a wada in Sadashiv Peth, and from her grandmother, who drew kolam in the rain because "the floor forgets everything by evening, and that is the point".

Marathi, Hindi, English. Right-handed; draws anticlockwise from the centre.

The first floor

My mother drew a rangoli every morning before she made tea. Not a large one — a foot and a half across, at the threshold, in rice flour and a little red. It was swept away by ten o'clock when the house was cleaned. I used to think this was a waste of a morning. She said the drawing was not for the day, it was for the hour between five and six when nobody was awake and the street was still dark.

I started drawing my own at fourteen, badly, in the corner where she would not see it. At eighteen I was drawing for neighbours who wanted a piece for a wedding and did not want to pay a "artist from the studio". At twenty-eight I stopped taking any other work.

A rangoli is not a picture that survives. It is a drawing made for the hour it is seen in, and then swept up. Knowing that changes how you make it: no line is precious, and no line should take longer than it needs to.

The year of mornings

In 2016 I drew a floor every morning for 365 days, whatever the weather, whatever the day held. It was not a project and there was no audience; I wanted to know what happened to a grammar when it is used every day instead of once a season.

What happened was that the symmetry stopped being a set of rules and became a way of measuring. I could walk into a room, look at the floor, and know that an eight-fold piece would fit the doorway and a twelve-fold would not. I stopped counting dots and started counting the space between them. That year is the reason I can quote a size and a pigment quantity over the phone and be within a hundred grams.

What I do not do

  • No stencils, projectors, printed outlines or rangoli sticker sheets. If it needs a stencil, it is not my work.
  • No glitter, no acrylic paint, no thermocol. It does not sweep up and it does not belong on a floor.
  • No drawing over a drain, a wet patch or a place where a door will swing through it. I will move the drawing instead.
  • No photographs of a piece before the person who commissioned it has seen it. They see it first, on the floor, with their own eyes.

Teaching

I teach two workshops a year, in June and December, at a school hall in Parvati. Twelve people, four hours, one ring each, no patterns to copy. Half of every workshop is spent on the floor with a broom, learning why a line holds or does not.

The rule is the same for everyone: draw your first ring in rice flour and do not touch the powder tins until the ring is true. People hate this. It is the only way I know to teach the difference between a drawing that is roughly circular and one that is actually circular.

Floors, in order

    Ashta-dala lotus, KothrudSix feet, wedding threshold, four hours forty. The family kept it under a sheet until the muhurta, then walked around it for two days.

  2. 2024

    Bund Garden atrium, 14 ftThree assistants, a string grid and nine hours overnight. The largest piece I have drawn, and the first one I quoted from a drawing rather than a feeling.

  3. 2022

    Ten thresholds, one laneDiwali morning, 5 am to 9 am, ten doorways in Sadashiv Peth, each drawn to the width of its own threshold.

  4. 2019

    School courtyard, 800 childrenEach child given one square foot and one colour. The result was not a rangoli and it was the best thing on the floor that year.

  5. 2016

    365 morningsOne floor a day for a year. The year the grammar became a measurement.
