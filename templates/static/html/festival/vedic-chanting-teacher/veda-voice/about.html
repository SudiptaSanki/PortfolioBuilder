The teaching

The line is older than the book, and it lives in a voice

I was taught by a man who was taught by his father, who was taught in a house that had taught it for six generations. The accents were not explained to me. They were said, and I said them back, for four years.

How I came to teach it

My teacher, Sri N. Ramanatha Sastrigal, lived three streets from this house. I was nine and I could not sit still. He gave me the base note and told me to hum it until he came back, which was forty minutes. He came back and gave me the same instruction for a month.

By the time I was sixteen I had the Gāyatrī, the first three anuvākas of the Rudram, and a voice that could hold a line without a tanpura. I taught my first student at nineteen — a neighbour's son who could not find a teacher who would take a boy who worked in the evenings. She taught at night. He learned at night. Both of them still chant.

I have taught for thirty-one years, in this room and in temple halls, and the method has not changed. Ear first, book later, and never the other way round. A student who learns the accents from a printed page learns a diagram of them, not the sound.

How a lesson goes

  • The base note. Before any word, we find the note your voice sits on without effort. If you cannot hum it for a minute without wavering, the lesson is the note, not the hymn.
  • The three svaras, on the vyāhṛtis. Raised, low, falling. This takes three or four weeks for most people, and longer for anyone who has sung film music, because the habit of sliding has to be unlearned.
  • The first line, by ear. No book in front of you for the first three months. I say a syllable; you say it back. If you need to look at a page, you are reading, not chanting.
  • Pada and krama. Once the line is in the ear, we take it apart and put it back together. Krama is where a student discovers which syllables they were guessing.
  • The hymn, and its grammar of breath. Where to breathe, where not to, and how to carry a long compound without running out of air at the wrong place. This is the part that takes a year.
  • Teaching somebody else. The tradition's own test. When my teacher judged I was ready, he sent me to a boy who could not find a teacher. That was the examination.

What I ask of a student

  • The same hour every week. A voice trained irregularly does not settle. If you cannot keep an hour, wait until you can.
  • An empty stomach, in the morning. Not for purity — the recitation needs the breath to move freely, and it does not when you have eaten.
  • No recording without asking. I am not protecting a secret. I am protecting a student from learning a recording of themselves instead of a teacher.
  • No book for the first three months. If you read, the eye leads and the ear follows, and the accent is lost.
  • You may stop. Plenty of students stop at krama and chant at the temple for thirty years. That is a complete practice, not a failure.

Who can learn this

Some houses will tell you that a woman cannot teach the Veda, and that a student must be born into a particular family. I have heard it for thirty-one years. My students include temple priests, a doctor, two schoolteachers, a bus conductor and my own granddaughter, and I have never found that the tradition suffered for it.

What I do ask is that you come to learn it as it is, not as a wellness practice or a language course. The accent is not decoration. If you change it, you are chanting something else.

“I had been chanting for eleven years from a book and I was wrong in the third syllable of the first line. She heard it in ten seconds. That is what thirty-one years sounds like.”

R. Kaushik, student since 2021, now chants at the Varadaraja temple
