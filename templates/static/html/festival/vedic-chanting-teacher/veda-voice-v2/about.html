परिचय · About the teacher

वसुधा कुलकर्णी

Vasudha Kulkarni, 52. Learned in Satara, teaching in Pune since 2007.

My grandfather was a schoolteacher in Satara who chanted before he did anything else in the morning — before tea, before the newspaper, before conversation. I learned sitting on the floor beside his chair, repeating lines I did not understand, in a voice that had to match his exactly. I was nine. I did not know I was being taught a method. I thought that was simply what mornings were.

I have taught in Pune for nineteen years now — in a small room in Kothrud with a harmonium, a blackboard, and a kettle — and the method has not changed, because it was never mine to change. What follows is the whole of it, written down the way I would write it in a student's notebook.

१

पद्धत · The method, in four entries

क

By ear before by eye

A chant is sound that happens to have been written down, not writing that happens to be read aloud. So every text in my classroom is heard first — my voice, your repetition — and the printed page arrives only after the line of the chant is already in your ear. Students find this slow for exactly one month. Then they find it is the only reason anything stays memorised.

ख

Metre is the skeleton

Every verse stands on a counted frame, and we count it before we chant it. The gāyatrī metre: three feet of eight syllables, twenty-four in all. The anuṣṭubh, the workhorse of the Sahasranāmam: four feet of eight, thirty-two. The triṣṭubh: four feet of eleven, forty-four. When a student loses the line mid-verse, it is almost always the count that went first — so the count is what we repair.

ग

Breath, counted not guessed

Chanting is breath with somewhere to go. We mark the breath joints of a long text before we chant it whole — an anuvāka of the Rudram has its natural places to breathe, worn smooth by centuries of voices, and fighting them is how throats tire and svaras sag. A verse per breath at practice speed; the long lines are built up joint by joint.

घ

Speed is the last thing, if it comes at all

I will say it plainly, because students arrive wanting it first: a Rudram chanted at a run is a race, not a recitation. Speed in this tradition is a by-product of years, never a target for a term. My students chant slowly enough that a listener can hear every svara land. If that bores a prospective student, I am not their teacher, and the contact page is a good place to find that out cheaply — before fees, not after.

२

What I will not promise

I teach chanting as a discipline and a tradition: pronunciation, metre, memory, and the company of a text older than any of us. I make no promises about health, luck, prosperity or merit, and I do not sell blessings by the course. If a student finds peace in the practice — many do — that is theirs, and it was never mine to invoice. What I can promise is narrower and checkable: you will chant the text you enrolled for, correctly, from memory, at the pace the syllabus page prints. That promise is on the first page, with its arithmetic showing.

Classes run in Kothrud, Pune, and by video call. Batches hold at most eight students; one-to-one teaching is in person or by call. The full syllabus, the course planner and the printed fees are on the syllabus page; recent teaching is written up in the teaching record.
