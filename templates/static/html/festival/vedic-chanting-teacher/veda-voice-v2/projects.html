अध्यापन वृत्तान्त · The teaching record

शिक्षणाची नोंद

What was asked, what I did, what came of it — kept like a ledger, because that is what it is.

A chanting teacher's work is other people's voices. These are four engagements from the last two years, written the way I keep them in my notebook: the request as it arrived, the teaching as it happened, and the outcome as it honestly turned out — including the student who needed a second term.

१

The Saturday beginners' batch — Kothrud, September 2025 to October 2026

Asked. Eight adults, aged 29 to 64, every one of them reading the mantras from Roman-letter printouts, every one of them flattening the svaras without knowing it. They wanted to chant "properly" — their word — at home and at the mandal's festivals.

Did. The foundation month first, printouts put away. Then the Śrī Sūktam at the printed batch pace — 15 ṛks at 3 a week, five weeks — then the Puruṣa Sūktam, then a long revision term of singing the two together until the line of the chant sat in the body and not on the page.

Outcome. Seven of the eight chanted the Śrī Sūktam from memory at the mandal's Diwali morning, Tuesday 20 October 2026, in front of about forty neighbours. The eighth took a second term — voices settle at different speeds, and a ledger should say so — and chants it with the batch now.

"I arrived reading the Śrī Sūktam off a Roman printout and chanting it like a railway announcement. Vasudha tai took the printout away for a month. At Diwali I chanted it from memory, slowly, in front of forty people — and my mother said it sounded like her father's house."

— Meera Joshi, of this batch, on the Śrī Sūktam course
२

One voice, one deadline — the Sahasranāmam, Baner, 2026

Asked. A retired bank officer, 61, rang me with a date circled: his father's first death-anniversary ceremony, fourteen weeks away. He had heard the Viṣṇu Sahasranāmam all his life and never chanted a line of it. He wanted to chant the whole text himself at the ceremony — not play a recording of it. His words: "It should be my voice."

Did. One-to-one, two sessions a week. The foundation month in full — his ear was good, but the month is the month, and it is where we found the two habits the rest of the course had to fix. Then the 108 ślokas at the one-to-one pace — thirteen to fourteen a week — eight weeks on the text, and a final fortnight of full run-throughs at ceremony speed, which in my classroom means slower than practice speed, never faster. Fourteen weeks, as circled.

Outcome. He chanted all 108 ślokas at the ceremony, from memory, in just under forty minutes. Two names stumbled; he recovered both without stopping, which is the skill the run-throughs are for. He still comes on Thursdays. He is learning the Rudram now, with no deadline at all, and says it is a different pleasure.

३

Forty voices at the Ganeshotsav — Kothrud, September 2026

Asked. The Kothrud Ganeshotsav mandal wanted morning chanting the whole street could follow and join — not a performance by experts behind a rope. "Loud enough for the lane, slow enough for grandmother" was the brief, and it was a good one.

Did. Ten weeks of rehearsals with a group of forty: my own students plus mandal volunteers who had never chanted in a group. The Rudram for the opening morning, Monday 14 September 2026; the Śrī Sūktam and Mantra Puṣpam for the mornings after. I printed svara-marked sheets for the congregation — the one time Roman letters are allowed anywhere near my teaching is never, so the sheets were Devanāgarī, large type, and the group carried the tune for anyone reading along.

Outcome. By the fourth morning the lane was chanting the refrains without the sheets. The mandal has asked the group back for 2027 — Saturday 4 September is the opening morning — and this year's rehearsal batch is the one enrolling now, on the syllabus page.

४

A student by call — the Īśa Upaniṣad, Dubai, 2026

Asked. A software engineer in Dubai, born in Satara, an hour and a half behind my clock. She had chanted as a child at her grandmother's knee and lost it in twenty years of work. She wanted the Īśa Upaniṣad — all 18 verses — back in her own voice.

Did. One-to-one by video call, two sessions a week, early morning her time. Not a beginner, so no foundation month — instead, two weeks of ear-checking folded into the first sessions, because a remembered svara and a correct one are cousins, not twins. Then the text at the printed one-to-one pace: 18 verses, done in six weeks.

Outcome. She chants the Īśa every morning before work. In August she began teaching her daughter the first four verses, the way her grandmother taught her — which makes her the fourth generation of that particular classroom, and the first one held over a phone line.
