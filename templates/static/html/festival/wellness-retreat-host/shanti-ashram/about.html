The ashram · since 1978

An orchard that
learned to keep still.

The house was built by a schoolteacher from Almora who planted the orchard first and the walls second. It has been a family home, a widow’s refuge, and — for the last eleven years — a small retreat with eight rooms and one long table.

Tara

I came here in 2011 to look after my aunt for six months. She had broken a hip and refused to leave the hill, so I took a leave from my job in Bengaluru and moved into the room nearest the kitchen. She recovered, slowly, and by the time she was walking to the temple again I had understood something I have not been able to explain to any employer since: I did not want to go back.

My aunt died in 2014 and left the house to me and my cousin, on the condition that we not sell it. My cousin, sensibly, wanted nothing to do with a leaking roof at 1,850 metres. I bought him out with a loan I am still paying, and opened three rooms to guests the following spring.

I had been teaching yoga since 2003, badly at first and then properly, in living rooms and a community hall in Bengaluru. What I did not know was how to run a house on a hill: how to get a plumber in November, how much wood a winter takes, how to keep a generator alive. Mohan taught me most of it, with a great deal of patience and one or two remarks about city people.

I teach four mornings a week and take three for myself. That is not modesty; it is the only way the place stays honest. A retreat run by someone who never sits is a hotel with better lighting.

There is no television, no room service, and no bar. There is a library of 400 books, most of them left behind by guests, and a dog called Bhola who has opinions about who sits where.

Who keeps it

Four people,
and a dog.

Nobody here is staff in the hotel sense. Everyone eats at the same table and is paid a wage that we publish in the kitchen ledger, because secrecy about money is how small places go wrong.

  • Host

    Tara Naidu

    Teaches four mornings, cooks nothing, keeps the bookings and the peace. Has been known to turn away a booking that felt wrong, which is a luxury of eight rooms.

  • Cook

    Kamla Devi

    From the village below the ridge, cooking here since 2016. Two wood fires, one gas ring, no recipes written down. Will feed you until you say stop, and then once more.

  • Caretaker

    Mohan Singh

    Runs the water, the wood, the generator, and the path. Walks the four kilometres to the temple most mornings and thinks anyone who drives is in a hurry for no reason.

  • Housekeeping

    Bimla Bisht

    Changes the beds, airs the quilts, and leaves a hot-water bottle in the bed on cold afternoons. Her daughter is studying in Almora and comes on Sundays.

What we ask

Five small agreements.

  1. 01

    Keep the morning quiet. Until nine, voices stay low and phones stay in the room. It is the only rule we enforce.

  2. 02

    Eat what is cooked, or say so early. Kamla plans the week on Monday. Tell her at booking, not at the table.

  3. 03

    Do not give food or money at the gate. It changes the relationship with the village in ways that are hard to undo. If you want to help, ask Tara.

  4. 04

    Walk the path, not the flower beds. The orchard is old and the soil is thin; a shortcut costs three years.

  5. 05

    Leave the book you finished. The library is a lending chain, not a collection.

Come and see

The kettle is on from six.

If you are in Almora and want to look before booking, come up for tea between four and five in the afternoon. Ring the bell at the gate; Bhola will announce you.
