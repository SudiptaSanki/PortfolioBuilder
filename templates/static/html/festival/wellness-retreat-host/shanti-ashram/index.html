The shape of a day

Nothing is compulsory.
Everything is at the same time.

We keep the timings so that you do not have to decide anything before breakfast. If you sleep through the six o’clock sit, the tea is still hot at eight. If you walk instead of stretching, the mat stays rolled.

  1. 06:00

    Silent sit

    Forty minutes in the upper room, cushions and a bell. You may simply sit and listen to the birds; nobody will check whether your eyes were closed.

  2. 07:15

    Yoga on the terrace

    Seventy-five minutes, slow and joint-led. Blankets for the cold, chairs for anyone who cannot kneel. Tara teaches four mornings; the other three are self-practice with a written sequence left on the mat.

  3. 08:45

    Breakfast, in silence

    Porridge, fruit from the orchard, eggs if you eat them, and a pot of chai that never quite empties. Silence is held until nine, then the verandah fills with talk.

  4. 10:00

    The hill or the kitchen

    A walk to the old temple and back is two hours at a slow pace. Or help the cook knead dough, or read on the wall. The point of the morning is that it belongs to you.

  5. 13:00

    Lunch, one table

    Rice, two vegetables, dal, and something sour. Everything is grown within thirty kilometres, most of it within three. You will be asked to serve the person opposite you before yourself.

  6. 16:30

    Tea and nothing to do

    The only structured thing after lunch is tea. It is deliberately early, so that the gap before dinner is long enough to feel.

  7. 18:30

    Evening practice

    Restorative postures, a short chant if there is a group for it, and a guided rest. Lamps only; the generator goes off at seven.

  8. 19:30

    Dinner, then quiet

    Soup, roti, a vegetable, and something warm. The dining room closes at nine. After that the only light is the path to the rooms.

    Where you sleep

    Eight rooms, three kinds.

    Kuti · 3 rooms

    The stone kutis

    Built from the hill’s own stone in 1978, with tin roofs that make rain sound like applause. A bed, a chair, a window seat, a bathroom with hot water at six and seven. No plug point beside the bed, deliberately.

    ₹4,800 a night, all meals

    Kamra · 4 rooms

    The verandah rooms

    Four rooms in the old orchard house, each opening onto the same long verandah and the same view. Shared bathrooms, thick quilts, and a chair that has been sat in by more people than anyone can count.

    ₹5,600 a night, all meals

    Tent · 1 room

    The cliff tent

    One canvas tent on a wooden deck at the far edge of the orchard, twenty steps below the house. A proper bed, a solar lamp, a shared bathroom up the path. Cold at night in January, and worth it for the sunrise.

    ₹3,900 a night, all meals

    Rates include both meals, tea, and all practices. Single occupancy only; we do not take groups larger than eight. Children over twelve are welcome if they can hold the morning silence with the rest of us.

    One table

    The cook decides.
    You eat what grows.

    Lunch and dinner are vegetarian, cooked by Kamla Devi on two wood fires and one gas ring, from what the village brings on Tuesday and Friday. There is no menu to choose from. If you cannot eat something, say so when you book and she will cook around it — she is not offended, she is organised.

    We do not serve coffee after two in the afternoon, alcohol at all, or anything in a plastic bottle. The water comes from the spring above the house and is boiled, then filtered, then poured from a steel jug that has a dent in the shape of a thumb.

    • Morning

      Millet porridge with jaggery; fruit from the orchard; eggs from the three hens; chai with buffalo milk.

    • Lunch

      Rice, madua roti, two seasonal vegetables, dal, and a small bowl of something sour — lemon pickle, or the fermented greens called gundruk.

    • Four o’clock

      Tea, and whatever Kamla has baked if the power was on. Usually a plain cake with cardamom.

    • Dinner

      Soup from the lunch vegetables, roti, a dry vegetable, and kheer on the last night of every stay.

    The calendar

    Three nights,
    or a week if it holds you.

    We take bookings two months ahead. The quietest weeks are in February and September; the busiest are the last week of December and the week of Diwali. If you are coming for a specific retreat, book against its dates rather than the room.
