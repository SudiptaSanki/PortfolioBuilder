One room, three tables, and a village that prints

Bagru has been a printing village for three hundred years. The Chhipa families came with the trade and stayed; almost every lane in Chhipa Mohalla still has a table in it, and the smell of harda and indigo is in the walls.

Bhanwar Lal started with two blocks

In 1986, Bhanwar Lal Chhipa rented a room at the end of the lane and printed for a wholesaler in Jaipur who supplied the cloth and paid by the metre. He had two blocks, one of them the lotus that still hangs on the wall, and a table his brother helped him build from four mango planks.

For twelve years the workshop printed only for that wholesaler. The break came in 1998, when a designer from Delhi saw a length of indigo booti drying on the roof and asked who had printed it. She ordered sixty metres, paid three times the wholesale rate, and told two friends.

Meera took over the table in 2009. She had trained as a textile designer in Jaipur, which her father considered a waste of a good pair of hands until she started bringing in work he could not have got himself: hotel linen, museum reproductions, a Japanese label that wanted the flaws left in.

The workshop now

Three tables, twenty metres each, in a room with a tin roof and a door that opens onto the lane. The cloth is washed in the village tank, dried on the roof, and printed while it is still damp enough to take the dye.

Six printers work here, two of them women. The blocks are cut upstairs by two carvers, father and son, who have cut for this workshop since 1994 and also cut for three others in the lane. The indigo vat sits in a sunken tank behind the door and is fed every morning.

How a length gets printed

Eleven days for twenty metres, most of it waiting: for the cloth to be washed, for the dye to fix, for the sun.

  1. 01

    Washing

    Grey cloth is soaked and beaten at the tank to strip the sizing. Nothing prints evenly on new cloth.

  2. 02

    Harda

    A tannin bath of myrobalan, which gives the cotton a yellow base and something for the mordant to hold.

  3. 03

    Drying

    On the roof, in the sun, for a day. In the monsoon this is where a commission loses a week.

  4. 04

    Blocking the outline

    The first block lays the registration. Every later block is set against it, which is why the first printer is the senior one.

  5. 05

    Filling

    A second, smaller block carries the background colour. Two blocks, two passes, one register.

  6. 06

    Mordant

    Alum for red, iron for black, printed where the colour will fall — not painted on afterwards.

  7. 07

    Dyeing

    Indigo in the vat, madder in the copper. The cloth goes in pale and comes out darker than it will dry.

  8. 08

    Washing off

    Repeated rinses until the water runs clear. A shortcut here is how a print bleeds onto a customer's skin.

  9. 09

    Sun and check

    The length is laid out full and read metre by metre. Misregisters are marked with tailor's chalk.

  10. 10

    Folding

    Folded selvage to selvage, tied with cloth tape, and labelled with the metre count and the block.

The water, honestly

Bagru's printers use a great deal of water, and for decades it went into the ground untreated. The village's wells are brackish in places, and the tank downstream is not what it was when Bhanwar Lal was a boy.

Since 2019 this workshop has run its wash water through a reed bed behind the building before it leaves the compound. It cost ₹3.4 lakh, it takes up a quarter of the yard, and it does not make the water clean enough to drink — it makes it clean enough for the field next door. Three other workshops in the lane have built the same bed since.

We also refuse reactive dyes for commission work, which costs us the bright turquoise that a screen printer can deliver in a day. If a customer wants that colour, we say no and send them elsewhere.
