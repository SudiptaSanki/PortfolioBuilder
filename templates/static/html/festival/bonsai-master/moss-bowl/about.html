Satoru Mori, born 1976 in Yamanaka Onsen. Apprentice at Kōyō-en from 1996 to 2007. Opened Moss Bowl in a former kimono store in Nomachi in 2003, and has kept the same four benches, the same courtyard, and roughly the same number of trees since.

Profile01

Where it started

A timber yard, and a tree nobody wanted

My father sold cedar and cypress for house frames, in a yard above the Yamanaka river. Behind the drying shed there was a kuromatsu in a cracked pot, brought in by a customer who had died before paying for it. I was nine. I watered that tree because nobody else did, and then I started reading about why it was shaped the way it was.

There was no bonsai nursery within an hour of us. The nearest was Kōyō-en, in Komatsu, run by Masao Kitajima, who took one apprentice every few years and did not advertise. I turned up on a bicycle with a photograph of the pine and asked for work. He looked at the photo, then at me, and said the tree was overwatered.

He was right. It died that winter. I have been trying to keep the next one alive for twenty-nine years.

The apprenticeship

Eleven years: three on cleaning, soil and pots; four on wiring and watering; four on cutting, and only at the end on deciding. The rule at Kōyō-en was that the apprentice may not make a design decision until the master can leave the nursery for a week and find nothing changed on his return. I was allowed to leave in 2007, and I am still not sure I passed.

What I took from him was not a style. It was a habit of looking at a tree for a full season before touching it, and a suspicion of any tree that looks finished.

“A bonsai is a tree kept in a conversation. If you stop answering, it becomes an object.”
Masao Kitajima, Kōyō-en, 1999

02 — The path

A slow chronology

Not a career. A series of rooms that happened to have trees in them.

  1. 1976

    Born in Yamanaka Onsen

    A hot-spring town built around a river and a timber trade. My first memory of a tree is a cedar being loaded onto a truck, which is not romantic but is accurate.

  2. 1996

    Knocks on the door at Kōyō-en

    Masao Kitajima takes one apprentice every four or five years. He took me on a trial of six months, which became eleven years. The first three years I was not allowed to hold a pair of shears.

  3. 2003

    Opens Moss Bowl in Nomachi

    A former kimono store, 18 metres by 6, with a courtyard where the bolts used to be aired. The rent was low because the ceiling was low. The pines did not mind.

  4. 2011

    First workshop, four seats

    Advertised with a handwritten card in the window. Three people came, one of them a neighbour who already grew chrysanthemums and wanted to argue about soil.

  5. 2019

    Kokufu Prize, Tokyo

    For a shimpaku with a deadwood vein that took nine years to expose. The tree was bought at the exhibition and now lives in Aichi. I still have the pot.

  6. 2026

    Still four benches

    I have been asked to expand three times. Each time I count the watering hours in August and say no. A bench you cannot reach in one morning is not a bench, it is a nursery.

03 — Method

What I actually do, and in what order

The order matters more than any single technique. Most ruined trees I see were not badly cut; they were cut in the wrong month.

Look for a year. Before a tree comes onto my bench, I want photographs of it in leaf, in flower if it flowers, and dormant. A tree photographed only in its best week is a sales photograph, not a tree.

Fix the roots first. Nebari is not a cosmetic. If the root flare is wrong, every branch above it is decoration. I will spend three repottings correcting a root before I touch the crown, and most owners find this frustrating.

Then the trunk line. One decision: which way does the tree lean, and into what. Everything else follows from that line. If a tree has no line, it has no future as a bonsai, only as a plant in a pot.

Branches last. Primary branches are chosen, not grown. I keep two candidates for every one I want, and cut the loser in the third year, when I know how it has thickened.

Wire is a temporary scaffold. Copper on pine, aluminium on soft bark, six weeks and off. Wire left on is the most common injury I see in amateur trees, and it cannot be undone.

Water is the whole craft. In August, Kanazawa is 33 degrees with 80 percent humidity, and a shallow bowl dries twice a day. Anybody can learn to cut. Fewer people learn to water.

The pot comes last. A pot chosen before the tree is chosen is a costume. I keep about 200 pots and choose one only when the silhouette is settled, sometimes years after the tree arrives.

Leave something unfinished. A tree that is complete has nowhere to go. Every tree on my bench has one branch I am still deciding about, and that is the branch that keeps me interested.

“The tree is not a sculpture and it is not a pet. It is a slow argument between what you want and what it will allow, and the argument is the point.”
Satoru Mori · bench notes, March 2024
