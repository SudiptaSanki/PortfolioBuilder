About

One oven, two hands, and a great deal of paper.

Rosa Lindqvist has baked for December in Nørrebro since 2007, in a kitchen with one window and a scale older than she is.

My grandmother baked the same cocoa cake every Christmas of her life, and wrapped it in the same paper, tied with the same string, for people she had decided were family. When she died the recipe came to me on the back of an electricity bill. I have been baking it for other families ever since.

The cake is not complicated. Butter and sugar beaten pale, eggs one at a time, cocoa and flour folded in, sour cream at the end. The chocolate cloak is poured while it is still warm, so it sinks a little and sets with a soft sheen rather than a shell. What makes it hers is not a secret ingredient. It is that she never once rushed the butter and sugar.

I worked in restaurant kitchens for eleven years before this, the last four as a pastry chef at a hotel that wanted everything small and identical. In 2007 I took the December off, baked for friends, and never went back. The kitchen in Nørrebro came with a long table, a window facing the courtyard, and a rent I could just about carry.

Why paper

A cake wrapped in paper is a cake somebody carried. The paper creases, the string presses in, and by the time it reaches the table it looks handled, which is the point. I use unbleached paper and cotton string, and a wax seal on the larger orders because my grandmother did, and because it makes people slow down before they open it.

Everything is baked in the week it is collected. In December that means the oven runs from six in the morning and I stop answering the door at six in the evening. There is no second oven and no plan to buy one. The limit is the reason the cake tastes the same on the twentieth as on the first.

What I will not do

I do not bake with fondant, and I do not make cakes in the shape of other objects. If you need a cake that looks like a reindeer, there are excellent bakers in this city who do that beautifully; I am not one of them. I also do not promise a cake I cannot make well in the week you need it. If the list is closed, it is closed.

Decembers I remember

  1. 2007

    The first order

    Twelve cakes for a bookshop on Blågårdsgade. I burned the first batch and cried, then baked it again.

  2. 2011

    The hotel that ordered forty

    Forty small cocoa cakes for a Christmas lunch. I learned to work in threes and to write everything down.

  3. 2015

    The classes begin

    Six people around the long table, one oven, and a great deal of flour on the floor. They still come back.

  4. 2019

    The paper

    A printer in Valby made me 2,000 sheets of unbleached paper with the seal printed faintly. I still have some.

  5. 2024

    1,240 tins

    The busiest December yet, and the first one where I said no to forty orders. The oven, not the ambition, sets the number.

Come to a class, or order the cake your family remembers.

Both begin the same way: you write to me and tell me what the table looks like.
