Handmade in Salem, Mass. since 2019

A copper pot, a thermometer, and a door with a bell on it.

Sugar Fang is June Vance and two part-time cooks in a nine-by-fifteen room behind the shop. Every sweet on the counter was made in that room, by hand, in a batch small enough for one person to watch.

The pot is French, copper, and older than I am. I bought it in 2018 from a man in Lynn who had used it for jam and had stopped making jam. It holds nine kilos of sugar comfortably and eleven if you are brave, and the reason everything here is made in small batches is that the pot will not permit anything else.

I learned confectionery the way most people learn it now, which is badly and from the internet, and then properly from a woman named Ruth who ran a fudge shop in Rockport for thirty-one years. Ruth did not use a thermometer. She used the back of a spoon, a bowl of cold water and a look on her face, and she could tell firm ball from hard ball by the sound the drop made. I use a thermometer. I have tested it in boiling water twice this year and it reads 100°C, which at this elevation is correct.

What actually happens in a batch

  1. One

    Weigh everything, twice

    Confectionery is sugar, water and interference. Glucose syrup stops crystallisation, fat changes the texture, acid inverts the sugar. Weigh to the gram on a scale that goes to two kilos; guessing is how you get grainy fudge.

  2. Two

    Dissolve, then stop stirring

    Sugar goes into water over heat and gets stirred until it is clear, and then the spoon comes out. After that the pot is swirled, never stirred, or the crystals on the side of the pot seed the whole batch and you get a mouthful of sand.

  3. Three

    Watch the temperature, not the clock

    Every stage has a number. Thread at 110°C, soft ball at 113, firm ball at 118, hard crack at 150. A batch is not a recipe, it is a curve, and the room's humidity moves the curve by a couple of degrees in August.

  4. Four

    Pour, cut, wrap, label

    Everything is cut by hand with a knife and a ruler because a cutter wastes the edges. Labels are printed on a thermal printer in the back and carry the batch number, the date and the allergens, which is the law and also useful when someone asks why Tuesday's caramels were darker.

Where things come from

Cane sugar from a refinery in Baltimore, glucose syrup from a supplier in New Jersey, cream and butter from a dairy in Ipswich that we visit twice a year. The pumpkin in the fudge is from a farm in Topsfield, roasted and puréed in September by the woman who owns it. The gelatine is beef, which is on every label, because a surprising number of people ask.

The chocolate is the one thing I buy by the box from a small importer in Somerville, and it is the reason the honeycomb costs seven dollars instead of five.

October

In October we make fangs from five in the morning until the trays run out and then we make more. The queue on the last weekend goes around the corner to the apothecary, and there is a man who dresses as a plague doctor and stands in it, and I have never learned his name. On the first of November we close for four days and I do not go near the kitchen.
