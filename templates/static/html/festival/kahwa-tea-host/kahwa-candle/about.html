About

Eleven winters of keeping the samovar lit.

Aaliya Zargar, born in Rajbagh, hosts kahwa the way her grandmother did — slowly, with more almonds than anyone expects, and never for a fee that makes a guest count the cups.

The pot came to the house before I did.

The samovar is copper, four and a half litres, and it arrived in this house in 1962 as a wedding gift carried across from Amira Kadal. My grandmother Hafiza kept it lit from the first snow to the last, and the smell of cinnamon in a cold corridor is the first thing I remember about winter.

She had a way of making the pot last through an afternoon: the spices went in early, the leaf went in late, and the tray came out with the cups already warmed. Nothing about it was difficult. All of it was decided before the water boiled.

I began hosting in 2015, two trays a month for neighbours, with no plan for it to become work. The wazwan closes came in 2018, after a cousin asked me to stand behind the serving line at his wedding. The teaching began in 2021, mostly for young couples moving into their first kitchens in Srinagar. The festival trays followed in 2024.

Everything in kahwa is a decision you make before the water boils. After that you are only watching.

The blend

Loose green tea, never a bag. Cinnamon quill rather than powder, green cardamom crushed just enough to open, three cloves to a litre, and a few turns of black pepper that nobody tastes but everybody misses. Saffron from Pampore, bloomed in a spoon of warm water before it meets the pot.

The almonds are Kupwara, slivered the same morning, and they go in at the end so they keep their snap. There is no milk in kahwa. There is no rose water unless a host asks for it by name, and then it goes in the cup, not the pot.

The table

  • The pot goes on before the first guest is expected, not after they arrive.
  • Cups are warmed with the first rinse, then dried on a cloth kept only for this.
  • Nobody gets a second cup until everyone at the table has had a first.
  • The tray is not cleared while someone is still drinking, however late the hour.

What I do not do

No milk, no tea bags, no saffron substitute, no more than fourteen at a house table. If a booking needs more than that, I bring a second host with me and we make two pots rather than one tired one.

The years

How it accumulated

  1. 1962The samovar arrives

    A wedding gift from Amira Kadal, carried across the river on a winter morning.

  2. 1997First pot on my own

    Nine years old, a stool at the stove, and strict instructions not to let it boil over.

  3. 2015Two trays a month

    Neighbours first, then their cousins. The first paid tray was for a name-day in Sonwar.

  4. 2018The wazwan closes

    Standing behind the serving line at a cousin's wedding, and every wedding after it that winter.

  5. 2021Teaching the pot

    Saturday mornings for couples in their first kitchens, six at a time, the bench in the kitchen.

  6. 2024Festival trays

    Herath, Eid and Nauroz trays carried hot across the city in lidded carriers.
