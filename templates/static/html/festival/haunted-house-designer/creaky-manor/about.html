Approach

The scare is a piece of joinery.

Fifteen years of building rooms that behave. A haunt is a route, a schedule and a set of doors, in that order — the fright comes out of the joinery.

I trained as a stage carpenter and spent six years building sets that had to survive eight shows a week. A haunt is the same problem with worse hours: a set that has to work forty times a night, in the dark, with people running through it.

The first thing I build is not a scare. It is the route: how many people per group, where they wait, where they turn, what they see when they turn. A corridor that turns left is a different corridor from one that turns right, and the difference is not the wall — it is what the visitor was looking at when they turned.

Scares are counted like any other material. A nine-room haunt with twenty scares is not twice as frightening as one with ten; it is twice as tiring, and the second half of the queue hears every scream. I would rather spend the budget on three scares that land than on twelve that do not.

What I will not do

  • I will not design a room that cannot be exited in the dark by someone on their hands and knees.
  • I will not use a scare that requires an actor to be touched, or to touch anyone.
  • I will not sign off a route that has only one exit, however good the finale is.
  • I will not run a haunt without a written safety plan and a rehearsal with the actual volunteers, not just the paid crew.

Who it is for

Half my work is charities and church halls with a budget under two thousand pounds; the other half is attractions that need a room rebuilt properly. Both get the same plan drawing. The charity haunt is usually the more interesting problem, because there is no money to hide behind.

I also teach a one-day course in October on safe scares and set carpentry. Six people, in the workshop, with saws.
