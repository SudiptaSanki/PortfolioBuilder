The atelier · Rua 31 de Janeiro

One room, two tailors, and a beader who counts out loud

I am Aloma Fernandes. I have been making carnival costumes in this building since 1998, in the room where my grandmother sewed church vestments. The sewing machine is still hers; the table is not.

Fontainhas is a lane of yellow and blue houses with balconies that almost touch. In February the whole neighbourhood smells of welding, glue and marigolds, because every second house is building a float or a costume in its front room. I grew up thinking that was normal.

My grandmother made vestments for the church of St. Sebastian. She let me hold the hem while she pinned it, and taught me that a garment is finished on the inside. I went to Mumbai to study fashion, came back with a diploma and a lot of ideas about minimalism, and discovered that carnival has no interest in minimalism whatsoever.

The first costume I made for a float was 9 kg. The dancer lasted four minutes. That is still the most useful lesson I have had.

Since then the atelier has been built around one question: what can a person actually wear for four hours in the Goan heat and still dance? Everything else — the sequins, the feathers, the colour — comes after. We weigh every piece on a hanging scale before it goes out. We fit twice. We walk the costume on the cobbles outside, in the shoes the dancer will wear, because a hem that clears the floor in the atelier will catch on a paving stone twelve metres away.

I do not use foam armour or moulded plastic. I use cane, wire, buckram and a lot of hand stitching, because those things can be repaired at the side of a parade route with a needle and a pair of pliers. Every costume goes out with a repair kit: matching thread, a curved needle, spare sequins in a film canister, and a length of fishing line.

The work is seasonal and it is not glamorous. In November the room is full of half-finished skirts and the floor is silver. In March it is empty and I clean the machine and swear I will not do forty costumes again. In June someone calls about a wedding and I say no, and in August the carnival committee calls and I say yes.

Atelier rules

Things I will not bend on

They are not aesthetic preferences. Each one came from a costume failing in public.

  1. 01

    Weigh it before you bead it

    The frame, fabric and base layer go on the scale first. If the empty costume is already 4 kg, the sequins have 2 kg to work with.

  2. 02

    Two fittings, no exceptions

    One in calico, one in the real cloth. A costume fitted once is a costume that will be altered in a hotel bathroom at midnight.

  3. 03

    Walk it on the cobbles

    Outside, in the real shoes, for a hundred metres. Anything that drags, catches or slips is fixed before the piece is photographed.

  4. 04

    It must be repairable

    No glue-only joins on anything structural. The repair kit that goes with the costume has to work under a streetlight.
