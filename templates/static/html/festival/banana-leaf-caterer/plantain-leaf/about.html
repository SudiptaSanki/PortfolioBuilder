A kitchen that only does sadya

We do not do biryani, we do not do buffet counters, and we do not cook anything on the leaf that we would not eat at our own table. One menu, cooked properly, at whatever scale the day asks for.

It began with a wedding that went wrong

In 1998, Ramanunni Menon cooked for his niece's wedding at the family house in Kunnathurmedu because the caterer who had been booked did not turn up. He had run a tea shop before that, and a small mess for mill workers. Four hundred and twenty guests ate that afternoon, the payasam ran out exactly at the last leaf, and by the following week he had three bookings.

The menu has not changed since. Twenty-six items for Onam, twenty-four at a wedding, sixteen for a house. What has changed is the number of people cooking it and the size of the fire.

Ramanunni still comes in at four in the morning and still tastes the sambar from the same steel ladle. His daughter, Lakshmi, runs the bookings and the accounts, and his son, Hari, runs the second kitchen in Pollachi. Nobody in the family has ever worked in another catering company.

SOURCING

Everything within sixty kilometres

Not a slogan — a rule that started as common sense and became the reason the food tastes the way it does.

  • RiceRed matta from a mill in Kizhakkumpuram, 11 km. Milled in the week it is cooked.
  • CoconutTwo acres of the family grove at Pudussery, plus a neighbour's. Grated by hand from 04.30.
  • Banana leavesMannarkkad, 42 km. Cut the previous evening, sixty to a bundle, delivered by six.
  • VegetablesPalakkad market at five, from four regular farmers. Ash gourd and pumpkin from Kongad.
  • TamarindPalakkad market, from stock aged two years in earthen jars.
  • JaggeryCoimbatore, 55 km, for the sharkara upperi and the ada pradhaman.
  • GheeThrissur, 76 km — the one exception, and the only thing that travels further than sixty.

What we refuse to buy

No pre-cut coconut, no bottled sambar powder, no banana leaves older than a day, no fried chips from a packet. The upperi is sliced at four in the morning by two people who have done nothing else for eleven years, and it is the item guests ask about most.

When a harvest fails — and the ash gourd did, in 2023 — we tell the family before the menu is agreed rather than substituting quietly. That year the olan was made with yellow pumpkin and we wrote it on the board at the venue.

Food safety

FSSAI licence 11322004000123. Cooking and holding temperatures are logged every hour on a paper sheet that travels with the van. A 100 g sample of every dish is retained for forty-eight hours in the fridge, labelled with the venue and the date. The kitchen is cleaned twice — once at the end of service, once at four the next morning.

THE MORNING

Four hours from fire to leaf

Nothing is cooked the day before, and nothing is reheated. This is the timetable the kitchen works to, written on the wall beside the door.

  1. 04.00Fires lit, coconuts openedFour burners. The sambar goes on first because it needs the longest; the payasam goes on last.
  2. 05.30Vegetables cutEleven cooks at three tables. Ash gourd, pumpkin, raw banana, cabbage, cucumber, beetroot — each in its own vessel.
  3. 07.00Chips fried, pickles openedUpperi and sharkara upperi fried in coconut oil, drained on newspaper, packed in steel trays with lids.
  4. 08.30Tasting, then loadingRamanunni tastes every vessel. Insulated boxes are loaded in serving order, not in cooking order.
  5. 09.00The first van leavesChips go ahead of everything, because the leaf is laid with them before guests sit.
