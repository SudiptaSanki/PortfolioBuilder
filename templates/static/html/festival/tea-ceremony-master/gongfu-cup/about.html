The table Method Sessions Book

Eight cups from the same leaf, and none of them the same tea.

Gongfu is not a ceremony in the way a wedding is a ceremony. It is a method of brewing that happens to be slow, and the slowness is a by-product of doing it properly rather than the point.

Where it comes from

The method is from the Chaozhou region of Guangdong and the mountains of northern Fujian, where it is called gongfu cha — tea made with effort, or with skill, depending on which character you read it as. It grew up around oolong: a leaf that is rolled, roasted and stubborn, which gives everything at once in a big pot and gives it in stages in a small one. The equipment is ordinary. A small gaiwan or a Yixing pot, a fair cup to even out the strength between pours, tiny tasting cups, a tray that takes the spill.

What surprises people who have only had tea from a bag is the quantity of leaf. Seven grams in a hundred millilitres is not a mistake; it is roughly four times what a Western teapot uses. The leaf is not steeped, it is flashed — eight seconds, ten, twelve — and each flash is poured off completely so the leaf never sits in water it has already given to. The result is a series of cups that move from roast to fruit to honey to something faint and sweet at the end.

“Anyone can make the first cup strong. The skill is in the fifth, when the leaf has nothing left to hide behind.”

How I came to it

My grandmother brewed tea in a covered bowl in a Tangra kitchen and called it medicine, which it mostly was — ginger, jaggery, too much sugar. The gongfu method I learned much later, and not from her. In 2009 I spent twenty months in Chaozhou, first washing cups and then, slowly, being allowed to pour, under a tea master who had no interest in teaching foreigners and even less in the word “ceremony”.

What she taught was arithmetic and attention. How much leaf for the vessel. What temperature for the leaf. How long before the water turns the tea from something alive into something brown. That is what I do here, in a room off Gobinda Chandra Lane, with a table that seats six and no music.

The room

It was a tannery storeroom. The walls are still the original brick, painted once, and the floor is stone, which is why the room stays cool enough to keep a kettle at a rolling boil without the whole place turning into a steam bath. There is one window facing north, a long table of sal wood, and a shelf of jars with the date and garden written on the lid in pencil. The room smells of tea and, faintly, of the leather that was here for eighty years before it.

How a seating runs

Two hours, three teas, in this order. The times are approximate because the leaf decides, and a room of six people who want to talk will always push the second tea later than planned.

  1. 0–10 min

    Water on. A green or a white tea first, poured while people are still arriving and still looking at their phones. It gives the room something to do with its hands.

  2. 10–45 min

    Six or seven infusions of the first tea, poured in silence for the first three cups. Then talk, if there is talk in the room.

  3. 45–50 min

    The leaf is emptied onto a small plate so you can see what you have been drinking. Wet leaf is more honest than any description.

  4. 50–80 min

    The second tea, usually an oolong. This is the centre of the evening and the one people remember. Eight infusions, the good ones in the middle.

  5. 80–105 min

    An aged tea, or a black if the room is tired. Two rinses, a full boil, and a much slower pace.

  6. 105–120 min

    Something to eat: salt biscuits, a slice of dark cake, fruit. Tea on an empty stomach makes people light-headed, and I would rather not send anyone home like that.
