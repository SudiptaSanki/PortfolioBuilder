Performances · Three engagements, told in full

What the work actually involves.

A taiko booking is logistics wearing a costume: drums that weigh as much as a person, skins that fear rain, counts that have to meet a dancer's counts. Each performance below is told the way it was planned — the brief, the decisions, the outcome — with the numbers attached.

Case 01 — The set the rain rearranged

Riverside Spring Matsuri, Tokyo · outdoor main stage · full set

EngagementFestival full set
Played38 of 46 min
Drums at risk11 — none got wet
OutcomeRebooked for 2027

The brief

The closing set of a riverside spring festival: full 46 minutes, six players, all eleven drums, on an open stage with the river behind it. The five-day forecast said dry. The two-day forecast said rain from 18:00 — twenty minutes before the set.

The decisions

The rain plan went to the committee before the weather did: the odaiko moved upstage under the shrine-side eaves line; the okedo piece — drums carried on straps, the first to get wet — was cut, and its minutes folded into an extended Matsuri Ji; every drum stayed under its cover until its piece; the shime player, not the stage manager, held the call to shorten. When the rain arrived on schedule, the plan simply ran.

The outcome

Thirty-eight minutes played, ending as the rain hardened. No skin got wet — a soaked nagado head is a re-skinning bill and a month without the drum. The committee rebooked the ensemble for the following May before the van was loaded, and the rain plan is now a standard page in every outdoor quote we send.

"The rain plan was in the first email — which drums move, which piece shortens, who decides, and by when. On the day, the weather did exactly what the forecast said, and the set still landed. That is why they are already booked for next year."

Programme Director, Riverside Spring Matsuri — on this performance

Case 02 — Twenty-eight children, one phrase

Minato City Primary School · four-week residency · workshop series

EngagementSchool residency
Sessions4 × 90 min
Students28, ages 10–11
Final pieceMatsuri Ji, 16 strokes

The brief

A primary school wanted more than a demonstration: by the end of a four-week residency, one class of 28 children would perform a real taiko phrase at the school assembly — on real drums, not plastic tubs, and without the ensemble playing along to carry them.

The decisions

The phrase was Matsuri Ji — the same 16-stroke ground rhythm on the pattern board — taught the way taiko is always taught: spoken first. Week one was kuchishoga only, no sticks. Then practice drums in pairs, one child playing, one counting. The shime part — the timekeeper's chair — went to a different student each session, because keeping time is the skill the whole residency was really about.

The outcome

At the assembly the class played the full phrase three times through, unaccompanied except for their own shime player, and stopped together on the final DON. Two students have since joined a community children's taiko group. The school booked the residency again for the next year's class, with the same condition: the children play alone.

Case 03 — Two counts, one stage

Collaboration with a contemporary dance company · theatre run · three performances

EngagementCollaboration
Performances3, full houses
The problemDance in 7, taiko in 8
Shared rehearsals5 days

The brief

A contemporary dance company built a 25-minute work around live taiko — then discovered in the first joint rehearsal that their central phrase was choreographed in seven-counts, and the taiko ground rhythm it had to sit on runs in eight. Neither side could simply switch: the dancers' phrase was the piece, and the ji is the ji.

The decisions

Five shared rehearsals produced a written cue map instead of a compromise: the shime player owns every transition, a two-bar bridge phrase (a counted SU and a DOKO) absorbs the missing beat at each cycle, and the odaiko stays silent for the first half so its entrance marks the dancers' unison section. The cue map — one page, 14 cues — was printed for both companies and is still how the piece is run.

The outcome

Three performances, full houses, no missed cue in the run — the bridge phrase became the moment audiences asked about afterwards. The collaboration is the reason joint rehearsal days now appear as a priced line in our quotes: interlocking timings are work, and the work is what makes the night look effortless.
