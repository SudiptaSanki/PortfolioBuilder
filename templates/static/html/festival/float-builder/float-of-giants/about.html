Three bays, eleven people, one weighbridge.

The workshop is a former tile warehouse on Rua do Amparo, rented since 2009. Steel in the front bay, foam and paint in the middle, sewing and silk in the back, and the float leaves through the blue door on the corner.

I learned the trade from my uncle, who built the first Homem da Meia-Noite float I ever rode on. He had one rule that has survived every machine we have bought since: the drawing gets signed before the frame gets welded.

That rule is why the workshop has a drawing table that is bigger than the welding bench. Every float starts as a side elevation at 1:50 with the axle positions marked, the deck weight written in the corner, and the name of the person who checked the arithmetic. Mariana checks the arithmetic. She is a civil engineer who works Tuesdays and Thursdays, and she has stopped three builds that I would have started.

How a float gets made

  1. 1

    The brief and the load sheet

    Length, height, number of giants, number of people on the deck, route gradient and the nearest hospital on the route. All of it goes on one page before a price is quoted.

  2. 2

    Chassis and deck

    A truck chassis is bought or donated, stripped and inspected. The ladder frame is welded onto it, the deck is plated, and the brake lines and lights are re-run to a separate circuit.

  3. 3

    Truss and access

    Towers are welded to the deck with gussets at the base, cross-braced, and fitted with guard rails at 1.1 m. Harness anchors go in before the first figure is lifted.

  4. 4

    Head and hands

    EPS blocks are glued into a billet, cut on a hot wire, then carved by hand with a saw blade and sanded. The head is hollowed to 30 mm and skinned with a mix of wood glue and water before paint.

  5. 5

    Silk and mechanism

    The suit is cut from silk and sewn over an aluminium spine. The head pivots on a bearing at the collar; arms are pulled by ropes run through pulleys at the elbows.

  6. 6

    Weigh, test, sign

    The finished float goes to the public weighbridge in Cabo, comes back with a ticket, and gets a road test at the parade speed of 4 km/h before the fire brigade inspection.

What we will not build

A float with people on an unbraced platform. A giant taller than the overhead cables on the route, unless the route is changed. A deck without a second exit. And nothing that has to be held together by tape on the morning of the parade: if it needs tape at 4 a.m., it was not built properly at 4 p.m. the week before.

We also refuse to skin a float in plastic sheeting, which is cheaper and looks it, and which turns a float into a sail in a crosswind. Silk costs more and moves correctly.

Season

Carnival builds run from August to January. February is for repairs on the route, not new work. We take one non-carnival commission a year, usually a school or a film.
