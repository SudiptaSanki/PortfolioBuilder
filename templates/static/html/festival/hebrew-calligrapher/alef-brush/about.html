The hand · training & method

Four years to learn
how to sit still.

I came to Hebrew calligraphy through graphic design, which is to say I came to it backwards: I knew how a letter worked on a screen before I knew how it behaved under a quill. The correction took four years, one apprenticeship, and a great deal of ruined parchment.

§01

The long way round

I grew up in Ramat Gan in a family that kept nothing handwritten except shopping lists. My grandmother, who came from Aleppo, wrote letters in a cursive Hebrew I could not read as a child and have since spent years trying to imitate. I studied visual communication at Bezalel, graduated in 2011, and worked five years in a branding studio in Tel Aviv designing logotypes that were, in retrospect, perfectly competent and entirely without a body.

In 2014 I took a short course in Hebrew lettering at the Jerusalem Print Workshop and discovered that I did not know how to hold a pen. The instructor, a quiet man named Eli, made us draw the same alef for three hours. I went back the next week, and the week after that. In 2016 he agreed to take me on as an apprentice for two years, on condition that I stop designing for the duration and copy manuscript hands until my hand stopped arguing.

What I learned in that room was not decoration. It was that a Hebrew letter is a set of decisions about weight and space, and that the decisions were made centuries ago by people who were thinking about reading aloud in a room with no amplification. The tagin, the crowns, the little serifs at the top of a leg — they are not ornament. They are the places where the ink slows down.

Since 2018 I have worked only on commission: ketubot, haggadot, lettering for signs and labels, and the occasional object that has nothing to do with paper. The studio is one room in Florentin with a north window, a very heavy table, and a cat who is not allowed on the table.

In the drawer

Eleven quills cut this month, three that I have kept for years because they happened to be right, and a reed from the Jordan that has never worked.

The table is a 1930s drafting table bought from a school in Jaffa that was closing. It is 4 cm out of level and I have stopped correcting it.

§02

The three materials

A commission begins with a phone call about text and a week later with a piece of skin on the table. None of the three materials is optional, and each one fails in its own way.

Klaf

Calf parchment, limed, scraped and chalked by a supplier in Bnei Brak. It has a grain and a direction; written against the grain, the line frays. I buy in small batches and reject about one sheet in five.

Dyo

Oak gall, iron sulphate, gum arabic, lamp-black. Mixed in a saucer, left a day to darken. It bites the skin rather than sitting on it, which is why a mistake cannot be washed out — only scraped, and then the sheet is scarred.

Kulmus

A reed, cut at an angle, split about 4 mm deep. The width of the nib sets the width of every stroke on the page; I cut three before deciding which one the page wants.

שלוםSephardi hand
שלוםAshkenazi hand
שלוםMy italic

Three renderings of the same word — shalom, peace. The Sephardi hand is rounder and sits lower; the Ashkenazi hand is more upright and often finer; the italic is mine, drawn for a sign in Neve Tzedek and used ever since.

A rule

האותיות אינן קישוט

The letters are not decoration. If the words cannot be read by someone standing a metre away, the page has failed no matter how beautiful the border is.

§03

What I will not write

There are lines I do not cross, and it is easier to say them here than in an email after a deposit.

  1. Torah
    No scrolls for ritual use

    A Torah scroll, tefillin or mezuzah scroll must be written by a certified sofer, and the certification is not something I hold. I will give you the name of one in Jerusalem who does it properly.

  2. Names
    No divine names outside a commissioned ritual object

    Writing the four-letter name on a poster or a business card is not a design decision; it is a question of what may be erased. I will use a substitute, and I will explain why.

  3. Time
    No ketubah in three weeks

    A ketubah takes six to ten weeks including text checks. If a wedding is sooner than that, I will do the best I can with a printed text and hand-lettered names, and tell you exactly what is unhandwritten.

  4. Copies
    No digital reproduction sold as original

    A print of a hand-lettered page is a print. It will be labelled, signed as a print, and priced as one.

Also

I do not write on Shabbat, which occasionally means a deadline moves by a day. It has never once been the reason a wedding was late.

«כל אות צריכה לנשום. אם היא לא נושמת, גם הקורא לא ינשום»

Every letter has to breathe. If it does not breathe, neither will the reader.

Eli Yosef, 1938–2020 · my teacher
