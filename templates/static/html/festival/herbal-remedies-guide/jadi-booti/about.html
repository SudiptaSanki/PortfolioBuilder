The hand behind the counter

I am a herbalist, not a doctor. The distinction is the work.

I studied botany because I wanted to know what a plant is before I knew what it does. I apprenticed with a vaidya because I wanted to know what a person is before I gave them a plant. Both are still unfinished.

Vaidehi Deshpande
b. 1986, Wai
Botany, Fergusson College, 2008
Apprenticeship, Wai, 2009–2012
Dispensary, Kothrud, since 2013

Wai, and a kitchen with no labels

My grandmother kept her medicines in the kitchen, not in a cabinet. A jar of ghee with a sprig of something in it, a twist of paper with seeds, a bottle of dark oil that smelled of camphor. She could not tell you the Latin name of any of it, and she could tell you exactly which one to take for a stomach that had gone wrong after a wedding feast.

I went to Fergusson College to learn the Latin names, because I am the kind of person who needs the label. What botany gave me was a way of seeing: leaf arrangement, margin, venation, the difference between a mint and something that merely smells like one. It also gave me a healthy suspicion of every herbal blog I have ever read.

At twenty-two I could identify two hundred plants and had no idea how to talk to a person about their body. That is the gap an apprenticeship fills, or fails to. I spent three years with a vaidya in Wai — sweeping, weighing, grinding, and sitting in on consultations where I was allowed to say nothing. He retired in 2012 and gave me his scales.

A plant does not know what you want it to do. It does what it does, and your job is to find out whether that is what the person needs.

The dispensary

One room in Kothrud with a long table, a set of brass scales, and jars on two walls. I see people on Tuesdays and Saturdays. A first consultation is an hour; most of it is talking, and the last ten minutes are about plants. I take notes by hand and give people a copy, because a prescription you cannot read is not a prescription.

I make small batches: a kadha mix, a balm, a churna, an oil. Nothing is made ahead of a need. There are no shelves of bottled “immunity” here, because immunity is not a bottle and I would rather sell you a spoon of sonf than a promise.

Where the plants come from

Some grow on a half-acre near Wai that belongs to my cousin; some come from a women's collective in Palghar that dries and grades well; the rest from two suppliers I have used for a decade and visit once a year. I ask for the district of origin, and I write it on the jar.

I do not buy from roadsides, and I do not buy “wild-harvested” herbs without a name attached to the harvester. If I cannot trace a jar, it goes back.

What I will not claim

  • That a herb cures cancer, diabetes, or any condition that needs a physician.
  • That a “detox” is a thing the body cannot do itself.
  • That you should stop a prescribed medicine to try a kadha.
  • That an ancient text is a clinical trial. It is a record of attention, which is different.

Teaching

The workshops began because people kept asking me to identify plants in their building compound. Six to ten people, a walk along the Mula in the cool part of the morning, and a rule: nobody picks anything without asking the person whose garden it is. We end at the table with a mortar and a cup of whatever is in season.

I also teach one free session a month for ASHA workers in the Kothrud ward. They know more about the neighbourhood than I ever will; what I can add is the difference between a plant that helps and a plant that only tastes like it should.
