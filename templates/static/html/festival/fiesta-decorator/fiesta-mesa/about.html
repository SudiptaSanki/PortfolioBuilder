A workshop that smells of marigolds and wet paper.

Two rooms behind a courtyard in the Barrio del Artista: one for flowers and one for paper. Everything on a table is either grown, cut, ironed or borrowed within four blocks of here.

The studio

Est. 2012

I started by dressing my sister's wedding table with borrowed silver and a runner my mother sewed from four metres of jade cotton. Eleven years later the runners have their own shelf, but the silver is still borrowed, and the cotton still comes from the same stall in the Mercado de Sabores.

There are three of us. I do the plans, the flowers and the last hour before guests arrive. My brother Beto drives the van and builds anything that has to stand up under its own weight. Ana, who has worked with me since 2016, cuts papel picado and irons linen, and can fold a napkin into a fan while telling you about her grandson.

How a table gets built

The work happens in three passes, and none of them is in the venue until the last one.

First: the measure
I visit the room, or ask for a photograph with a chair in it for scale. Seat pitch is 60 centimetres; that number decides everything else. Then the runner, the flower line and where the serving dishes can land without covering the centrepiece.
Second: the dry run
Two days before, we lay the whole table out on the workshop floor with empty pots standing in for flowers. Beto photographs it from a ladder. That photograph is what we build from at six in the morning, not the plan on paper.
Third: the build
Linen first, then the runner, then glassware, then the flower clusters, then candles last, because candles are the thing that gets knocked over. Papel picado goes up after the flowers, when we can see how much air the room has left.

What things are made of

Linen
Jade and cream cotton from Telas El Carmen, washed twice before it goes on a table so it does not shrink under a hot dish.
Papel picado
Cut in the workshop from tissue bought by the ream. The designs change for the occasion: skulls and cempasúchil for an ofrenda, doves for a wedding, a name cut into the centre flag for a quinceañera.
Cempasúchil
Bought at four in the morning from the growers who come in from Atlixco. Two bunches to a metre of runner, more if the room is wide and the ceilings are high.
Terracotta
Unglazed pots from a family in Santa María Tonantzintla. We own 240 of them and replace about forty a year.
Candles
Stearin, not beeswax. They burn without smoke and the flame is steady enough to photograph. Three quarters of a candle per guest is the rule.
Glassware
Borrowed from three families on the block, which is why there are always a few mismatched goblets. Nobody has ever complained.

My grandmother covered every table with a cloth, even the kitchen table for a Tuesday lunch. That is the whole philosophy. The table gets covered before anyone sits down.

Marisol Robledo

What the workshop does not do

No balloon arches, no chair covers, no printed backdrops with the couple's names on them. No plastic flowers, even when the budget is small: a shorter table with real cempasúchil looks better than a long one with silk. No borrowing against a date: fifty per cent confirms, and the other half is due on the morning of the build.

We do not travel further than two hours from Puebla in October, because ofrenda season is short and the flowers will not wait in a van.

Who we work with

Planners, families and one museum

About half the work comes from families booking directly and half from wedding planners who need a table that photographs well without blocking the room. The museum work is the exception: a two-tier ofrenda for the Museo Amparo courtyard, which we have built three years running.

Families
Mostly Puebla and Cholula, usually through a sister or an aunt who saw a table at another wedding.
Planners
Six planners in Puebla and three in Mexico City. We invoice the planner and stay out of the family's seating politics.
Venues
Hacienda de Chautla, Hotel Cartesiano, Casa Nueve in Cholula, and a long list of family courtyards with one working socket.
Institutions
Museo Amparo, for the Día de Muertos ofrenda in the courtyard, 2022 to 2025.
