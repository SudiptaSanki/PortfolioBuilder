The maker · Kraków, 1981

I learned to raise
silver from a stubborn
old man in Nuremberg.

His name was Herr Bauer and he did not believe in casting. For two years I made nothing but bowls, and he rejected most of them. The menorah I make now has the same seam he taught me to close: one pass of the hammer, one pass of the file, and no solder where the eye can find it.

Before the bench

My grandfather Józef was a silversmith in Lwów before the war. He came to Kraków in 1946 with a bag of hand tools and no workshop, and spent the rest of his working life repairing watches in a kiosk on Dietla. The tools went into a cupboard when he died in 1994; I found them at sixteen, and they were the reason I applied to the academy.

I studied conservation and restoration of metalwork at the Academy of Fine Arts in Kraków, which is a strange training for a maker: you spend five years learning how not to change an object. It gave me a respect for seams and a horror of over-polishing that I have not lost. After graduation I went to Nuremberg for two years, to a workshop that made church silver — chalices, censers, a lot of repair work — and learned the part the academy could not teach, which is speed. A chalice has a deadline. A congregation is waiting.

I came back in 2009 and rented a room on Miodowa, in Kazimierz, above a shop that sold antique postcards. The rent was low because the building had no lift and the floor sloped. The slope turned out to be useful: a bench that leans two degrees towards you makes the filing easier, and I have never levelled it.

Now there are three of us: me, Marta, who is in her fourth year of apprenticeship, and Pan Tadeusz, who comes on Tuesdays and Thursdays to polish and to tell me that I am using too much rouge. He is right.

The room is 4 by 5 metres, with one window facing north. In winter the anneal takes longer because the metal cools before you reach the stake, and everything is planned around that.

01

Four techniques,
one object.

Raising. A flat disc is hammered in circles over a stake, annealed whenever it hardens, until the walls stand up. This is how the menorah stems and the kiddush bowls begin. It leaves hammer marks, which are then planished away with a polished hammer — a slow, quiet, repetitive stage that cannot be rushed without making the surface lumpy.

Repoussé and chasing. The shape is worked from the back, then refined from the front. Used sparingly. A little chased ornament on a rim is enough; a whole object covered in it looks like a souvenir shop.

Hand sawing. The pierced dome of a spice tower is drawn in ink, drilled at the corners, and sawn with a blade finer than a hair. Each opening is filed from both sides, or the light will catch the burr.

Soldering. Hard solder only, in order from the base upward. A soft-soldered joint is a repair, not a making, and it will fail the first time the object is dropped.

The mark

Struck by hand with a 2 mm punch on the base, beside the standard mark. If a piece comes back to be repaired, the mark goes back on last.

02

Where the metal
comes from.

Recycled silver

From a refiner in Gliwice who takes photographic waste and old cutlery. It arrives as grain, is alloyed to 925 with copper, and is assayed by the Kraków office before I use it. About four fifths of what I melt is recycled.

Gold for gilding

24-carat leaf and a mercury-free gilding bath, which is slower than the old method and does not shorten the lives of people in the room. Kiddush cups only.

Steel for weighting

A hidden plate in the base of every standing piece. Silver is light; a menorah that a cat can knock over is a bad object, whatever it looks like.

Parchment and ink

Klaf from a sofer in Kraków, written for each mezuzah. I pay him directly and add his fee to the price openly, because a scroll is not an accessory.

03

What I will not do.

01Cast a menorah and finish it to look raised. Casting is honest for some objects; it is not honest for this one, because the weight and the ring of it give the lie away.

02Sell a mezuzah case without a scroll, or a kiddush cup without telling you the interior is gilt.

03Copy another maker’s piece exactly. I will make a version, and I will say whose work it is a version of.

04Polish out the age on an old object. If you bring me a 1920s candlestick, it leaves with its dents and its patina, only straighter.
