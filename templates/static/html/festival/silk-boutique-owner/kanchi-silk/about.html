The family

One cupboard, four looms, forty-eight years

My mother opened Kanchi Silk with eleven saris in 1978. The cupboard is still behind the counter, the fourth weaving family still works with us, and the coffee is still made on the same stove.

Vaidehi Sridhar

I grew up under the counter, and I have never wanted another job.

My mother Kamala sold her first sari on a Tuesday in June 1978 to a schoolteacher who paid in two instalments. She wrote both payments in a ledger with a blue cover, and the ledger is still in the cupboard, though we stopped writing in it in 1996 when the accountant insisted on a proper book.

The shop was one room then, with a raised platform at the back where saris were stacked in cotton covers. I did my homework on that platform. I learned to tell a korvai from a power-loom border before I learned to tell the time, mostly by being made to feel the join with a finger until I could find it with my eyes shut.

The weaving families

We buy from four families, all within twenty kilometres. The oldest, the Murugans in Pillaiyarpalayam, have woven for us since 1979 and now work only for us and one shop in Chennai. Their loom takes one sari at a time and their son, who is thirty-four, has started drawing his own pallus, which his father pretends to disapprove of.

We pay by the piece, not by the day, and we pay half when the warp is set. It is not a generous arrangement by city standards; it is the arrangement that keeps four looms running through a bad monsoon, which is the point.

Real zari, and how to tell

Real zari is silver wire, drawn fine and gilded with gold. Imitation zari is polyester with a metallic coat. At the counter we test it two ways: draw a thread across an unglazed stone, and hold a short length to a flame. Real zari leaves a gold streak on the stone and a small metal bead in the flame; imitation leaves a grey smear and a hard black ball that smells of plastic.

A sari with real zari is heavier by a hundred grams or more. That is usually the honest test: hand it to someone with their eyes closed and ask which one is the real one.

What I will tell you, even when it costs the sale

If a colour does not suit you in daylight at the door, I will say so. If a cheaper sari on the shelf does the same job, I will show you that one first. My mother did the same, and the shop has survived four recessions and a fire by being the place where the answer is not always the expensive one.

The years

Forty-eight years on one street

The shop has moved twice, both times within the same row of buildings. The cupboard has moved with it every time.

  1. 1978
    The shop opens

    Kamala Sridhar, eleven saris, one room on Gandhi Road, and a blue ledger.

  2. 1979
    The first weaving family

    The Murugans in Pillaiyarpalayam begin weaving for the shop; they are still weaving for it.

  3. 1986
    The second room

    The wall comes down and the shop doubles. The platform at the back becomes the children's homework table.

  4. 1994
    Vaidehi behind the counter

    Twenty-one, just married, and put in charge of the bridal shelf because her mother said she had the better eye.

  5. 2004
    Taking over

    Kamala stops coming in on Mondays, then on Tuesdays, and by the end of the year the shop is Vaidehi's in everything but name.

  6. 2011
    The fire

    A fire in the building next door takes the roof and forty saris. The cupboard survives with smoke damage and a warped door.

  7. 2019
    Online, badly and honestly

    A website with no cart, because a sari bought without being held against the light is a sari returned.
