About — The Guest with a Camera

Anya Deshpande

Festive videographer, Pune. Nine seasons of filming other people's biggest days of the year — from inside them.

The first festival Anya filmed was her own housing society's Ganpati, in 2016, on a borrowed camera, edited on a laptop that shut down twice a night from the heat. The society still opens its festival with that film. It is three minutes long and it has never once been late, because it was finished before the festival ended. That accident became the practice.

The first paid booking came in 2018. The lesson that built everything since came in 2019: a Navratri aftermovie, beautifully cut, delivered nine days after the organisers' sponsor window had closed. A good film, and a failed job. Since that winter, no booking is confirmed until the planner's arithmetic says the film can land inside its window — and if the arithmetic says it cannot, the organiser hears that before any deposit is discussed.

Nine seasons on: 47 festivals — housing-society Diwalis and a 90-year-old mandal's visarjan in Pune; Kolkata for Durga Puja, Madurai for Pongal, the Hooghly bank for Chhath, Delhi for Eid morning. The bench is small and named on every booking sheet: three second operators, two editors. The camera stays at shoulder height, in the crowd's own light. A procession does not repeat itself for a second take; neither does she ask it to.

What she will not do is also part of the record: no drones over a crowd, no staged re-enactments passed off as the day, no filming where the community has said no — even when nobody would ever know. Especially then.

The Working Page

How a booking runs

  1. STEP 01Conditions first

    Before fees, a meeting with the organisers. Where the camera may go and where it may not; the community's consent basis for crowd footage; which faces stay out of promotion. All of it written into the booking — the no-film zones in Case File 02 were agreed at this table, not discovered on the day.

  2. STEP 02A daylight recce for a night festival

    The route walked in daylight: crowd pinch points, the edge of the aarti circle, cable runs, where the light actually falls and where it dies. Night festivals are planned in the day, so the night itself holds no surprises the camera has to solve.

  3. STEP 03The shoot

    Ground level, shoulder height, inside the crowd's own light. No drones, no staging, nothing asked twice. If the festival changes — rain, a diverted route, a cancelled session — the film changes with it, and the case file says so, with the footage count to prove it.

  4. STEP 04The edit

    Ratios printed in Table E1, delivery date from the planner, both agreed before the festival begins. A second editor is insurance you can buy in the open — priced on the booking sheet, called only with the organiser's knowledge.

  5. STEP 05Delivery, inside the window

    Review copy, the committee's corrections, then the master and an archive copy. If a film is ever late, it is reported as late — in the record, with the reason — not quietly re-dated. Nine are on that shelf. The reasons are published with them.

The Honesty Page

A guest with a camera

The community's face

A festival film is the organiser's memory and the community's face. The videographer is a guest at both. That is not a sentiment; it is the working rule the bookings are written around.

  • Consent basis: crowd footage is made under the organiser's stated consent framework — filming announced by the organiser, camera positions marked where the organiser requires them, and an opt-out honoured on the spot, without discussion.
  • Close-ups: an identifiable person's close-up appears in this practice's own promotion only with that person's separate consent. Children are never used in promotion, in any form.
  • The right to be cut: anyone filmed may ask to be removed from a film — before or after delivery — and the cut is made without argument and without a fee.
  • No-film zones: where a community says the camera does not go, it does not go. The gap is named in the delivered film, as in Case File 02.

Footage rights

  • The finished film is licensed to the organiser in perpetuity — their channels, their sponsors, their archive — at no further charge.
  • Raw footage stays with the practice, archived for 24 months against a re-edit, and is never sold, never licensed to stock libraries, never reused for another client.
  • The community's faces are not content inventory. Outtakes are deleted with the archive, not mined.

Money, plainly

  • Fees are Table F1. The written quote matches the estimator's arithmetic line for line — try it before you write.
  • A 40% deposit holds the dates; the balance falls due on delivery.
  • Rain: if weather cancels a festival day, filmed days are billed and the edit fee is waived. The cancellation goes in the record book like everything else — three festivals in the record were rained out or cancelled, and they are counted, not quietly dropped.
  • If this practice must cancel, the deposit returns in full within seven days. It has happened once, in 2021, and it is in the record too.
