About

The camera is the least interesting thing on the street

Eleven years, eighty-four films, and the same lesson every season: the festival does not need you to make it look dramatic. It needs you to be there when it happens.

It started with my father’s puja

In 2012 my father’s para in Bagbazar had a puja with a borrowed camcorder and nobody who knew where to point it. I filmed the five days and cut forty minutes, mostly out of focus, and the committee screened it on the last evening on a borrowed television. Halfway through, an auntie started crying at a shot of her own hands rolling the prasadam. I have been chasing that shot ever since.

The work is a record, not a trailer. Most of a festival is waiting, and the waiting is what people want in ten years: the lane at five in the morning, the men arguing about the rope, the child asleep on the folded pandal cloth. I film the loud parts too, because they are the shape of the evening, but I do not cut them into a rhythm that the festival never had.

I work alone or with one other person. I do not direct crowds, I do not put up lights, and I do not ask a priest to repeat a mantra for the camera. If the mantra is missed, it is missed; that is what the day was.

Four rules

What I will and will not do on the day

  • 01

    Be early, and stay after

    The two hours before a festival are worth more than the hour of it. The light is better, people are not performing yet, and the way a lane fills is the story of the evening.

  • 02

    One subject, held

    A family, a drummer, the man who has carried the idol for nineteen years. A film with a person in it survives; a montage of beautiful frames does not.

  • 03

    Sound before picture

    Record from the moment you arrive. The dhaak, the conch, the crowd at the corner, the silence at 3 a.m. — the cut is built on those, and a festival film with library music is a tourism advert.

  • 04

    Commit to a look, not to a filter

    I shoot flat and grade for the warm sodium of the pandal lights, but I will not put a glow on a lamp that is already bright. The market for teal-and-orange festival films is not short of suppliers.

In the bag

  • Two matching bodies — one on the shoulder, one on the monopod
  • 24–70 f/2.8 — the working lens, 70% of the film
  • 70–200 f/2.8 — the priest, the far bank, the faces
  • 35 mm f/1.4 — the ghat before light, the lane at night
  • 14 mm f/2.8 — the pandal that does not fit in the street
  • Shotgun + softie — the dhaak, the conch, the room tone
  • Two lavs — the priest and the family, nothing else
  • Recorder, 32-bit float — so a shout does not ruin the take
  • Six batteries a body — the pandal supply will drop
  • Two rain covers — one for the body, one cut up for the lens
  • Three cloths, one wet wipe — sindoor, charcoal, salt
  • Dry bag — the ghat, the beach, the boat
  • Monopod — a tripod cannot stand in that crowd
  • Two 128 GB cards — never one card for a whole festival
