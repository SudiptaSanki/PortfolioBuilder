The hand

Forty years, three colours, one river

I am the third generation at this table. My grandmother drew temple cloths, my father drew them faster, and I have spent most of my working life trying to draw them the way she did.

Padma Reddy

The kalam is a stick of bamboo and a piece of cloth.

My grandmother Subbamma drew on the floor of the front room because the light was better there, with the cloth stretched over a mat and a stone on each corner. She kept her kalams in a brass box and would not let anyone else open it.

I was nine when she gave me one of my own, a short one for small work, and a corner of a cloth to ruin. I ruined a great deal of cloth. The first thing she taught was how to sit: left leg under, right knee up, the cloth across the knee so the hand can move from the shoulder and not the wrist. The second thing was to draw the border last, because the border tells you when to stop.

In 1990 I drew my first full cloth, a temple banner for a festival in the town. It took three months and it was not good, but it hung for eleven years, which is longer than anything I have made since.

The three colours

Madder for red, indigo for blue, kasimi for black. Everything else is a meeting of those three: myrobalan gives the pale yellow ground, and where the indigo sits on a myrobalan ground it makes a green that no dyer can mix in a pot.

There is no green dye in this house, no orange, no pink. When a customer asks for a colour that is not one of the three, I say no and explain, and about half of them come back a week later having thought about it.

The river

The Swarnamukhi is a small river and in March it is mostly stones. Washing is the part of the work that cannot be scheduled: the cloth goes in when the water is right, and the red that comes out depends on how fast it is moving. My grandmother said a slow river gives a better red. I have never been able to prove it and I have never been willing to test it in a fast one.

What I teach

Four women come to the house on Tuesday and Thursday afternoons and learn the outline and the myrobalan bath. Two of them will still be drawing in five years, which is the usual proportion. The kalam is cheap; the patience is not.

Yearsii.

In order, as it happened

  1. 1971
    Born in Srikalahasti

    The house is two lanes from the temple, which is why the first cloths I saw were gods.

  2. 1980
    First kalam

    A short bamboo pen from Subbamma's brass box, and a corner of cloth to ruin.

  3. 1990
    First full cloth

    A temple banner for the festival, three months, hung for eleven years and then repaired twice.

  4. 1999
    The dye house

    Married into a family that boiled madder for a living. I brought the drawing; they brought the vats.

  5. 2008
    Took over the house

    Subbamma stopped drawing at eighty-one and handed over the brass box, which I have not opened for anyone else either.

  6. 2016
    Began teaching

    Four women, two afternoons a week, on the condition that they learn the myrobalan bath before the peacock.

  7. 2024
    The Dasavataram

    Three panels for the mandapam, eleven months, and the largest drawing I have made without help.

The border tells you when to stop. That is the whole of composition, and it took me thirty years.
