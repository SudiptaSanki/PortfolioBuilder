I work in the Srikalahasti style — a bamboo kalam, a charcoal sketch, then myrobalan, alum, madder root and an indigo vat. A temple hanging takes eleven weeks before it takes its colour, and none of it can be hurried.

Chapter Ii.

A kalamkari begins as a length of unbleached cotton and a decision about what it will hold. In Srikalahasti the cloth is drawn by hand with a kalam — a bamboo pen wrapped with cloth, its nib a sliver of wood — and coloured with dyes that are cooked, not printed. Nothing about it is quick.

The word means pen work. The cloth is washed, soaked in myrobalan so the fibres will take a mordant, and left to dry in the yard. Then the drawing starts: charcoal first, so a wrong line can be brushed away, then a kasimi outline made from iron filings and jaggery, which smells like a blacksmith's shop and dries to a soft black that never quite leaves the cloth.

Reds come from alum and madder root, boiled and then washed in the river until the water runs clear. Blues come last, from an indigo vat that has to be kept warm and fed. A cloth goes in and out of the water eleven or twelve times before it is finished.

What I will not do

No chemical reds, no screen printing, no outline drawn by a machine. I have been offered all three by people who mean well and want the cloth to be cheaper. A kalamkari that costs a third of the price is a different object, and the woman who buys it will find out in four washes.

Chapter IIIiii.

The process

Eight stages, and the river in the middle

Nothing here is a shortcut and nothing here is a secret. The order is fixed; only the drawing changes.

3 daysi

Wash the cloth

Handloom cotton beaten on stone and soaked to strip the sizing. It shrinks a little, which is why nothing is measured twice until it dries.

1 dayii

Myrobalan bath

Harda powder in warm water. The cloth takes a pale yellow that later makes the greens, and the mordants bite where they are painted.

2 daysiii

Charcoal drawing

The whole composition, drawn freehand and corrected with a cloth. A monkey's tail or a lotus petal can be redrawn a dozen times at this stage.

5 daysiv

Kasimi outline

Iron filings and jaggery, fermented and boiled, drawn with the kalam. It goes on pale green and turns black as it oxidises on the cloth.

1 dayv

Alum mordant

Wherever the cloth will be red. Painted, not dyed, which is why the reds in a kalamkari sit inside their outlines and do not run.

2 daysvi

Madder bath

Madder root ground and boiled. The cloth goes in, comes out, and is washed in the river until the water runs clear.

1 dayvii

River wash

Beaten against the stones at the ghat. The flow of the river does the last of the work; a slow river gives a softer red.

3 daysviii

Indigo vat

Cold vat, kept warm by the sun. The cloth goes in yellow-green and comes out blue as the air reaches it. Then one last wash.
