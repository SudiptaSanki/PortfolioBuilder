I learned nusach by sitting next to someone older and copying, badly, for years.

The rest of this page is about how that happened, what I do at Kehillat Or Hadash, and what I think a choir is actually for on a Friday night.

Budapest, then Cleveland

My grandmother sang in the choir at the Dohány Street synagogue before the war and stopped afterwards. She never explained the stopping. What she did instead, in the kitchen of a flat on Ráday Street, was hum the Friday evening nusach while she cooked, and I learned it the way a child learns a household language: without being taught, and without ever being able to say where a phrase begins.

We left in 1991, when I was nine. Cleveland was cold and the synagogue was large and the music was different — faster, more American, with an organ in the balcony. I joined the children’s choir because a girl in my class did, and I spent two years singing alto parts I did not understand before I realised that the melodies my grandmother hummed were in the service too, buried under the harmony and the tempo.

“A congregation does not need to be told what it feels. It needs to be given the melody it already knows, at the speed it can actually sing.” From a workshop handout, 2023

Training, and what it did not teach

I studied voice at Oberlin, which taught me to breathe and to count and to stand still. Then five years at the Hebrew Union College–Jewish Institute of Religion in New York, which taught me the liturgy, the modes, and the uncomfortable fact that a cantor is not a soloist with a congregation attached.

The part nobody teaches is the room. A Friday service is not a concert with an audience; it is a group of people, some of whom have had a terrible week, trying to say something together. The person at the amud is there to make that possible, not to demonstrate range. Most of my work is deciding when to sing loudly, and then deciding — more often — not to.

Kehillat Or Hadash

I have led the music here since 2012. The congregation is about 240 households in Squirrel Hill, and the building is a converted 1920s school with good plaster and terrible heating. The choir is eighteen volunteers, no auditions, ranging from a retired opera coach to a sixteen-year-old who joined because her grandmother did.

We rehearse on Tuesdays from 7:30 to 9:00, in the social hall, with the folding tables pushed to one wall. Attendance is the only requirement. In exchange I ask people to sing in a language they may not speak, to hold a part while the part next to them moves somewhere unexpected, and to accept that the congregation will always sing flatter than the piano.

Hanukkah, and the rest of the year

Eight Nights, Eight Voices began in 2021 as a way to bring the choir out of the balcony and into a room where people could hear each part separately. Forty singers from four congregations, a small instrumental ensemble, and a programme built around the eight nights rather than around a single hit tune. It has become the thing I am most often asked about, which is a little unfair to the Tuesday rehearsals that make it possible.

Outside the congregation I coach b’nei mitzvah students in nusach, run workshops for congregations trying to start a choir without hiring a professional one, and occasionally sing at weddings and memorials. I say no to more than I say yes to. That is not temperament; it is the only way the Tuesday rehearsal stays good.

The building

A former school hall with a high ceiling and a balcony that holds the choir. The ark is on the eastern wall; the choir stands to its left, which means on Friday nights we sing across the congregation rather than at it. The heating knocks in the pipes during the silent amidah. We have learned to wait for it.

What I am not

I am not a composer of liturgy, though I arrange a great deal of it. I am not the person to hire if you want a concert of Jewish art music for a hall of two hundred; there are better singers for that, and I will tell you their names. I am also not interested in making a service feel like a performance. If the congregation leaves humming, the work succeeded. If they leave admiring the choir, it did not.
