Studio / About

Fifteen years photographing fires for a newspaper. Then a friend asked me to photograph Santa.

The skills turned out to be the same: arrive early, watch the room, wait for the moment when people forget the camera is there.

Wenatchee, and a newspaper

I grew up in Wenatchee, which is a town that knows what winter costs. My father sold farm equipment; my mother taught fourth grade. I got a camera at sixteen because the school paper needed someone to photograph a fire, and I discovered that a camera is a licence to stand closer than anyone else is allowed to.

I studied photojournalism at Central Washington University and then spent fifteen years at a regional daily, most of it on the night shift. Fires, crashes, courtrooms, the fair. I learned to work fast and quiet, to find the one face in a crowd that tells the story, and to leave when the family asks you to.

What I did not learn was how to keep doing it. By 2015 I had photographed more bad nights than good ones, and the lens had begun to feel like something between me and the room instead of a way into it.

“The picture is not the point. The point is that a person let you stand there while they were being themselves.” Notebook, 2014

The first Santa, 2016

A friend who ran a gift shop on Front Street asked whether I would photograph her Santa for an afternoon. She had a chair, a borrowed backdrop, and a Santa named Ray who had done it for thirty years and had no interest in being directed. I brought one light because that was all I had that would fit in the car.

The first child was terrified. Ray did not stand up, did not boom, did not say ho-ho-ho. He stayed in the chair and said, quietly, “You can stand over there if you like.” The child stood by the door for four minutes and then walked over on her own. I took eleven frames and one of them was the best photograph I had made in a decade.

I have done nine Decembers since. The chair is the same; Ray retired in 2022 and the man who replaced him was trained by Ray for a full season before he was allowed to sit in it.

How the room works

I shoot on a 50mm lens at f/3.2, which is close enough that I have to be in the room and far enough that a child can forget me. The key light is a single softbox at forty-five degrees; the fill is a white board that I move with my foot. There is no second light and no assistant, because a second person in the room is another thing to be frightened of.

Sessions are staggered so that no family ever meets another in the doorway. The waiting area is the shop next door, where there is a train set and a pot of coffee, and the parents can hear the door but the child cannot see the queue.

What I will not do

I will not sell a photograph of a crying child. I know the picture is funny to an aunt and will be humiliating at fourteen. If a child is upset, we stop, and the session is moved or refunded. That is not a marketing promise; it is the rule I have kept for nine years, and it has cost me perhaps one session a season.

I will not use fake snow, a smoke machine, or a backdrop printed with a fireplace. The room is warm and real, and the photographs that last are the ones where the room is visible.

I will not upsell at the end of the night. The gallery is the gallery; the prices are on the website; the prints arrive in a tube.

THE RULES

Four things that are not negotiable

They are worth writing down because people ask, politely and often, for the opposite.

01

The child decides

No child is placed on a knee. Santa sits; the child approaches. If they do not, we photograph the room, the tree, or the parent holding them, and that is a real photograph too.

02

No crying frames

Not sold, not shown, not kept. The card is formatted at the end of the session while the family is still in the room, so nothing embarrassing survives the night.

03

One family at a time

The door closes. There is no queue in the frame and no stranger watching. This is the single biggest reason the photographs look calm, and it costs me about thirty sessions a season.
