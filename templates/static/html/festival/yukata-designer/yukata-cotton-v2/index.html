Kyoto · Gion Matsuri season

Cut in Kyoto. Worn to the fireworks.

Every July, Kyoto walks out in cotton for Gion Matsuri — a month of floats, lanterns and hot nights. I am Yui Hoshino. I design and sew yukata, the summer kimono, from single bolts of cotton cloth: measured to your height, cut on this table, and finished before your festival — not after it.

A polyester yukata wears like a raincoat, and a Kyoto July is no place for a raincoat. Cotton breathes. That is the whole argument, and I have it every summer.

The craft · Tanmono

One bolt, one garment

A tanmono is a single bolt of cloth woven narrow — about 37 cm wide and 12 m long — because that is the width a kimono has always been cut from. Nothing is wasted. The two body panels, the two sleeves, the collar and the ties all come off the one bolt, and what remains is a hand's width of selvedge and the pattern matched at every seam.

Measuring takes three numbers: your height, your hip over your clothes, and your yuki — the reach from the back of your neck to your wrist bone, which sets the sleeve. Bring them to the studio, or send them by email; I check every set against the cutting table before a bolt is opened.

Commissions

Sewn for other people's summers

Twenty-four yukata for a Kinosaki ryokan

The ask: guest yukata for a hot-spring inn that would survive commercial laundry and still look like the inn, not a uniform. What happened: katazome seigaiha on plain-weave cotton, seams double-stitched, sizes S–LL — and after two seasons only 3 of 24 needed replacing. The inn ordered 12 more this spring.

"Guests ask where the yukata come from. Nobody has ever asked that about the ones we bought in bulk. Yui-san's survive our laundry and our winters."

— Keiko Arima, Okamoto-ya inn, Kinosaki Onsen, on the ryokan commission

Twelve kingyo for a Bon Odori circle

The ask: a neighbourhood dance circle in Sakyo ward wanted to match — but every dancer chose her own obi colour. What happened: katazome kingyo cut from one bolt so the fish run the same way on all twelve, finished four days before the dance. The circle has since grown to fifteen; three more were sewn from the same bolt's end.

A mother and daughter in asagao

The ask: matching morning glory for Gion Matsuri — for a mother, and for a daughter of nine who would grow out of anything by August. What happened: the daughter's yukata was sewn with growth tucks at the shoulder and waist, the way children's kimono have always been made. Three summers worn; the tucks were let out once.
