The designer · 黒澤 綾

Aya Kurosawa

Born in Arimatsu, where the streets still smell of indigo vats in July. Trained as a dyer, not a fashion designer, which is why she talks about cloth before she talks about clothes.

有松から京都へ

Arimatsu, before the machines

Aya grew up in a house attached to a shibori workshop on the old Tōkaidō road. Her grandfather tied knots; her mother did the dyeing; the whole family did the untieing in winter, sitting around a table with a bowl of hot water and a pair of scissors. The first thing she learned was not design but patience: a single bolt of the fine dot pattern takes about four thousand knots, and every one of them has to be pulled tight enough to resist the dye and loose enough to release it.

She left Aichi at twenty-two for Kyoto with the intention of becoming a dyer of kimono cloth, which is a different trade from making yukata and a harder one to enter. The workshop that took her on had been dyeing for four generations and had two rules: mix your own indigo, and never dye a bolt you have not washed first. She still follows both. The second rule is why her cloth does not shrink on the first hot evening of summer.

“A yukata is the only garment I know that is designed to be worn while it is too hot to think. Everything follows from that.”

Seven years in other people's workshops

Between 2008 and 2014 she worked in three studios: a chūsen dyehouse near the Kamo river, a shibori workshop that supplied theatre costumes, and a small kimono atelier where she learned to draft a pattern from a body rather than from a size. The last of the three mattered most. Drafting by hand means a yukata can be made for a person whose shoulders are uneven, whose hips are wider than the standard, or who is eight months pregnant and wants to wear something beautiful in August anyway.

She opened Tōri Cotton in 2014 in a narrow building in Nakagyō-ku with one vat, two sewing machines and a window that opens onto a roof full of drying cloth. The studio has not grown much since. Two sewers, one dyer (her), one apprentice who is learning to tie knots at a speed that does not yet embarrass the family.

Cotton is unforgiving. It shows every pulled seam and every badly matched pattern. Silk hides a mistake; cotton hands it back to you at the fitting.

What the studio makes, and what it refuses

About ninety yukata a year, in three seasons: spring commissions for the summer, a small ready-made run in May, and repairs from October. The studio does not make jinbei, happi coats or costumes, and it does not print photographs onto cloth. It also refuses, politely, to copy a design from a department store catalogue, on the grounds that the person asking deserves something cut for them instead.

The cloth comes from three places: a weaving mill in Mie for the Matsusaka stripe, a Chita mill for the plain weave, and Arimatsu for the shibori blanks, which are tied by her mother's workshop and dyed in Kyoto. Every bolt is 38 centimetres wide because that is the width of the looms and, more importantly, the width that lets a pattern cross a seam without breaking.

  • Nine to eleven metres of cloth in an average yukata, before the pattern allowance.
  • Six weeks from measurement to fitting, longer for shibori, which is tied in the slack season.
  • Two fittings — one standing, one with the hem pinned, usually on a Tuesday.
  • One repair a year free, for as long as the garment exists, because cotton should be mended and not replaced.

Chronology

  • 1986

    Born in Arimatsu

    Third generation of a shibori family. Learns to untie knots before she learns to read music.

  • 2008

    Kyoto

    Apprenticeship at a chūsen dyehouse. Mixes indigo, washes bolts, and discovers that the cloth decides half the design.

  • 2011

    Pattern drafting

    Moves to a kimono atelier and learns to draft from a body rather than a size. This becomes the centre of the studio's method.

  • 2014

    Tōri Cotton opens

    One vat, two machines, a roof for drying. The first commission is a plain indigo yukata for a bookseller in Demachiyanagi.

  • 2019

    The Arimatsu bolt

    Dyes a 40-metre run of fine-dot shibori with her mother's workshop, the largest single order the studio has taken.

  • 2023

    Repair service

    Opens the October repair bench after a client brings back a yukata worn for nine summers. It needed two patches and a new collar.

  • 2026

    Ninety a year

    The studio holds its ceiling: ninety garments, two sewers, one dyer, and a waiting list that starts in March.
