Cotton that keeps
the evening cool

Yukata cut and dyed in small numbers for the three weeks of summer when Kyoto is too hot to move and too beautiful to stay inside. Every bolt is 38 centimetres wide. Everything else — the pattern, the sleeve, the fall of the hem — is decided with the person who will wear it.

01Cut for one person

No sizes on a rail. Measurements are taken once, from the nape to the ankle and across the shoulders, and the pattern is drafted to them.

02Dyed before cutting

Chūsen dyeing pushes colour through folded cloth. The pattern appears where the dye is pulled, not printed on top, which is why it softens with every wash.

03Sewn in the studio

Two sewers, one iron, no factory. A yukata is four panels, two sleeves and a collar; the whole garment can be unpicked and remade if the wearer changes shape.

Three cottons, three summers

The studio keeps three cloths in stock. None of them is especially precious; all of them get better after ten washes, which is the only test that matters for a garment worn in August.

Matsusaka stripe

Woven in Mie from thick-count cotton, once the cloth of merchants who needed something that would not show the dust of the road. The stripe is woven, not printed, so it survives years of washing and a hot iron.

38 cm · 190 g/m² · stripes in indigo, brown or black

Arimatsu shibori

Cloth bound by hand with thread before dyeing. Each dot is a knot tied by a person, which is why no two bolts repeat exactly and why a shibori yukata is the one the studio makes fewest of. About 4,000 knots to a bolt.

38 cm · 120 g/m² · dots from 3 mm to 12 mm

Chita plain weave

A fine, slightly stiff cotton from the Chita peninsula, dyed by chūsen. It takes indigo deeply and dries fast, which makes it the practical choice for anyone who intends to wear the yukata on a bicycle.

38 cm · 140 g/m² · 14 colours, indigo richest

What a commission actually involves

One visit, one fitting, six weeks. You choose the cloth and the pattern from the sample box; Aya measures you standing in the studio with the window open, because a body in August is not the body you measured in March. The pattern is drafted by hand, the cloth is dyed in a batch of two or three, and the yukata comes back pinned for a second fitting before the hem is closed.

Most clients come once and leave with a garment that fits better than anything they own. A few come back the following summer for a second, usually in a quieter colour.

Start a commission
  • 1

    Measure

    Forty minutes in the studio. Height, yuki, hip, nape to ankle, and the width of the wrist you want the sleeve to clear.

  • 2

    Choose cloth

    Three cottons, a box of pattern samples, and a conversation about where the yukata will be worn. Fireworks need a darker ground.

  • 3

    Dye

    Two to three bolts per batch. Chūsen in summer, shibori whenever the knots are finished. Six weeks, weather permitting.

  • 4

    Fit and close

    Pinned at the shoulders and hem, then sewn closed. The offcut becomes a kinchaku bag at no extra cost.
