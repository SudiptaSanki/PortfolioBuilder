About

The woman at the bench

Moradabad has spun brass for two hundred years. I grew up three lanes from the furnaces, and I have been setting thalis since 2012.

My grandfather kept a bartan shop — pots, pans, lotas by the hundred. What I remember is not the stock but the customers: a woman buying a kalash would lift three, tap each one, and listen. Brass answers when you tap it. A clear ring means the metal is honest all the way through. I still buy my plate blanks that way, and it is why every set I sell is weighed in front of the person who will pray with it.

I started by setting thalis for my own lane — neighbours brought loose pieces inherited from three houses and asked me to make one plate of them. Fourteen Diwalis later that is still the heart of the work: a plate where everything stands where the prayer expects it, nothing rattling, nothing missing, nothing there only to look rich.

“A battery diya on a brass thali is a photograph of a flame. It looks like light and warms nothing, and the prayer knows the difference even if the camera doesn't. I don't sell photographs.”

What belongs on a thali, and why

  • The diya — light is offered first, before words. A pair, because one flame is a lamp and two are a welcome. Deep enough that the oil does not spill when the plate is carried.
  • The kalash — water, the witness of every vow. It stands at the back of the plate, the tallest thing on it, where it cannot be knocked by a reaching hand.
  • The bell — sound clears the room before the prayer enters it. It sits at the front right, in the hand's reach, and it must ring true or it does not go on the plate.
  • The incense stand — scent rises the way a prayer is meant to. Kept apart from the flowers, because ash on petals helps no one.
  • Roli and kumkum — the mark. Roli is rice flour for the tilak that stays; kumkum is turmeric for the mark that blesses. Two small bowls, always side by side, always filled level.
  • The camphor holder — camphor burns and leaves nothing behind, which is rather the point of an aarti. The holder is the deepest piece on the plate, so the flame can be circled without fear.
  • The panchpatra and spoon — water for achaman, sipped and sprinkled. Small, plain, and washed more often than everything else combined.
  • Flowers — the one living thing on the plate, replaced daily. A shallow bowl, so yesterday's petals are seen and changed, never hidden.

The metals, honestly

Brass is the plate's native metal — warm in colour, honest in weight, and it rings. It asks for a polish before festivals and forgives everything else. Copper is softer and darker in temper; it will deepen to a brown-red within a year, and people who love it love exactly that. Silver plate is brass wearing its best clothes — a true silver skin over a brass heart, priced as plating, never as solid silver. And steel: steel is good metal in the kitchen, but on a thali it clatters where brass rings. A plate you can hear is a plate you can trust. I don't set steel.

Care — five minutes before the festival

  • Brass and copper: rub with tamarind pulp, or lemon and salt, rinse, and dry at once with a soft cloth. The shine is already in the metal; you are only removing the year.
  • Never steel wool. It scratches the surface into a thousand tiny lines that hold grime forever. A cotton cloth and patience do the whole job.
  • Camphor soot washes off with wood ash and water — the old way, and still the gentlest.
  • Silver plate: soft cloth only, no dip cleaners. Dips strip the plating a little every time, and a plate should outlive its maker.
  • Storage: wrap in cotton, not newspaper — print ink marks brass. Keep the bell separate so it does not dent the bowls.

How a set comes together

The plates are spun and the pieces cast and turned in Moradabad's workshops, by men and women whose benches stand within a mile of mine. I choose the blanks by ear, set the plate, weigh it, and write the weight on the card that travels with the set. Engraving — names, dates, a line of verse — is cut by hand in the lane behind mine; it takes nine clear days, and I will not promise it in eight. Plain sets leave in three days. Then I light the diyas once, blow them out, wrap the set in cotton, and send it to be lit again by its own people.
