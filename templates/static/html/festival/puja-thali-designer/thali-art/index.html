A thali is not a tray. It is an arrangement of nine things in the right order.

My grandfather raised the first plate in a courtyard off Johari Bazaar with a hammer, a stump and a brass sheet from Alwar. The tools have changed little. What has changed is the occasion: a griha pravesh now asks for a smaller plate than a wedding, and a Navratri thali has to hold nine items without any of them touching. That geometry is the work.

A griha pravesh plate carries an odd number of items. Five is the smallest count that includes a lamp, both powders and rice.

02

What belongs on the plate

Nine objects, each with a job. None of them is decoration, which is why a thali looks wrong the moment one is swapped for something prettier.

  1. Diya

    The lamp is the first thing placed and the last thing lit. A single wick faces north-east on the plate; five wicks are for the aarti that follows.

  2. Kumkum

    Vermilion in a small katori. The bowl is always slightly larger than the amount needed, so the powder can be taken with the thumb without touching the rim.

  3. Haldi

    Turmeric, dry and coarse. It sits opposite the kumkum so the two powders never mix in the carry bag.

  4. Akshat

    Unbroken rice, usually mixed with a little turmeric water and dried. It is thrown, not placed, during a blessing, so it goes on the plate in a loose mound.

  5. Bell

    Held in the left hand during the aarti, never set down on the plate while it is ringing. The handle is cast separately and riveted, so it can be replaced.

  6. Conch

    The right-handed shankh, kept on the plate only for a puja that includes a bath of the deity. It is the one item that must stay wet, so it travels in its own cloth.

  7. Camphor

    Burned on a small stand at the end of the aarti. The stand is a separate piece in a good thali; it is the part that fails first.

  8. Flowers

    Loose marigold or a single lotus, never both. Flowers are the only item replaced during a long puja, so the bowl for them is shallow and easy to empty.

  9. Prasad

    Added at the end, after the aarti, and covered with a small cloth square. Anything with ghee goes in a katori set inside the plate, not directly on the metal.

03

How a plate is raised

A thali begins as a flat disc, 1.2 mm brass, cut with a shears and annealed over a coal fire. The raising is done with a rounded hammer on a wooden stump: forty minutes of circling, then the metal hardens and goes back into the fire. A 24 cm plate takes three annealings.

The engraving comes after the shape, never before. A motif drawn on a flat sheet will distort as the plate rises; the family motif is therefore chalked in once the metal has stopped moving, and cut with a chisel guided by the left thumb.

The last step is the well — the shallow depression that keeps the katoris from sliding. It is pressed with a wooden die, not cut, because a cut well leaves a ridge that catches the cloth when the plate is wrapped.
