The workshop

Three generations, one stump, and a hammer that has been re-handled twice.

The workshop has moved twice inside Johari Bazaar and never further than a hundred metres. My grandfather Girdhari Lal raised plates for jewellers who wanted something to present a necklace on; my father added the engraving; I added the well, because a plate that slides across a table during a puja is a plate that gets returned.

01

Five steps, in the order they happen

Nothing in the process can be hurried, and nothing can be reversed. If the well is pressed too deep, the plate goes back to the scrap bin.

  1. 01 · Cutting

    The disc

    A 1.2 mm sheet is marked with a divider and cut by shears. The edge is filed before it goes anywhere near the fire, because a burr becomes a crack when the metal moves.

  2. 02 · Raising

    Forty minutes a pass

    The disc is annealed over coals until it turns a dull cherry, then hammered in circles over a wooden stump. It hardens, it is annealed again. A 24 cm plate takes three passes.

  3. 03 · Planishing

    Quieting the hammer

    The last pass uses a polished hammer with overlapping, even blows. This is what makes a plate feel smooth to the thumb and what most cheap thalis skip.

  4. 04 · Engraving

    The family motif

    Chalked by hand after the metal has stopped moving. A lotus border is standard; families often bring their own mark, which is copied onto a paper template and kept in the order book.

  5. 05 · The well

    Pressing, not cutting

    A shallow depression is pressed with a wooden die so the katoris sit still. Cutting a well leaves a ridge that catches the cloth when the plate is wrapped for giving.

02

Why the count is odd

A puja thali is arranged in odd numbers: five for a house blessing, seven for a katha, nine for a wedding. The reason is not mystical in origin. An even arrangement has a gap on the axis, and a gap on the axis reads as something forgotten.

The rule survives because it works. Put a lamp at the top and three pairs below it and the eye looks for a fourth pair. Put a lamp at the top and four single items around it and the plate looks finished. Every plate I make is laid out on a paper circle first, with the items drawn at their true size, before any metal is touched.

“A thali is read before it is used. If the eye finds the order, the hand already knows where to go.”

Girdhari Lal, workshop notebook, 1981
03

Keeping it for thirty years

Brass is forgiving and copper is not. Both will outlive the household if they are washed and dried the same day.

  • Wash, then dry immediately

    Warm water and a soft cloth, no detergent. A plate left wet on a counter will spot overnight, and the spots go green in a month.

  • Tamarind, not polish

    A paste of tamarind and a pinch of salt lifts tarnish without removing metal. Commercial polish takes a layer with it every time.

  • Store wrapped, not stacked

    Cotton cloth, never newspaper. Stacked plates scratch their wells, and a scratched well is where the katori starts to wobble.

  • Return it once a year

    Any plate made here can come back for re-polishing and a new foot ring at no charge. We have re-finished plates from 1983.
