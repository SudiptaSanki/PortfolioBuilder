The craft · six stitches

Chikankari is a white flower you can only see at an angle.

The cloth is white, the thread is white, and the flower appears in the shadow the thread casts on the cloth. Everything in the craft follows from that: the cloth has to be thin enough to hold a shadow, the stitches have to be close enough to cast one, and the light has to come from the side.

Where it comes from

Chikankari is usually traced to the Mughal court at Lucknow, and to Nur Jahan, who is said to have liked a white-on-white embroidery so fine that it disappeared in candlelight. The more honest history is that it is a Lucknow craft made by many hands over three centuries, with a vocabulary of stitches that no single person invented.

What is certain is that by the nineteenth century the city had a working trade in chikan: block-printed cloth went out to women who embroidered at home, came back to be washed and ironed, and was sold in the Chowk. That is still roughly how it works. The difference now is the GI tag, granted in 2008, which names Lucknow as the place and sets a standard the trade has found hard to enforce.

The old pieces were on mulmul so fine that a dupatta could pass through a ring. Modern chikan is often worked on georgette or cotton voile because the cloth is cheaper and the embroidery faster. Both are legitimate; only one of them behaves like a shadow.

The six stitches, in the order they meet the cloth

A piece is not one stitch. The design is separated into areas and each area is given a stitch that suits its size and its job. A karigar may work six stitches on one kurta, in an order that has not changed much in a hundred years.

Bakhiya
The shadow work, and the hardest. The outline is worked from the back of the cloth in a herringbone so that the thread shows through as a faint line on the front. It is slow, it cannot be corrected, and it is what people mean when they say the embroidery looks lit from inside.
Tepchi
A running stitch used for long lines and vine stems. Simple, and the place where a careless hand shows first: the stitches must be the same length on both sides of the cloth.
Hool
A tiny buttonhole stitch that binds an edge — the neckline, the hem, the inside of a jaali hole. It is what keeps cutwork from fraying in the first wash.
Murri
A knotted stitch, a French knot worked with the needle coming up through the loop. Used for flower centres and for filling small shapes. A densely murri'd area looks frosted, and weighs almost nothing.
Phanda
A slightly larger knot, worked like a bead, used for the centres of big flowers and for the dots at the end of a vine. Where murri is frost, phanda is seed.
Jaali
Cutwork. Threads are drawn out of the cloth in a square, the hole is bound with hool, and the remaining threads are worked into a lattice. It is the only stitch that changes the cloth rather than sitting on it, and the only one that cannot be undone.

How a piece is made

1. The drawing and the block

The motif is drawn on paper, then carved in reverse into a block of sheesham wood. A buti needs one small block; a border needs a long one, sometimes six inches, carved so the repeat lines up at the join. Blocks are cut in the Chowk by a family we have used since 2012.

2. The print

The block is dipped in a washable dye — usually a pale blue or a pink — and printed onto the cloth in the pattern the embroidery will follow. The print is a map, not a decoration. It disappears in the first wash after the embroidery is finished.

3. The frame

The cloth is stretched on a wooden frame, or held over the index finger for small pieces. Georgette is framed with almost no tension; mulmul needs more. A badly framed piece will pucker at every outline, and no amount of ironing will take it out.

4. The embroidery

Worked from the printed guide, area by area, with the stitches above. A buti of four centimetres takes about four hours. A full jaali border on a dupatta takes three hundred. The work is done in daylight; a lamp is not the same and the stitches come out uneven.

5. The wash

The finished piece is washed in cold water with a mild soap to take out the block print, then dried in shade and pressed while slightly damp. This is the step that makes the embroidery sit into the cloth instead of on top of it. It is also the step that ruins a piece if the water is hard or the soap is wrong.

The studio

Fourteen karigars work with me: eleven at home, three at the long table in the front room in Aminabad. Most have been with the studio for more than five years. They are paid by the piece at a rate agreed before the work begins, and the rate is written on the back of the design sheet so there is no argument at the end.

I design, cut the blocks, buy the cloth and sell. I do not embroider for money any more; my hands are too slow and the karigars are better. The label carries my name and the name of the person who worked the piece.

What it costs

  • A buti on a kurta front: ₹900–₹1,400 in labour.
  • A full kurta: ₹12,000–₹22,000, six to twelve weeks.
  • A jaali dupatta: ₹45,000–₹70,000, twelve to fourteen weeks.
  • A sari: ₹48,000–₹90,000, ten to sixteen weeks.

The cloth is extra and depends on what you choose. There is no discount for a rush; there is only a longer wait or a smaller piece.

Care, plainly

Hand wash in cold water with a gentle soap, never wring, dry flat in shade, and iron on the reverse while slightly damp. Do not bleach, do not tumble dry, and do not send a jaali piece to a dry cleaner who has not seen cutwork before — the holes are bound thread, not cloth, and a harsh solvent will soften the binding.

Chikan softens with wear. A kurta that has been washed ten times is better than a new one. If a thread lifts, do not pull it; bring the piece back and we will secure it. Most repairs are five minutes and a cup of chai.
