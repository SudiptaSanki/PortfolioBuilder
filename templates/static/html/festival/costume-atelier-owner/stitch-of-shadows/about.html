01 · the cutter

Twenty-eight years of bodies that move.

I trained in a Philadelphia costume shop, spent nine years on tour, and opened this workroom in 2013. The work is period cutting, modern wiggle room, and a great deal of hand-sewing.

A shop on Filbert Street

I learned to sew from my grandmother, who made wedding dresses in a front room in South Philly and never once used a machine for a hem. My first job, in 1998, was sweeping up in a costume shop on Filbert Street that built for the opera and for a touring children's theatre. I was supposed to be there for a summer. I stayed six years, and by the end I was cutting principal men's coats.

The shop's head cutter was a woman named Ruthie who had come off the road from a Broadway tour. She taught me the thing this workroom is built on: a costume is not a garment with a story attached, it is a garment that has to survive a performance. A sleeve that looks beautiful standing still is a sleeve that will split when the actor lifts both arms in the second act. We fit in the rehearsal room, not the fitting room, from the first week.

Nine years of tours

From 2006 to 2015 I worked as wardrobe on touring productions — a musical with forty-two wigs, an opera that travelled with six crates of period corsetry, a dance company whose quick-change existed because a costume could be opened and closed with one hand in a wing. Touring teaches you what a costume is for: it is a machine that has to work in a different city every night, with a dresser you have just met and a laundry that runs on hotel hours.

I opened the atelier on East Passyunk in 2013, while still touring, and went full-time in 2015. The workroom is on the second floor above a shoe repair, with two industrial machines, one overlocker, a cutting table that came out of a shuttered uniform factory, and a mirror that is long enough to see a hem from the floor.

You cannot fit a character. You can only fit a person who is playing one.

What I make

  • Theatre. Principal and ensemble costumes for regional houses, built to a designer's sketches and a production's schedule.
  • Dance. Tutus, rehearsal skirts, men's jackets that can take a lift. Measured in fifth position, never standing still.
  • Film and television. Period work with multiples, because a scene may take three days and the costume cannot.
  • Private commissions. One or two a year: a wedding suit, a drag costume, a reproduction of a family photograph.
  • Reproductions. Museum mannequins and study garments, cut from a pattern taken off an original.

The rules of the workroom

Measurements are taken over the underwear the performer will wear, and kept on a card that is destroyed when the production closes. Nothing is cut until the toile is approved. Every costume is fitted on the person at least twice, and the last fitting happens under the lights, in the shoes, with the wig or hat. If a seam fails in a rehearsal, that is a design change, not a repair. And no one is ever blamed for a body; the pattern is what is wrong.
