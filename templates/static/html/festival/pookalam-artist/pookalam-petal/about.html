The artist · Thrissur

My grandmother counted flowers out loud.

Janaki Amma laid pookalams for the Vadakkunnathan temple before I could hold a flower. She never drew a plan; she walked the circle and the circle came out right. I learnt it from standing beside her with a basket.

I was born in Thrissur in 1978, in a house on the road to Punkunnam with a verandah wide enough to hold a three-metre pookalam and a mother who did not mind the mess. My grandmother lived four doors away. Every Chingam she would arrive before dawn with baskets of thumba, sit on the floor with her legs folded to one side, and begin at the centre.

She counted as she placed. Not the flowers, exactly, but the rhythm of them: eight, sixteen, twenty-four, always even, always symmetric. If a ring came out short she would take flowers out of it rather than extend it, because, she said, an uneven ring shows from the gate.

I did my first full pookalam at fourteen, at a neighbour's grahapravesham, while she watched from a stool and said nothing for two hours. It was lopsided and the marigold was too sparse, and when I finished she said, “Now you know what four metres feels like.” I have not stopped since.

I studied painting at the College of Fine Arts here, which taught me colour and taught me nothing about flowers. Flowers wilt on a different clock. You have from four in the morning until the sun clears the roof, and the design has to be simple enough to lay at speed and complicated enough to be worth the walk down the lane to see it.

The studio is a shed behind my house with a cold room, twelve baskets and a table where the flowers are sorted by size. Six women work with me through Onam; the rest of the year it is me and two of them. We do not use a projector, a template or a printed grid. We use thread, a stick, and the length of an arm.

Ring 02 · Years

A life in circles

Four decades of Chingam, and a few other months that paid the bills.

  • 78

    Born in Thrissur

    A house on the Punkunnam road, with a verandah three metres across. My mother still lives in it.

  • 92

    First pookalam, with Amma

    Fourteen years old. Thumba only, because that was what the garden had. She let me place every flower and corrected nothing until it was finished.

  • 96

    First paid commission

    ₹600 for a two-metre circle at a shop opening on MG Road. The owner complained that the marigold was not orange enough. It was the marigold the market had.

  • 04

    The shed

    A cold room, a sorting table, twelve baskets. Built with the money from three wedding seasons and a loan from my brother.

  • 11

    The ten-metre temple pookalam

    Vadakkunnathan, Uthradam night. Nine hours, four of us, eleven thousand thumba. I did not sleep and I have never been happier with a floor.

  • 18

    Six women, one season

    The team that picks, sorts and lays with me from Atham to Thiruvonam. Most of them have been with me longer than my car.

  • 23

    Teaching

    Saturday workshops at the studio: six people, one three-metre circle, all of you on your knees by nine. Nobody leaves without a finished ring.

  • Now

    Onam 2027

    Twenty-two commissions booked for the ten days. Four slots left, all of them for Uthradam morning, which is the hardest one to fill.

Ring 03 · The craft

Three things I was taught and did not improve on

I.

The ground first

Clean the floor, wet it, smear cow dung paste thin and let it darken. On tile or marble, use a cotton sheet soaked and wrung. Flowers laid on dry cement are dead by seven.

II.

Count in even numbers

Every ring divides by four. Whatever number you have picked, take flowers out until it does, because a ring that is short by three shows from the gate and cannot be hidden.

III.

Work outward, never back

Start at the centre and do not return to a finished ring. Going back means kneeling on flowers, and a pookalam with a knee print in it is a pookalam that was laid by somebody in a hurry.
