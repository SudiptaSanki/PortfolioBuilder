About

The stage was a courtyard first

I was nine when a jatra troupe set up in our threshing yard and asked the village for four planks and a table. I carried water to the musicians all evening to stay close to the harmonium. I have not really left that yard since.

Training

My first guru was Bimal Krishna Das, a sutradhar of the old school who had played female roles before women joined the troupes in the eighties. He taught me to speak from the back of the throat so the last row of a field can hear the consonants, and to keep the hands still while the voice carries.

At seventeen I joined the Alpona Night company under Nirmal Adhikari, who ran it for thirty-one years. He was impatient with imitation. “Do not copy my villain,” he said. “Copy the moment you first hated someone, and then put it in the fifth act.” Everything I know about playing a scene rather than performing one comes from that instruction.

Music came later, from Ashok Saha, who played harmonium for the company and insisted that every actor learn the ragas they would stand inside. I can still not sing a full bandish. But I know where the song will turn, and I can place a line of dialogue on either side of that turn.

Method

A jatra is not rehearsed to completion before it meets an audience. Nine days give us the shape: the entrances, the songs, the two places where the comic subplot must release the tension of the main story. The rest is found in performance, night after night, in front of people who have seen the pala before and will tell you at the tea stall exactly which line you rushed.

I work from a written script, but a third of what an audience hears is not on it. The sutradhar's opening address names the village, the occasion and often a family in the front row. That address is never written down. It is the oldest part of the form and the part most easily lost when a troupe starts playing to cameras instead of courtyards.

A folk form stays alive by being slightly different every night. The moment it is identical, it belongs to a museum.

The company

Alpona Night is fourteen people: five actors, four musicians, two backstage hands, a backdrop painter, a driver and me. We travel in a hired truck with the flats stacked flat and the lamps in straw. Between October and March we play between forty and sixty nights; from April to September we rehearse, repair and teach.

Four of the younger actors come from the same two villages I grew up between. Two of them had never seen a jatra before they auditioned at the school ground. That is the ordinary arithmetic of the form now, and it is why I spend the off-season teaching rather than touring.

What I am asked most

Can a jatra be shortened? Yes. A festival slot of ninety minutes is common, and we cut to the main story with two songs. It changes the rhythm, and I will tell you honestly when a pala does not survive the cut.

Do you play outside Bengal? Assam, Tripura and the Bengali-speaking belts of Jharkhand, most winters. Travel and accommodation are part of the fee discussion.

Twenty-eight seasons

  1. 1998

    First paid night

    Chaitanya pala in Dhubulia. I played a palace guard with four lines and was paid two hundred rupees and a meal.

  2. 2004

    First lead

    Behula in Behula's lament, opposite a snake charmer's son who could actually handle a cobra. The snake was replaced by rope after the second night.

  3. 2009

    Sutradhar

    Took over the opening address from Nirmal Adhikari. Learned to read a crowd in the first ninety seconds, from whether they stand or sit.

  4. 2014

    Assam circuit

    First invited tour to Nagaon and Jorhat, three nights each. The audience knew the palas better than we did and called for specific songs.

  5. 2019

    Teaching

    Started a nine-day winter workshop for school students in Santipur. Four of the current company came through it.

  6. 2024

    Company lead

    Took charge of Alpona Night after Nirmal's retirement, with the backdrop painter and the harmonium player from the old company still in place.
