Method

Measured first, then read

I am an architect who reads the vastu texts, not a priest who reads plans. The order matters: if the sun and the drains disagree with the mandala, the house will tell you which one is right.

An architect who reads the old texts

I studied architecture in Bengaluru, worked six years in a practice that did schools and small hospitals, and spent most evenings reading the vastu texts my grandmother kept in a steel trunk. The two did not agree often. The texts put the puja room in the north-east; the practice put the store there because it was the coolest corner and the store needed no window.

What I do now is a reading, not a ceremony. Someone sends me a plan, usually a flat they have bought or a house they are about to build. I mark the sun path for their latitude, the fall of the ground, the walls that carry, and then the nine zones. The report is four pages and ends with three changes ranked by what they cost.

Most of the time the plan is not as wrong as the family fears. The kitchen is in the wrong corner but the corner is inside the same room, and moving the plate costs four thousand rupees. Occasionally the plan is genuinely bad, and then the honest answer is that no reading will fix it and they should think about a different flat.

What the texts actually are

The vastu purusha mandala is a square divided into a grid — usually 64 or 81 squares — with a deity assigned to each division and the centre left open for Brahma. The eight directions carry the eight guardians: Kubera in the north, Yama in the south, Indra in the east, Varuna in the west, and the four corners between them.

It is a diagram for placing a building on a plot, and it was written for a village and a climate, not for a ninth-floor flat with a shared wall and a drainage stack. That is why I read it as a set of defaults with reasons attached, not as a code. Where two traditions disagree — and on the puja room, the kitchen and the entry they often do — I say which one I am following and why.

The reasons are usually good. The south-west is heavy and hot; the north-east is light and cool; the south-east takes the morning sun; the north-west dries out. A plan that follows those facts will also, more often than not, follow the mandala.

What vastu cannot do

  • It cannot promise health, money or a marriage. I will not tell you a corner will change your fortune, and if a consultant does, ask them what happens to the family that did everything and still had a bad year.
  • It cannot replace a structural engineer. A wall that carries a floor above it is not moved because a text prefers the stair there.
  • It cannot fix a plan that has no light or no cross-ventilation. Those are architecture, and no placement of a mirror will make a dark room bright.
  • It cannot be checked after the fact. If you have lived in a house for eleven years and nothing has gone wrong, the house does not need correcting because a website said the kitchen is wrong.
  • It is not medical advice. If someone in the house is ill, the answer is a doctor. I will not read a plan to explain an illness.

What I do sell

A reading costs ₹8,000 for a flat and ₹14,000 for a house or a site, including one site visit within Bengaluru and a four-page report. A second visit after the changes costs nothing. If the plan is on paper only, send the PDF and the site address and I will mark it up without a visit.

I take about ten readings a month, and I say no when the plan is already built and the family is only looking for reassurance. There are cheaper ways to be reassured.
