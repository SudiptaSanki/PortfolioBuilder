The chef · Kenta Mizuno

Twelve years behind
someone else's counter.

I opened this room in 2016, at thirty-four, after twelve years of standing one step behind a chef who did not explain very much. What I know came from watching, from being corrected, and from a great deal of rice that went into the bin.

I grew up in Yaizu, a fishing port in Shizuoka, where my father worked a boat and my mother sold the catch. I did not intend to cook. At nineteen I went to Tokyo to study business and lasted a term, then took a job washing dishes at a sushi counter in Tsukiji because it came with a room above the shop.

The chef there was sixty-one and had trained under a man who trained under a man from the Edo period's last generation of street stalls. He let me touch rice after two years and fish after four. The first thing he taught me was how to look at a fish's eye and its belly, and how to say no to a supplier without losing him.

The rice took the longest. For eight years I cooked it every morning and he rejected it most mornings — too wet, too cold, too sour, or once, memorably, "correct but not honest". I learned the recipes in a month and the judgement in a decade, which is roughly the ratio the work demands.

In 2016 I took a lease on a ground-floor room in Nihonbashi with a counter that had come out of a demolished kaiseki restaurant in Kanda. We sanded it, found hinoki under forty years of lacquer, and built ten stools around it. There is no kitchen behind the counter — only a rice pot, a neta case, a grill and a sink.

The menu changes with the tide, not the calendar. I buy at Toyosu four mornings a week and from two brokers I have known since Tsukiji. If the fish is not good enough, I close the counter rather than serve it; that has happened twice in nine years, and both times the guests were telephoned before they left home.

Sushi is not a cuisine of addition. Everything you take away — the water in the fish, the heat in the rice, the time between the cut and the hand — is a decision. Kenta Mizuno, 2024

The counter is sanded every Sunday. The knife is sharpened on a whetstone on the first Monday of the month, which is a habit rather than a necessity.

  5. 2021

How a piece is made

Four decisions, ten seconds

  • 01

    The cut. Against the grain, at an angle that depends on the fish's fat and its age. A tuna belly cut straight will sit heavy on the tongue.

  • 02

    The nikiri. A brush of reduced soy, mirin and sake, made each morning. It is not a sauce; it is a way of closing the surface.

  • 03

    The shari. Body temperature, packed loose enough to fall apart as you lift it. A tight ball of rice is a ball of rice.

  • 04

    The gap. Wasabi between the fish and the rice, never on top. The distance from my hand to yours is about forty centimetres and ten seconds.

A last thing

If you are coming for the first time, tell me

There is no wrong way to eat at the counter, but there is a pace to it. If you have never had Edomae sushi, say so when you sit down and I will tell you what is coming and how to eat it. Nobody has ever been laughed at in this room for asking.
