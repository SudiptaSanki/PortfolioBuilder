Katha Mic/Studio method

A story is not a resource you extract.

Every episode begins with a conversation that is not recorded, about whether this telling should exist at all. What follows is the working method behind those conversations, the gear, and the people who translate.

Consent before microphone

The first visit is tea and no recorder. I explain what the podcast is, who listens, how long the episode will run and what will happen to the tape. The teller decides the version we record. If they want a section off the record, it stays off, and the episode is built around the silence rather than filled with it.

Consent is written in the language the teller reads most comfortably, Assamese, Bengali or English. It covers the recording, the transcript, the translation, the website and the archive. It can be withdrawn by phone, and when it is, the episode comes down within a week. Two episodes from season two are no longer available for that reason, and I have not re-released them.

“You can tell the story. But do not say that it happened to my family, because it did not happen to me.”

A teller in Nalbari, before recording, 2024

Translation with a name on it

I do not translate alone. Every episode that includes Assamese or Bengali speech is translated with a collaborator who is credited in the audio and on the episode page, and paid at the same day rate I pay myself. Where a word has no clean English equivalent, we leave it standing and explain it in a short note. The word khati is a good example: it can mean a cut, a piece of land, or a kind of presence on the hill, and none of those is the whole of it.

Transliteration follows the teller's preference, not a house style. Names of deities appear as the community writes them, which means the same figure can be spelt two ways in two episodes. I would rather be inconsistent than correct someone's mother tongue at them.

The edit is where stories get flattened

Early episodes tried to reconcile variants into one confident narrative. That was a mistake. A tale told in Nalbari and the same tale told in Barpeta differ in who survives and what the bird says, and the difference is usually the most interesting thing in the recording. The edit now keeps the seams visible: two tellers, two endings, one episode.

Music is sparse and licensed, or played by someone I can name. There is no library drone under the serious parts. Silence is allowed to sit for four or five seconds, which is uncomfortable in headphones and correct for the material.

What the archive holds

Raw tape is kept on two drives in different rooms, with checksums and a paper index. Tellers can ask for their own recording at any time, and receive it as a WAV, not a stream. The unedited archive is not public and will not be sold. When I stop making the podcast, the tapes go back to the families who made them, or to a university archive with their written permission, whichever they choose.

This is a small operation: one host, one editor, a rotating group of translators and a borrowed second microphone. It is not a museum. But it tries to behave like a guest.

Gear, since season three

Two Sennheiser MKH 416 microphones, a Sound Devices MixPre-6 II, Rycote windshields, and a decade-old pair of Sony MDR-7506 headphones that have outlived three laptops.

Room tone is recorded for forty seconds at every location. Backups are written twice before the kit is packed.
