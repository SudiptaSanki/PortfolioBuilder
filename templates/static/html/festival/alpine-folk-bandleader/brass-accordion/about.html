Home/The band

The band is older than the republic.

The Musikkapelle Falkenbach was founded in 1898, when the village belonged to the Austro-Hungarian Empire and the post came twice a week. It has played through two republics, one annexation, a war, and a long stretch in the 1970s when the average age of the band was sixty-three.

E

What a Kapellmeister does

Not conduct, mostly. The job is choosing the programme, writing the arrangements, keeping forty volunteers in the same key, and knowing which of them will not speak to which after the second beer.

Sepp Hauser took over the band in 2007, after eleven years playing flügelhorn in the front row and two years studying at the conservatory in Innsbruck. He came back with a diploma, a baton he rarely uses, and a conviction that the band's problem was not talent but repertoire.

The village band plays for the village. That means a programme that has to work outdoors, at a procession, with the wind coming down the valley and the tuba player standing on gravel. It means marches that can be played while walking, waltzes that do not outstay their welcome, and one piece per year that is slightly too difficult, so the band has something to rehearse toward.

The Wednesday rehearsal

Tuning at 19:30, which in practice means 19:40. The first forty minutes are sectionals: horns in the side room, clarinets in the kitchen, tubas wherever they can sit. Then the full band for an hour, then the last twenty minutes on the piece that is not working, which is usually the new one.

Sepp rehearses with a piano and his voice, not with a baton. He sings the tuba line badly and expects the tubas to improve on it. When a phrase is not together he stops, counts it in slower, and does it again; he does not explain the phrase in words unless the third attempt also fails.

Transposition, briefly

Half the band reads a different note from the one that sounds. Flügelhorn and clarinet are in B♭: when they play a written C, the room hears a B♭. Tenor horn is in E♭, which means written C sounds E♭. Trombone, tuba and accordion read concert pitch. Sepp writes the score in concert pitch and transposes the parts himself, because a wrong clef in the horn part has cost the band more rehearsal time than any difficult passage.

The youth wing

Founded in 2012, the Jugendkapelle has twenty-two players between nine and eighteen. They rehearse on Fridays, play two engagements a year, and are the reason the band's average age has fallen from sixty-three to forty-one. The rule is that a young player joins the main band on the second stand, beside someone old enough to correct them without making a thing of it.

How the sound is built

Three notes, spread across four octaves, with the middle voice deciding everything. The same arithmetic sits under every arrangement in the book.

4

Octaves covered

From the tuba's low F to the flügelhorn's top line. The band's usable range is wider than most village halls need.

3

Notes per chord

Root, third and fifth. Everything else is doubling, and doubling is a decision about volume, not harmony.

2

B♭ transpositions

Flügelhorn and clarinet sound a major second below their written pitch. The part is written up, the ear hears down.

1

Voice that matters

The middle. Move the third up an octave and the chord opens; drop it and the chord closes into itself.

The founding year

The original statutes survive in the village archive, in ink, with a list of sixteen founders and their instruments.

"You can hear a bad voicing from the back of the meadow before you can name what is wrong. It sounds like the band is standing too far apart."

Sepp Hauser

The archive

Four binders, two of them handwritten, one held together with electrical tape. The oldest part is a march from 1902, copied by the founding Kapellmeister and still played at the patronal feast.

Sepp has been digitising the collection since 2019, at a rate of about one binder per winter. The scanner lives in the parish hall and is borrowed more often for wedding photographs than for music.

Come to a Wednesday

Rehearsals are open to listeners, and the band is always short of a tenor horn. The door at the side of the parish hall is unlocked from seven.
