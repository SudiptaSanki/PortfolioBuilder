Three notes, forty ways to play them.

Concert programmes, the pieces that keep coming back, and the working repertoire with the keys and tempos the band actually uses. Some of this is written down; most of it is remembered.

G

Programmes

Each engagement gets its own order, built around the length of the walk, the size of the room and whether there is a blessing in the middle.

2026

Advent concert, Falkenbach

Four chorales, two arrangements for band and choir, and a flügelhorn solo in the second half. The programme is deliberately short: the church is cold and the audience is older than the band.

Sepp's note in the margin: "No marches. Nobody wants a march in Advent, whatever the drummer says."

2026

Almabtrieb, the upper meadow

The cattle come down at two, the band plays from a flatbed trailer, and the audience arrives on foot from three directions. The set is all marches and polkas because the wind carries the middle register away and anything subtle is wasted.

One new piece this year: a polka in A♭ written for the drive, with the clarinet on the melody and the accordions out until the trio.

2025

Patronal feast, village square

Procession at ten, concert at eleven, beer at twelve. The programme moves from a chorale at the church door to the band's own march in the square, which is the closest thing the village has to a national anthem.

The tenor horns were one player short, so the third horn part was covered by a clarinet reading down an octave. Nobody noticed, which is the highest compliment a dep can get.

2025

Wedding, the neighbour's daughter

A short programme: the bride's family requested two waltzes and a march, the groom's family requested nothing and got a polka anyway. The band played from the church steps, then again at the marquee at nine.

The flügelhorn solo was moved to the second set because the bride's grandmother was still on the church steps being photographed.

2024

Recording, parish hall

Twelve pieces recorded over two evenings with four microphones and a borrowed interface. The hall has a three-second tail that nobody expected and everybody now wants to keep.

The record was pressed on CD, 300 copies, sold at the feast and given to the founding families. It is not on any streaming service, by unanimous vote of the band committee.

I

Writing for the band

Sepp arranges about four new pieces a year. The method is the same every time: find the tune, decide where the middle voice sits, then write the parts so that the weakest player in each section can still play them.

1

Find the tune

Usually from a recording, sometimes from a singer in the village who has the words but not the key.

2

Fix the voicing

Close for the church, open for outdoors. The middle voice is chosen before anything is written down.

3

Write the parts

Transposed by hand into B♭ and E♭, with the tuba line written an octave lower than it sounds in the score.

4

Rehearse it badly

The first read-through is always bad. The second is where the arrangement learns what it actually is.

5

Cut a section

Every new piece loses one section after the second rehearsal. Usually the one the arranger liked most.

6

Play it outdoors

If it survives the wind and the gravel, it goes in the book. If not, it goes in the drawer with the others.

Ask for the programme list

Tell us the occasion and the size of the room; a proposed programme comes back with the quote, and usually one piece nobody expected.
