I learned to tie an obi in a room with no heating and a teacher who counted the folds aloud.

Four years in Kyoto, a decade in Portland, and a strong opinion about who should be allowed to touch a wedding kimono.

京都 · 修業 · 十五

Astoria, and a grandmother who did not explain

My grandmother was born in Hood River to a family that had come from Wakayama, and she wore a kimono perhaps four times a year: New Year, a wedding, a funeral, and the Obon dance in the park. I remember the boxes more than the clothes. They lived on the top shelf of a closet that smelled of camphor, and she opened them with a seriousness she did not bring to anything else in the house.

She never taught me to tie anything. She would let me hold the obi while she turned, and then she would say “not like that” and do it herself in under a minute. I was eighteen before I understood that this was not a refusal but a kind of patience: she was waiting for me to ask a real question instead of a polite one.

Kyoto, 2008–2013

I went to Japan on a language scholarship with no plan beyond staying a year. I lasted five. The turning point was a dressing class in a cold second-floor room near Shijo, run by a woman in her seventies who had dressed brides for forty years and had no interest in making the skill look easy. She counted the folds aloud. When I got one wrong she made me start again, from the underlayer, without comment.

I stayed as her apprentice for three years and then as an assistant for one more. The work was not glamorous: I steamed, folded, carried boxes, watched. In the fourth year she let me dress a client alone, a woman in her sixties going to a tea gathering, and I tied the obi too high. She corrected it in front of the client without embarrassment for either of us, and afterwards told me that a dressing is not a performance; it is a service that has to be right when the client stands up.

The studio, and the decision to teach

I came back in 2013 because my mother was ill and because Portland is a good place to be poor in. For three years I dressed clients out of a spare room in my apartment, which is not legal and was not comfortable. In 2016 I took the lease on Division Street: one room, a mirror, a steamer, and a rack that I built badly and have never replaced.

The decision that shaped the business was to teach. Most kimono professionals in this country keep the tying to themselves; it is how they make a living. But a client who cannot dress herself cannot wear the kimono she owns, and a wardrobe that stays in a box is not a wardrobe. So I teach the four-session course, and I teach it to people who will never hire me again. Some of them become the people I call when a wedding needs a second dresser.

What I will not do

I will not dress anyone as a geisha for a party. I will not put a synthetic kimono on a bride and call it formal. I will not take a wedding booking I cannot staff properly, and I will not pretend that a dressing takes twenty minutes when it takes ninety.

I will also tell you when the kimono you inherited does not fit, and what can be done about it. That conversation is usually the most useful hour in the whole process, and it is free.

“The kimono does not make the person elegant. The person stands up, and the kimono has to be ready for that.”
