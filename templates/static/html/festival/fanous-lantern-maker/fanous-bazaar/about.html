A fanous is a box for light, and a box is only as good as its corners. We cut the brass with hand shears, fold it over a wooden jig, solder the seams with lead-free wire, and set the glass last, because glass set early will crack when the frame moves.

My grandfather's shop, 1962

Hassan Farag opened the shop with one bench and a set of shears bought from a tinsmith in Bulaq. He made fanous in the month before Ramadan and repaired pots for the rest of the year. The shears are still on the wall, with an edge that no longer cuts anything.

My father added the soldering iron and the second bench. I added the electric wiring, which was not a small argument: a fanous with a bulb is still a fanous, as long as the bulb is warm and can be dimmed to the colour of a candle.

“The glass does not need to be perfect. It needs to be the same thickness in every pane, or the light will look tired before the month is over.”

What the month demands

Ramadan is the year, for us. From the middle of Sha'ban the shop works until midnight; the bodies are cut in the morning, soldered in the afternoon, glazed at night when the workshop is cooler. The last ten days we sell from a stall near the Bab al-Futuh gate, three deep in customers, and the price of brass rises by the week.

Outside the month we make commissioned bodies for hotels and mosques, and repair work. The Coptic quarter orders small red lanterns in December, and in the spring a few film companies come for props that must look older than they are.

The workshop's rules

No lead solder, though the old men say it flows better. No glass thinner than 2.5 mm. No lantern leaves without being lit for an hour in the dark room behind the shop, because a body can look sound in daylight and still leak light at every seam.

And no two lanterns exactly alike, even in a set of forty: the glass cutter follows the pattern, but the hand that cuts it does not repeat itself.

Four pairs of hands

  1. 01

    Cut

    The pattern is traced onto 0.4 mm brass sheet, then cut with the shears. A medium body takes nine pieces: six sides, a dome, a base and a door frame.

  2. 02

    Fold

    Every edge is folded over a wooden jig so the seam becomes a corner rather than a cut. The folds decide whether the lantern stands straight on a sill.

  3. 03

    Solder

    Lead-free wire with a small flame. The seams are wiped while warm, then brushed. A body that has been soldered too hot will show a grey bloom under the finish.

  4. 04

    Set the glass

    Panes are cut two millimetres over size and bedded in a thin bead of high-temperature sealant. The red and amber are alternated by tier, so the light reads warm from far away.
