Bywater, New Orleans

I photograph the part the broadcast misses

Tessa Boudreaux, photographer. Fifteen carnival seasons, a darkroom in the Bywater, and a stubborn preference for the corner rather than the reviewing stand.

I moved to New Orleans in 2009 for a newspaper job that lasted eleven months. The job did not, but the city did. My first Mardi Gras I shot on a borrowed 50 mm and a roll of pushed Tri-X, and I came home with two usable frames: a man carrying a flame on a pole, and a woman on a ladder with her shoes off. I have been chasing those two pictures ever since.

The parade is not one event. It is a hundred small events moving at four miles an hour. A float turns a corner and the light changes. A brass band stops to play for a porch. Somebody’s grandmother is lifted onto a ladder. If you stand in the reviewing stand you get the official version; if you walk the route you get the one people remember.

Night light in this city is orange, and it falls off a face in about three metres.

Most of the technical work is deciding what to give up. At f/1.4 and ISO 6400 you can hold a face at 1/60, but the beads will smear; at 1/250 the beads freeze and the face goes under. I usually choose the smear, because the throw is the point. That is why the meter on the front page reports both the exposure and the travel: there is no setting that gives you everything.

I print in the darkroom on fibre paper. It is slower and more expensive than inkjet, and it is the only way I have found to make a photograph that looks like it was made at night rather than corrected into daylight. The prints in the archive are editioned at twelve.

How I work with people

  • A Black Masking Indian suit is a year of beadwork and a vow. I ask before every frame, and I do not photograph a masker who is resting or eating.
  • No children without a parent’s yes, even in a crowd, even in a wide frame.
  • I do not stand in the bead line between a float and the crowd. It ruins the frame and somebody’s evening.
  • Prints go back to the people in them. If you have been photographed by me, write and I will find the negative.
  • No photograph of a costume is sold as stock without the maker’s name in the caption.
