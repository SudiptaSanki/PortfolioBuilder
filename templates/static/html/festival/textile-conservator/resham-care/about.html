Practice file · 2007–2026Joy Bhatt

Nineteen years of looking closely at other people's cloth

I am a textile conservator, not a restorer. The difference is a word in a code of ethics and about forty decisions a day: what to leave, what to hold, and what to write down so the next person does not undo it.

A conservator's training is mostly handwriting

I studied painting at the Faculty of Fine Arts in Baroda, then spent two years in a museum textile store because I kept asking how the objects were stored and nobody would answer. That turned into an apprenticeship in a conservation lab in Ahmedabad, then a diploma in textile conservation in Delhi, then nineteen years of my own bench.

The first thing I learned was to write. A treatment that is not documented is a rumour. Every object here has a file: photographs before, during and after, the tests, the materials with batch numbers, the instructions for the next person. Families who inherit a sari also inherit the file, and increasingly the file is what they call me about.

What minimal intervention means in practice

It does not mean doing nothing. It means every intervention has to earn its place. A crease that does not threaten the weave stays creased. A darning from 1962 that is holding a tear stays in, because removing it means making the tear new again. A yellowed ground stays yellowed, because whitening it takes the size out of the silk and shortens its life.

I work to the AIC code of ethics: all treatments reversible where possible, materials documented, and the object's evidence — including its stains — treated as part of the object.

What I will not do

  • Bleach a textile white because a buyer prefers it that way.
  • Trim loose warp ends so that a photograph looks tidier.
  • Iron over metal thread, or dry-clean a zari sari in a commercial plant.
  • Re-weave a loss so closely that a later scholar cannot tell it from the original.
  • Give a written value. That is an appraiser's work, and I am not one.

People sometimes leave when I say these things. The ones who stay tend to come back with the next object in the family.

The long file

Nineteen years, in order

Teaching, lab work and the occasional object that took a year. Museum contracts are named only where the institution allows it.

  • 2003 – 2005

    Museum textile store, Baroda

    Re-housed 1,800 objects in a collection that had been folded into steel cupboards since 1971. Learned to roll a sari around a padded core without a single sharp fold.

  • 2005 – 2007

    Apprenticeship, conservation lab, Ahmedabad

    Two years of other people's treatments: couching, wet cleaning, humidification, and the slow business of testing a dye before touching it.

  • 2007

    Resham Care opens

    One bench, one north window, a borrowed suction table. The first client was a temple trust with a Pichwai that had been hung in incense smoke for sixty years.

  • 2012 – 2016

    Zari survey across Gujarat

    A four-year survey of corroded metal thread in nineteenth-century brocades, funded by a small research grant. The finding that changed my practice: corrosion advances fastest where the object is folded, because the fold traps moisture.

  • 2019

    Suction table built

    Designed with a local fabricator. A quiet table with a controllable plenum, which made localised cleaning possible on painted cottons that could never be washed.

  • 2021 – 2025

    Teaching and exhibition rotas

    Short courses in condition reporting for small museums in Gujarat and Rajasthan, and light-budget plans for three travelling exhibitions. The rota is the part that saves colour.

  • 2026

    The bench now

    Roughly forty objects a year, a waiting list of about three months, and one rule that has not changed: no object is cleaned in the week it arrives.

How an object moves through the lab

A textile is booked in with photographs and a receipt, then quarantined if there is any sign of insect activity. The condition report comes next, usually ten working days, and it is a separate, paid piece of work. About a third of the objects that arrive do not need treatment; the report says so and the owner keeps their money.

If treatment goes ahead, the object is worked on in daylight hours only. No adhesive is used without a test on a spare thread. Nothing is cut, trimmed or replaced without a photograph of what was removed and a sample kept in the file.

At the end there is a care sheet: how to fold it, how often to refold it, what light it can take, what to do if it gets wet. Most of the life of a textile happens after it leaves the lab, and the care sheet is the part of the treatment that keeps working.

For families A wedding sari does not need to look new to be safe. If it is going to be worn again, say so at the start — a wearable treatment is different from an archival one, and the difference is worth planning for.
