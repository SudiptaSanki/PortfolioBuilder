Five objects, and the compromise each one needed

A case file is written before and after the work, and it records the treatment that was refused as carefully as the one that was carried out. Owners' names are withheld where asked; catalogue numbers are real.

RC–2024–118Varanasi, 1911 · private

A wedding Banarasi whose gold had turned black

The sari came in a tin trunk with its original purchase receipt, folded along the same three lines for ninety years. The silk ground was remarkably sound. The pallu was not: the zari had sulphided to a dull black and the silk core beneath had perished, leaving the metal floating in a lattice of broken ends.

Water was out of the question. Corroded zari swells in moisture and takes the silk with it. The metal was consolidated dry under the microscope, a patch at a time, with 3% Paraloid B-72 in acetone applied with a fine brush. The shattered areas were then couched, from the reverse, to a silk crepeline dyed to the ground's shade at the nineteenth attempt.

It hangs now on a padded board with a slanted face, so that no fold falls across a broken weft. The receipt is in an archival sleeve on the back of the board, where the family can read it without touching the cloth.

ObjectKadhwa brocade wedding sari, 5.4 × 1.2 m
FibreMulberry silk, silver-gilt zari, silk core
Condition in38 / 100 — corrosion across 60% of pallu, 14 shattered bands
Condition out82 / 100, stable, not wearable
Bench time11 weeks

Treatment

  1. Document at 1:1; map every shattered band
  2. Surface clean with a soft brush and smoke sponge
  3. Consolidate zari, panel by panel, dry
  4. Dye crepeline to ground; 19 trials
  5. Couch broken weft from the reverse
  6. Mount on padded board, 12° slant
RefusedWet cleaning, and any attempt to polish the zari back to gold. Polishing removes the silver and leaves the core exposed.
RC–2025–007Patan, late 19th c. · private

A patola that could not be washed on either face

Double ikat is dyed before weaving, so the pattern sits in the yarn and meets imperfectly at the edges. This one had been folded in a cupboard and the folds had abraded both faces at the same points — the pattern was worn on the front and the back in mirror image. The reds were the problem: a blotter test on a hidden corner moved colour in under a minute.

No immersion. The object was surface-cleaned with a smoke sponge, the losses laid with dyed silk of a slightly lighter tone so that a later eye can see the repair, and the whole mounted on a board with a 30° slant, because a flat mount let the weft shift under its own weight. The mount is built so the sari can be turned over without being lifted.

ObjectPatola sari, double ikat, 3.2 × 1.1 m
FibreSilk, madder and indigo, natural dyes
Condition in46 / 100 — fold abrasion, fugitive red, 9 losses
Condition out79 / 100, stable on a slanted mount
Bench time9 weeks

Treatment

  1. Blotter test on all five colours
  2. Surface clean, both faces, with smoke sponge
  3. Humidify folds at 48% RH, weight flat
  4. Lay losses with dyed silk, reversible adhesive
  5. Mount at 30°, turnable without lifting
RefusedWet cleaning and any reducing bleach. Both would have moved the red into the cream ground and could not be undone.
RC–2025–031Kalahasti, 1890s · temple trust

A Kalamkari hanging held together by its own outline

The hanging had been in a shrine for sixty years, in incense smoke and lamp soot, and had been washed annually by the temple staff with soap and a soft brush. The iron-gall black used for the outlines had oxidised and eaten the cotton along every drawn line, so the composition was held by a mesh of corroded fibre.

Any immersion would have carried black into the cream ground and dissolved what was left of the line. The hanging was cleaned on the suction table, with localised humidification from a Gore-Tex pack, one square metre at a time. The corroded lines were toned — with dry pigment in a methyl cellulose poultice, not paint — so the drawing reads from two metres without pretending to be original.

ObjectTemple hanging, painted cotton, 2.1 × 1.4 m
FibreCotton, iron-gall black, madder, myrobalan
Condition in31 / 100 — outline corrosion, soot, 40+ splits
Condition out74 / 100, stable, reading legible
Bench time14 weeks

Treatment

  1. Document and map every split
  2. Suction-table clean, dry first, then humidified
  3. Local humidification, 40 minutes per square
  4. Stabilise splits with toned silk crepeline
  5. Tone corroded lines with dry pigment
  6. Mount on a padded, slanted board
RefusedThe annual soap wash the trust asked to continue, and repainting the lost lines. Both were explained in writing, in Telugu and English.
RC–2026–004Jamnagar, 1950s · family

The one that could be washed, and the stain that stayed

A Bandhani odhani in cotton, tied in Jamnagar in the 1950s, worn at three weddings and then stored in a plastic bag against a wall. The cotton was sound, the dyes were direct dyes of the period, and the yellow had run into the white ground in three places. Unlike the patola, this one passed its blotter test.

It was washed twice in soft water at 28 °C with a non-ionic surfactant at pH 7.5, rinsed in deionised water, and dried flat on a mesh. A reducing bleach on the suction table lifted most of the yellow. The owner asked for the remaining tide-line to be removed as well. I refused: another bleach cycle would have taken the ground with it, and a tidier-looking odhani with a weakened ground is a worse object.

ObjectBandhani odhani, tie-dyed, 2.4 × 1.1 m
FibreCotton, direct dyes, metallic tinsel border
Condition in54 / 100 — dye bleed, soil, bag creases
Condition out88 / 100, wearable with care
Bench time5 weeks

Treatment

  1. Blotter test each colour; direct dyes stable
  2. Wet clean, 28 °C, pH 7.5, two rinses
  3. Localised reducing bleach on three areas
  4. Dry flat on mesh, no direct sun
  5. Care sheet: wash by hand, never store in plastic
RefusedA third bleach cycle to remove the last tide-line. The ground was beginning to lose tensile strength; the file records the refusal and the reason.
RC–2025–044Ahmedabad, 2025 · museum

Nineteen brocades, one travelling case, six weeks of light

A museum had accepted a travelling exhibition of nineteenth-century brocades and had forty-five days to make the display safe. The textiles were in good condition; the plan was not. All nineteen were scheduled to hang at 200 lux for eleven weeks, which would have spent more than twice the annual light budget in one season.

The work was mostly arithmetic and carpentry. Fifty lux at the object, UV-filtering glazing, a rotation at week six, and mounts that could be dressed by two people without lifting a textile above shoulder height. Each object got its own lux-hours calculation, and the museum got a one-page rota it could hand to the next curator.

Objects19 zari brocades, 1860–1910, various owners
IssueLight exposure, mount handling, no rotation plan
Result50 lux at object; rotation at week 6; 19 care sheets
Budget used48,000 lux-hours per object over 11 weeks
Bench time6 weeks (mounts and rotas, no treatment)

Work done

  1. Condition check of all 19 on arrival
  2. Lux-hours calculation per object
  3. Specify UV-filtering glazing, 50 lux
  4. Design mounts for two-person dressing
  5. Write the rotation rota and care sheets
RefusedAny treatment of the brocades themselves. Nothing needed it, and a travelling exhibition is the worst place to start.
