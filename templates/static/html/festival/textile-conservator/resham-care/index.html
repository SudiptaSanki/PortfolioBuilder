01 / The bench

Conservation is mostly deciding what not to do

Every treatment removes something — soil, an old repair, a crease, sometimes the evidence of how the object was used. The report states what will be kept, what will be stabilised and what will be left exactly as it is.

The bench faces north, because north light is the only light in Ahmedabad that does not change colour by the hour. On it: a low-power stereo microscope, a suction table, a humidification chamber built from a photo tray and aquarium stones, and a pH meter that gets calibrated more often than the kettle is boiled.

Textiles arrive folded in newspaper, in a trunk, in a plastic bag that has held a wedding certificate since 1981. The first hour is spent photographing all of it, including the newspaper. Provenance lives in the wrapping as often as in the weave.

What follows is a written condition report: fibre identification under magnification and a burn test where there is a loose end to spare, weave structure, dye solubility on each colour, metal thread corrosion, previous repairs, and a map of every loss drawn onto a photograph at 1:1.

02 / What fails

Six failures, six different answers

Two textiles of the same age can need opposite treatments. A painted cotton that has bled cannot be washed; a plain cotton with the same stain must be. These are the six conditions I record most often.

Shattered weft

Age and light break the silk weft while the warp survives, leaving a lattice of floats. The band cannot be sewn; it is couched to a silk crepeline overlay with hair silk dyed to match.

F–01 · silk only

Fugitive dye

Indigo and some madder reds bleed the moment they meet water. A blotter test on a hidden seam decides it: if colour walks, the object is surface-cleaned only, and never immersed.

F–02 · test first

Metal-thread corrosion

Zari is silver wound round silk, and silver sulphide turns black then eats the silk core. Water accelerates it. Corrosion is stabilised dry, under the microscope, with a 3% solution of Paraloid B-72 in acetone.

F–03 · no water

Insect loss

Silverfish eat the sizing first, so a hole appears where the finish was. Anything arriving with frass is sealed and frozen at −20 °C for seven days, twice, before it enters the lab.

F–04 · quarantine

Old repairs

A 1960s darning may be holding a 1900s tear. Stable repairs are documented and left alone; only those staining the ground or pulling the weave are reversed, and then slowly.

F–05 · document

Creasing and set

A sari folded for forty years keeps the fold. humidification at 45–50% RH, then glass weights and time, one crease at a time. Ironing is not used on metal thread.

F–06 · slow

04 / Case files

Four objects, four compromises

Names are withheld where the owner asked. Each file lists the condition on arrival, the treatment chosen, the treatment refused, and why.

RC–2024–118

Wedding Banarasi, 1911, Varanasi

Silk · kadhwa brocade · 5.4 m · inherited, Surat

The ground was sound; the zari had sulphided to black across the pallu and the silk core beneath had gone. Wet cleaning was impossible. The metal was consolidated dry, the shattered areas couched to a dyed crepeline, and the sari mounted on a padded board so no fold fell on a broken thread.

Patan patola, late 19th c.

Silk · double ikat · 3.2 m · private collection, Mumbai

Both faces carried the same pattern and both had fugitive red at the joins. A single drop of water moved colour, so the object was surface-cleaned with a smoke sponge, the losses laid down with dyed silk, and the whole supported on a slanted board at 30°, because a flat mount made the ikat shift.

RC–2025–031

Kalamkari temple hanging, 1890s

Painted cotton · 2.1 × 1.4 m · temple trust, Kalahasti

Iron-gall black had corroded the cotton into a lace at the outlines. Immersion would have carried the black into the cream ground, so the hanging was cleaned by suction table with localised humidification, and the corroded lines were toned — not repainted — with a dry pigment in a methyl cellulose poultice.

RC–2026–004

Bandhani odhani, 1950s

Cotton · tie-dye · 2.4 m · family, Jamnagar

This one could be washed, and was — twice, in soft water at 28 °C, with a non-ionic surfactant and a pH of 7.5. The yellow had run into the white in three places; a reducing bleach on a suction table lifted most of it. The owner asked for the stains to be removed entirely. I refused, and wrote why.

05 / Materials & fees

What a bench costs to run

Materials are listed at cost, marked up nowhere. The fee is time. A report that says nothing should be done still takes a week and is charged as one.

The shelf

  • Silk crepeline, 18 g/m², dyed to a 24-step shade card
  • Hair silk in nine weights, for couching
  • Paraloid B-72, 3% and 5% in acetone
  • Klucel G, 2% in deionised water, for consolidation
  • Synperonic N-free non-ionic surfactant
  • Archetype buffered tissue, pH 8.0, and unbuffered for silk
  • Conservation-grade Coroplast, 4 mm
  • A suction table I built in 2019 and would build again

Nothing on the shelf is reversible by accident. Every adhesive used here can be undone with a solvent I keep in the cupboard and name in the report.
