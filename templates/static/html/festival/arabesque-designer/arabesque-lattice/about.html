The compass is older than the software, and less forgiving.

Nastaran Farhadi has drawn patterns for nineteen years: three of them on a restoration crew at the Jameh Mosque, the rest in a north-facing room with two tables and a wall of plaster samples.

The studio · 05

How the work began

Nastaran grew up in a house with a tiled courtyard, in a city where the buildings are covered in geometry. She studied architecture at the Isfahan University of Art, where the first-year drawing course was still taught with a compass, a set square and a very hard pencil. It was the only course she was good at immediately.

She spent three years after graduation on a restoration crew at the Jameh Mosque, measuring existing tilework and drawing replacement pieces for damaged panels. The work was mostly documentation, which is another word for looking closely. When a panel was too damaged to read, the crew would find the same pattern elsewhere in the building and copy the geometry, not the tile.

The studio opened in 2012, in a room three streets from Naqsh-e Jahan Square. The first commission was a book cover; the second was a courtyard wall; the third was a small plaster panel for a restaurant that has since closed, though the panel is still there.

The five tiles

In 2007, two physicists, Peter Lu and Paul Steinhardt, showed that the girih tiling on the Darb-i Imam shrine in Isfahan encodes a quasi-periodic pattern — a geometry the West did not describe until the 1970s. The tiling on its northeast portal is dated 1453. Nastaran keeps a copy of the paper in a drawer and does not bring it up at dinner.

The system rests on five shapes: a regular decagon, an elongated hexagon, a bowtie, a pentagon and a small hexagon. Each edge is the same length, and each is decorated with a line that meets its neighbours at a fixed angle. Assemble the tiles by their edges and the lines connect themselves into the pattern. It is a construction kit that has been in use for six centuries, and it still surprises the people who use it.

Materials

The drawing is paper, pencil and sometimes gold. The built work is glazed tile, carved plaster or painted wood. The tile is cut by a workshop in the bazaar that has been in the same family since the 1950s; the plaster is carved wet by one plasterer who works with a knife and a steady hand, and who refuses to work from a printout.

For print, the pattern is redrawn as vector linework and printed in one or two colours. Nastaran insists on seeing a proof on the actual paper before a run, because the gold behaves differently on coated stock than on uncoated, and the difference is the whole point.

Teaching

Two afternoons a week, four students at a time, around a table with compasses. The first exercise is a six-fold rosette, drawn from a single circle without a ruler. The second is the same rosette at twice the size, to show that the geometry does not depend on the measurement. Most students are architecture students; a few are calligraphers; one is a retired engineer who has been coming for four years.

Geometry · 06

What the folds decide

The number of divisions in the circle is not a style choice. It decides which grid the pattern can sit on and what shape the gaps between rosettes will take.

8

Eight-fold

Sits on a square grid. The natural choice for brick walls, rectangular rooms and book pages.

10

Ten-fold

The girih proper. Decagons, bowties and elongated hexagons; the geometry of domes and the Darb-i Imam shrine.

12

Twelve-fold

The shamsa, or sun. Twelve points, twelve axes, and a rosette that reads as a circle from a distance.

5

Girih tiles

Decagon, pentagon, bowtie, hexagon, elongated hexagon. Every edge the same length, every edge decorated.

1:5

Drawing scale

The working drawing is one-fifth of full size, enlarged on site with a chalk line and a long tape.

1453

The shrine

The Darb-i Imam tiling that encodes a quasi-periodic pattern, five centuries before the mathematics.

The wall of samples

Every pattern that has been built is on the studio wall as a hand-sized square: tile, plaster, painted wood, paper. Thirty-one squares so far, in four rows. The wall is the archive, and it is also the portfolio, because a visitor can touch it.
