Patterns that had to survive a wall.

Selected commissions from the last four years: a courtyard in Yazd, a dome in Qom, a book cover in Tehran, and a screen that does nothing but make the afternoon interesting.

Courtyard panel, Yazd

An eight-fold pattern for a hotel courtyard, built in glazed tile on a 4.2-metre wall. The panel had to work at two distances: as a field of colour from the courtyard, and as a set of lines you can follow with a finger when standing close.

The palette was fixed by the client — a warm plaster ground with an ink line and one gold accent — which meant the geometry had to carry the variety instead of the colour.

Poetry cover

A ten-fold pattern for a Tehran poetry press, printed in gold on deep lapis board. The cover is small, so the pattern had to lose two levels of detail without losing its identity; the final version uses the outline and the main star only.

The gold was proofed four times. On uncoated board it sits into the paper and looks older, which is what the publisher wanted and what the first three proofs did not do.

2025

Afternoon screen

A carved plaster screen for a house in Isfahan, 2.8 by 1.9 metres, set into a west-facing wall. The pattern is twelve-fold because the screen is seen mostly at an angle, and twelve-fold rosettes keep their shape in perspective better than eight.

The plasterer carved it wet over eleven days. The studio's drawing was enlarged on the wall with a chalk line, then corrected by eye where the wall was not true — about a centimetre and a half out across the width.

Dome interior, Qom

A ten-fold scheme for the interior of a small dome, 9 metres across, executed in tile and mirror work. The pattern had to be drawn on a curved surface, which means every straight line in the drawing is a curve in the dome.

The studio produced the flat drawing and a set of projections for each ring of the dome. The tile cutters worked from the projections, and the joins were adjusted on site, as they have been for six hundred years.

Garden wall

An eight-fold screen wall in brick and turquoise tile for a private garden outside Isfahan. Brick decides the repeat: the pattern had to land on a whole number of bricks in both directions, so the drawing was adjusted by about four per cent.

This is the smallest change the studio has ever made to a pattern, and the one that took the longest to agree on. The mason was right.

Commissions · 09

What a commission involves

The studio takes six to eight commissions a year, so that every drawing gets the time the geometry needs.

1

Surface first

Tile, plaster, brick or paper. The material decides the repeat, the strap width and how much detail survives.

2

The light

A north wall and a west wall need different patterns. A photograph at the hour the space is used is worth more than a plan.

3

First rosette

One rosette at 1:5, drawn by hand, within two weeks. Not a mood board; a geometry that can be built.

4

The repeat

The rosette is placed on a grid and the interstices are found. This is where most of the time goes, and where the pattern is decided.

5

Full-size drawing

Chalked onto the surface, or delivered as a set of projections for a curved one. The mason or plasterer corrects it on site.

6

Making

The studio does not make the tile or carve the plaster. It draws, supervises and signs the plate. The makers are named on it.

One rosette is a good place to start

Send a photograph of the wall and a sentence about the light. The first study is drawn at 1:5 and posted flat, because it should be seen on paper.
