The workshop

Three people, two furnaces,
one hundred and eighteen years

My great-grandfather opened this workshop in 1908, in a lane of workshops that had been blowing glass since the 1880s. The furnaces have changed twice. The order of the work has not changed at all.

01 · The people

Who is at the bench

I am Klara Böhme. I blow the glass and do the silvering, which is the part that smells of chemistry and must not be rushed. I came back to the lane in 2004 after six years in Jena, where I trained as a materials engineer and learned why our glass behaves the way it does.

Dieter Raum has been at the second pipe since 1989 and can judge the wall thickness of a bauble by the sound it makes when it goes back into the furnace. He is retiring in two years and has not yet agreed to train anyone, which is the largest problem this workshop has.

Marta Kessel paints. She is 31, learned from her mother, and does the gold lines and the small freehand patterns that appear in the highest-priced part of the catalogue. Her pace is about sixty pieces an hour, and she stops at forty because the fifth line starts to wander.

There is also a dog called Nuss, who is not allowed in the workshop and comes in anyway.

02 · The day

The furnace decides the timetable

Glass does not wait. The furnace is brought up at four in the morning and is at temperature by half past five, and from then until early afternoon the work is continuous, because letting the furnace idle costs more than the gas and ruins the day's rhythm.

An ordinary working day
04:00Furnace lit, workshop cold, coffee on
05:30Blowing: the day's run of one form, forty to seventy pieces
09:00Break, then silvering the pieces blown the day before
11:00Marta paints; Dieter caps and tests the loops
13:30Furnace down, packing and orders
15:00The shop at the front, if it is November

03 · The floor

Why the work is split into five stages

A single person can make a bauble from gather to cap. It takes about eleven minutes and the failure rate is one in five. Split into five stages, with each person doing the same motion for an hour, it takes ninety seconds and the failure rate is one in forty. This is not a compromise of craft; it is the reason the craft survived into a century where a machine can print a bauble for forty cents.

We keep one exception. The painted pieces in the top of the catalogue are done start to finish by one person, and they cost accordingly. If you buy one of those, the name on the card is the only name involved.

“The machine can make a sphere. It cannot make the moment the silver catches, which is the only thing anyone actually buys.”

04 · Glass, heat, air

What changed, and what did not

We stopped using lead in 2019, ahead of the European restriction, and spent a year learning to blow the replacement. It is stiffer, it cools faster, and it does not take the very deep reds as easily. The ruby in the current catalogue is 4 per cent lighter than the one my father made, which nobody has noticed except us.

We reuse broken glass, and about a third of what we blow comes back to us from customers who would rather it became a bauble than a landfill. We have not found a use for the silvering waste beyond proper disposal, and we say so on the box.

05 · The lane

A street of people doing the same thing

There were eleven workshops on this lane in 1960. There are four now, and one of them is a museum. The Christmas market in Lauscha still runs in December, the glass museum is worth a morning, and the lane smells of hot glass in the cold like nowhere else I have been.

If you come, come on a weekday morning, wear closed shoes, and stand where you are told: the floor around the furnace is not a place to be curious.

One honest warning

Glass baubles break. If a piece arrives cracked, send a photograph and we replace it in the next firing. If it breaks after a season on the tree, that is the material being honest, and we will still sell you a matching one.
