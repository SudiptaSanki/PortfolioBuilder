Kumkum Glow·The dancer

I was seven when the guru corrected my shoulders.

Ipsita Mohanty trained in Bhubaneswar from the age of seven and gave her arangetram in 2011. She performs the classical Odissi repertoire in the lineage of Guru Kelucharan Mohapatra and teaches a small group of students in the same room where she learned.

The first thing my guru taught me was not a step. It was how to stand: feet turned out, knees open, spine long, weight low. I stood in chowk for twenty minutes while the other children learned a mangalacharan. At seven, I thought it was a punishment. At thirty-one, I understand it was the whole dance.

Training, plainly

I began at the Odissi Research Centre in Bhubaneswar in 2001, under Guru Ramakanta Behera, who had trained with Kelucharan Mohapatra. For the first four years there were no performances, only exercises: the basic stances, the walks, the twenty mudras, the neck and eye movements that make abhinaya legible from the tenth row.

My arangetram was in 2011, in a hall with a ceiling fan that clicked through the whole Pallavi. I remember the fan more than the dance. The guru said afterwards that the fan was the best thing that could have happened, because a dancer who can keep time against a clicking fan can keep time against anything.

“Your face is not decoration. It is the second hand of the clock.”

Guru Ramakanta Behera, during an abhinaya class, 2014

The body, and its three bends

Odissi is built on two stances. Chowk is the square, equal stance of the temple friezes, used for the powerful nritta passages. Tribhangi is the three-bend stance: head, torso and hip deflected away from the vertical, which gives the form its serpentine line. Almost every graceful moment in the repertoire passes through tribhangi.

What looks effortless is an argument with gravity held very still. The lower back carries the bend, the knees carry the weight, and the face must not show the argument. When I am tired, the bends shorten first, and the audience can see it even if they cannot name it.

The orchestra is not accompaniment

An Odissi performance is made with four or five musicians: a vocalist, mardala, violin or flute, and sometimes a sitar. The dancer does not follow a recording. The mardala player follows the dancer, and the dancer follows the singer, and the whole thing is held together by the tala. When it works, nobody is accompanying anybody.

I rehearse with the same musicians whenever I can. A new mardala player is a new conversation, and a full evening is not the place to begin it. For outstation performances I travel with at least the mardala and the vocalist, and we find the rest locally if we must.

Teaching, and the room

I teach eleven students on Sunday mornings in the same room where I learned, which is now a small studio with a wooden floor and a photograph of the guru on the wall. Four of them are preparing for their arangetram. I teach them the old order: Mangalacharan first, then Pallavi, then abhinaya, then Moksha. There are no shortcuts through the warm-up.

I also teach a quarterly workshop for adults who have never danced. Most of them arrive convinced they have no rhythm. By the end of the first session they are standing in chowk and counting to four, which is all that is required to begin.

Money, and the floor

Performances are paid at a rate agreed in writing, which includes the musicians, the travel and the rehearsal. I do not perform for exposure. School and college shows pay a reduced fee, and one free performance a year goes to a government school in Khordha.

The rider is short: a wooden floor, a stage at least twenty feet wide, two washes and a special, and a green room with a mirror and a door that closes. Everything else is negotiable.

On stage

Costume: the sambalpuri silk patta and the fan-pleated skirt, stitched in Cuttack. Jewellery: silver filigree from Bhadrak, including the matha tika and the waistband.

Ghungroo are tied by my mother before every performance, which is not a superstition. It is simply the only way they sit right.

The orchestra

Vocal: Sasmita Panda. Mardala: Bibhu Tarai. Violin: Aditya Rath. Flute: Debasish Sahu. All four are named in the programme, and all four are paid before the dancer.

Lineage, said plainly

I dance the repertoire as it came to me through Guru Ramakanta Behera, who trained with Kelucharan Mohapatra. Where I have changed an item for my own body, I say so. The tradition is not a museum, but it is not mine to rewrite either.
