Method · the studio

A flame is a calculation that keeps moving.

I trained as a lighting designer for theatre, which is why I plan installations the way a rig is planned: a plot, a count, a burn time, and a person whose only job is to watch the wind.

I came to diyas from the other end of the problem. In a theatre you spend weeks deciding where the light goes and then a computer holds it there. In a field, the light moves, drinks oil, and goes out when a gust comes through. The first installation I made, in 2015, lost a third of its flames in twenty minutes because I had planned the geometry and not the wind.

Since then the studio has kept a record of every burn: the count, the spacing, the oil, the wick, the wind at the site, and how many flames were still lit at the end. Those records are the real archive; the photographs are a by-product.

The terracotta comes from a family workshop in Kutch that has thrown lamps for four generations. They can make about 400 a day in the dry season and nothing at all in the monsoon, which sets the studio calendar more than any exhibition deadline does.

The oil is ordinary mustard oil from a mill in Mehsana. We have tried other oils; mustard burns steadier, smells like a kitchen, and is the oil people recognise, which matters when the public is invited to relight.

How an installation is made

  • Eight weeks out

    Measure the site

    A walk at the same hour the work will be seen, an anemometer at ankle height for twenty minutes, and a note of where the wind comes from after sunset.

  • Six weeks out

    Order the clay

    The Kutch workshop throws and fires the count plus five per cent. Nothing is glazed; a glazed lamp holds heat and cracks.

  • Two weeks out

    Test burn

    Fifty lamps on the studio roof with the actual oil and wick, timed to the minute. This is where the burn-time figure in the plan comes from.

  • Day before

    Lay the plot

    String lines and a chalk grid. The array is laid unlit, photographed from above, and checked against the drawing before anyone fills a lamp.

  • The evening

    Light from the middle out

    Lighting outward from the centre means the last flames are lit by people who can already see the work. One person watches the wind and has the authority to stop.

The public

The work is not finished until someone kneels down to relight a lamp that has gone out. I leave a jar of oil and a bundle of wicks at the entrance for exactly that. It changes the piece: an installation becomes a small maintenance job shared by strangers.

Safety, plainly

Nothing is lit near a thatched roof, a fuel line, or a crowd that cannot step back. Every site gets a water drum per fifty lamps, a fire watch, and a written plan for putting the whole array out in under four minutes. No exceptions, including for weddings.
