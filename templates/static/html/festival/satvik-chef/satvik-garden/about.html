The chef · Anasuya Rao

I learned to cook in a kitchen where nothing was measured and nothing was wasted.

My grandmother cooked for the temple on festival days, for the family on the other days, and for anyone who arrived at the door in between. I am still cooking her food, with a spreadsheet.

The kitchen in Kuvempunagar

I was born in Mysuru in 1981. The kitchen in our house had a stone grinding stone, a shelf of brass boxes and a window that looked into a small garden. My grandmother, Kamalamma, cooked there every day and on feast days she cooked there from four in the morning. I was given the job of picking curry leaf and grinding coconut before I was tall enough to see over the stove.

She never wrote a recipe down. When I asked how much dal, she said “enough”. When I asked how long, she said “until it smells right”. I learned to cook by standing next to her and being wrong until the smell told me otherwise. The spreadsheet came much later, when I had to cook for four hundred people and “enough” stopped being a safe answer.

A satvik plate is not a plate with the good things removed. It is a plate where the season has been allowed to speak. Anasuya Rao

Why no onion or garlic

In our house the rule came from the temple, not from nutrition. Food offered to the deity was cooked without onion, garlic, mushroom or egg — ingredients considered rajasic, too stimulating for a mind that was meant to be quiet. On ordinary days we ate onion in other people's houses and thought nothing of it.

When I started cooking for festivals, I kept the rule because it was the tradition I had been given. I stayed with it because it made me a better cook. Without onion and garlic, you cannot hide. The asafoetida, the curry leaf, the pepper, the coconut and the ghee all have to do their own work, and the vegetable has to be worth eating on its own.

What I buy, and from whom

Vegetables come from Devaraja Market on Monday and Thursday, from three families I have bought from for years. Ghee comes from a dairy in Mandya that churns on Wednesdays. Jaggery comes from a farm in Hunsur, in blocks that still have the string marks. Rice is sona masuri from a mill near Mysuru; coconut from a grove in Nanjangud.

I do not use a supplier who cannot tell me where a thing was grown. That is not a marketing position; it is how I know what the food will taste like in a month when the rain has been wrong.

How a menu is written

  • Monday

    The market

    Walk the market at six, before the heat. Buy what is good, not what was ordered. Come home, write down what I have.

  • Tuesday

    The menu

    Six parts, one season. The menu goes to the host with a shopping list and a price. Two changes are allowed; the third means we chose the wrong month.

  • Wednesday

    The shopping

    Dry goods, ghee, jaggery, coconut. The vegetables are bought again on Thursday so they are two days old at most.

  • Thursday

    The prep

    Soak, grind, cut. The team of four preps in the afternoon; the kitchen is quiet by seven.

  • Friday

    The cook

    Rice at ten, dal at ten-thirty, vegetables in the last hour. Everything is cooked to be eaten within two hours of the fire.

Then and now

  • 1981

    Born in Mysuru

    Third child, first daughter, in a house with a garden and too many relatives.

  • 1994

    The first festival

    I grind coconut for a temple feast for 300 people and am allowed to temper the mustard at the end.

  • 2003

    Hotel school, Bengaluru

    I learn professional kitchens, knives and timings, and that I do not want to cook continental food.

  • 2008

    Leaving the hotel

    I come home and start cooking for friends' weddings and housewarmings, in their kitchens, with my grandmother's brass.

  • 2014

    The first private thali

    Twelve guests, six dishes, one plate. The format that became Satvik Garden.

  • 2019

    Yadavagiri kitchen

    A small kitchen of my own, with a wood-fired stove for the festival days and a gas range for the rest.

  • 2023

    The Garden Thali

    A cookbook of 48 recipes, written with measurements because my daughter asked for them.

  • 2026

    Four hundred plates

    A wedding in Mysuru, twelve dishes, nine cooks, and not one onion in the building.

Bed 06Rules

Four things I will not do.

01

Cook out of season

If the beans are tired, the menu changes. I would rather serve a good pumpkin in June than a bad bean because a menu said bean.

Season first
02

Hide the ghee

Satvik food is not low-fat food. If a dish has ghee, the menu says so. If a guest avoids dairy, the whole plate is rebuilt, not just one bowl.

Declared always
03

Use onion by another name

No onion powder, no garlic paste, no “natural flavouring”. The rule is the rule, and a guest should not have to interrogate a sauce.

No exceptions
04

Cook more than we can serve

Feasts produce leftovers; we pack them for the volunteers and the host, and compost what cannot be eaten. Nothing is fried twice to look fresh.

Zero waste target

Come to a Saturday kitchen.

Six people, four hours, one thali. We shop at Devaraja Market at nine, cook in Yadavagiri, and eat at a long table at one.
