How I run it

I plan for the minute the crowd stops moving

Every festival has a minute where the crowd is pressed in one place and nothing is happening yet. That is where a festival is won or lost, and it is decided three months earlier on a piece of paper.

I started at a gate, counting

In 2011 I was a volunteer at the Arupathimoovar procession in Mylapore, standing at the north gate of the temple with a clicker that broke by nine in the morning. We had 63 idols, eleven streets and no idea how many people were inside the loop. The police asked for a number and we gave them one from the previous year, which had been a different route.

The next year I brought a paper tally and a watch, and counted every ten minutes. That is still the first tool. A tap counter lies when the crowd is tight and the gate is held open; a person with a sheet and a pencil does not.

I run festival operations, not festival programming. I do not book the artists and I do not design the pandal. I decide how many gates, how many marshals, where the water is, who can stop the inflow, and what happens when the ambulance needs the lane at 19:40.

The sequence

Six things happen in this order, and none of them move

  1. 01

    The site walk at the hour of the event

    A ground at noon tells you nothing. Walk it at 18:30 with the light failing, and note the three narrowest points, where the queue will form, and where the water can go without blocking a lane. Photograph the four corners and the meter box.

  2. 02

    The numbers, before the stage

    Footfall, area, gates. Density at 45% standing, arrival at 28% in the peak hour, gates at 600 an hour a lane. If the density is over four, the plan changes before anyone is asked to pay for a stage.

  3. 03

    The ritual time is fixed first

    The priest gives one time and it does not move. Everything else — the cultural slots, the speeches, the prasadam — is arranged around it, and the procession route is agreed with the temple before the stage is booked.

  4. 04

    The permissions, in one envelope

    Police, traffic, fire, corporation, electricity. One envelope, one covering letter, one set of drawings, and a named person who walks it through each desk. Permission is not paperwork; it is the gate you are allowed to open.

  5. 05

    The rehearsal, with radios

    Two days before, with the marshals on the ground and the radios on the channel they will use. We walk the hold: gate two closes, the crowd is stopped for ninety seconds, the announcement is read. A plan that has not been spoken out loud does not exist on the day.

  6. 06

    The debrief, in writing, within 48 hours

    What broke, at what time, and who fixed it. The committee gets three pages, not a thank-you speech. The next year starts from that document, not from memory.

What I refuse

  • A

    A stage in the only exit lane

    It happens every year, because the stage looks better there. It turns a site with four ways out into a site with one, and no amount of marshalling fixes it.

  • B

    A footfall figure nobody counted

    If the committee says one lakh and the ground holds forty thousand, I plan for forty thousand and write the reason in the brief. Somebody has to be able to say the number out loud at the police station.

  • C

    Marshals borrowed from a contractor at 18:00

    People who have not walked the site cannot hold a gate. If the budget cannot pay for trained marshals, the budget is wrong, not the plan.

  • D

    A festival that ends after the last bus

    If the last ritual is at 23:30, the transport plan is part of the operations, not an afterthought. Ten thousand people walking home on a road with no lights is a crowd problem at midnight too.

“Devika told our committee the ground would take forty thousand and not the lakh we had been announcing for years. It was an uncomfortable meeting. She was right, and the festival was better for it.”

S. Raghunathan, festival chairman, Mylapore, 2024
