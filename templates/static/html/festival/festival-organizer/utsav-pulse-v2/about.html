About the producer

Ninety days of paper.
One night of noise.

A festival looks like a weekend. It is actually a quarter of a year of licences, contracts and site plans, arranged so that for two days nobody has to think about any of it.

I started in 2009 as a volunteer stagehand at college festivals in Pune, carrying cable and learning the only lesson that matters in this trade: the crowd can feel a badly run festival before it can see one. By 2012 I was stage-managing other people's shows for hire. In 2014 I produced my first festival under my own name — a food truck rally in Koregaon Park, 2,100 people, one generator, borrowed fencing, and a rain shower that taught me more than the previous five years combined.

Since then: twenty-three festivals for city boards, cultural trusts, merchants' associations, brands and two promoters who swore they would never do it again and then did. Pune is home — this is the city that moves a god through its streets for ten days of Ganesh Chaturthi, closes its old city for my Lantern Fair, and still expects every road back by 23:00. Producing here teaches you respect for a crowd, because this city's crowds have seen everything.

A festival is a promise made to three parties at once: the artist, the audience, and the officer at the gate. My job is that none of the three ever has to think about the other two. The artist gets a stage that works and a fee that lands. The audience gets a gate that opens when the poster says it opens. The officer gets a safety case that was walked, on foot, three weeks earlier — not typed the night before.

“Fireworks at a food festival are a confession — you did not trust the food.”

That sentence has cost me two contracts and won me nine. It is the whole method in one line: every element of a festival has to earn its place by serving the people who came, or it is decoration bought with the safety budget. I would rather lose the booking than produce the version with the money spent in the wrong order.

How every production runs

The 90-day runway

This is why I will not take a festival inside ninety days. Each line below is a real lead time — paper, people and concrete all move at their own speed.

T – 90 days

Licences filed. Police permission, fire NOC, local body clearance, ground booking confirmed in writing. If a licence takes sixty days to issue, we file at ninety — paper does not care about your poster date.

T – 60 days

Artists contracted. Advances paid, travel and stay booked, the cover act signed as a real slot. A booking that exists only in an email thread is a rumour, and rumours do not play sunset sets.

T – 45 days

Vendor grid locked. Every stall pitch numbered against the site plan, power and water costed per pitch, PPL and IPRS music licences in hand. Stall holders sign the grid they will actually stand on.

T – 21 days

Safety case walked. The full route — gates, entry lanes, exits, water points, toilets, medical posts — walked on foot with the authorities who will sign it, at the hour the crowd will be there.

T – 7 days

Build week. Site marked, stages up, power tested at full load with everything switched on at once. The rain plan is checked against the forecast and the ground, not against hope.

Show day

Gates open thirty minutes before the poster says. The show is called from one desk, on one radio net, by people who have run the plan in rehearsal. In eleven years, my gates have never opened late.

Standing practice

  • Crowd maths to published ratios — the same planning figures printed in the scale planner on the home page, applied to every site
  • Certified core crew of nine — first-aid and fire-marshal trained, on my payroll, not hired by the day
  • Public liability insurance on every production — certificates shared with the client before the contract is signed, not after
  • Post-show report in ten days — counted attendance, every incident, and the full accounts, whether the news is good or not
