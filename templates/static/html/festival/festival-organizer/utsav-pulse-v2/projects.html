01

Deccan Riverfront Food Festival

The ask. The Riverfront Cultural Trust had grounds, a monsoon problem and a board to convince. They wanted a food festival that proved the site could hold a crowd — 96 stalls over three days — and they wanted it in October, eight weeks after the rains.

What I did. Walked the site in September while it was still soft, and moved the whole vendor grid onto the high terrace: rows of twelve, one-way food lanes, the stage at the far end so queues never crossed the music. Then, before a single stall was sold, I wrote the rain plan — every pitch was sold with a numbered terrace twin, marked and powered, so a flooded stall was a move, not a disaster. And no fireworks. Fireworks at a food festival are a confession: you did not trust the food.

The outcome. 38,000 people over three days. Stall holders reported their best weekend of the year, the Trust's board stopped questioning the site, and the festival was rebooked for the following October before the fences came down.

What went wrong. Day two, 16:40: a thunderstorm dropped 40 mm in an hour and the low row — parking and service only, by then — took water. Fourteen stalls on the terrace edge still had to move. They moved in 90 minutes, in the rain, because their twins were numbered and powered in advance. Dinner service lost one hour, not the festival.

02

Hillside Echoes

The ask. A first-time promoter, a beautiful hillside site above Lonavala, three stages, thirty-four artists — and a headliner flying in from Delhi on the afternoon of his own set. The ask, in their words: make it look like we have done this ten times.

What I did. Rebuilt the running order so that no stage, no lighting cue and no sponsor moment ever depended on that aeroplane. Contracted a Pune band as the standing cover act — paid properly, written into the schedule as a real slot, not a favour. Put the headliner's fee on a landed-not-booked clause, so a cancellation cost him money instead of costing the promoter a festival.

The outcome. 12,500 people across two days, and a second edition commissioned on the strength of the first.

What went wrong. The flight was cancelled — not delayed, cancelled — at 14:00 on show day. The cover act played the sunset slot to a crowd that had been told the truth by the MC, the headliner drove down the expressway with a police-coordinated entry, and closed the night ninety minutes late and entirely forgiven. Refund requests: 41 out of 12,500 tickets.

03

Old City Lantern Fair

The ask. Five evenings of street fair through six streets of Pune's old city — 140 stalls, live corners, thousands of visitors — under a licence condition most producers refuse outright: every street reopens to traffic by 23:00, every night, or there is no second year.

What I did. Built the entire fair as a kit. Stalls on wheels with their stock in crates, lighting on battery so no cable ever crossed a doorway, and a teardown crew of sixty walking a printed route behind the last visitors, street by street, the way a tide goes out. Residents got a printed card each year: what closes, when, and my phone number.

The outcome. 61,000 visitors across five evenings, and the streets reopened at 22:52, 22:47, 22:55, 22:49 and 22:51. The licence was renewed without a hearing, and the merchants' association added a sixth street the next year.

What went wrong. Night three, a delivery scooter entered a closed street through an unmanned lane and parked against a stall. Nobody was hurt; the lane map was wrong, and it was my map. From night four, every lane had a named steward with a radio, and the map is now checked on foot at 15:00 daily — a line in every street-fair plan I write since.

04

Harbour Lights New Year's Eve

The ask. The Cantonment Board wanted a New Year's Eve the whole city could attend free: 15,000 people — the parade ground's exact licence cap — one stage, and a fireworks finale at midnight.

What I did. Free entry, but ticketed. Every entrant registered in advance, gates counted in and out against the 15,000 cap all evening, and the ground zoned so that at midnight each zone left by its own gate in sequence. A full ground is a fine thing; a full ground with one exit is a headline.

The outcome. The ground reached its cap at 21:20 and the gates held the count there — turned-away visitors were told the number and the reason, and the big screen outside the fence carried the show for them. The ground emptied in 26 minutes with no gate reporting a surge.

What went wrong. At 23:40 the wind swung across the fall-out zone and the pyrotechnician held the finale. The MC told 15,000 people exactly why, every three minutes, in three languages — no countdown theatre, no pretending. The wind dropped at 23:50. The finale fired at 23:52, close enough to midnight that the cheer arrived on time.
