The hand

Twelve years of one brush stroke at a time.

Training, materials and the discipline behind a painted panel — from a studio in Vontikoppal with two windows and a north light.

WasliHandmade paper, glued in five layers, burnished with agate until it holds a hair line.

I was taught by B. S. Krishnappa, a temple painter in Mysuru who had learned from his father, who had painted processional chariots for the Dasara route. He gave me a squirrel-hair brush and a rule: paint the same lotus forty times before you paint anything else.

That is still how I begin with a new student, and how I begin a difficult commission. The lotus teaches the hand the pressure of a petal, the way a line thins where it turns. Indian miniature painting is not an art of gesture. It is an art of patience and of very small decisions, repeated until the panel holds together at arm’s length and at close range.

What a panel is made of

Everything is prepared in the studio. The wasli is layered and burnished by hand; the pigment is ground from stone or earth and bound with gum arabic; the gold is laid on a cushion of gesso so that it catches light from a lamp without glare. A single folio takes three to six weeks. A temple panel can take four months, and I do not take more than two panel commissions in a year.

I work from texts and from conversation. Before a commission I read the passage aloud, twice, and ask the person commissioning it what they see when they hear it. That answer usually decides the composition: who stands in the centre, where the light comes from, and whether the frame closes the story or leaves it open at the edge.

What I will not paint

I do not paint divine figures for advertising, and I do not paint a deity in a setting that the tradition would find careless. If your project needs a mythological image for a commercial product, I will say no politely and point you to illustrators who work in a contemporary idiom. These are not my rules alone; they came with the training, and I keep them.

I do paint for children. Some of the happiest work of the last five years has been for picture books, where the figures are smaller, the borders are wider, and a tiger may reasonably wear a garland.

How I teach

Two students at a time, in the studio, on Wednesday afternoons. We start with the lotus and the mango leaf, then the human profile, then a full figure. There is no fee for the first month; after that we agree on something that suits both of us. Three of my former students now paint for publishers in Chennai and Bengaluru.

A commission, end to end

  1. १

    Reading

    Your text, your memory, the passage you keep returning to.

  2. २

    Block

    Pencil composition at full size, sent as a scan for your notes.

  3. ३

    Colour

    Flat fields laid first, darkest to lightest, then the fine line.

  4. ४

    Gold

    Leaf laid last, when nothing else will be moved again.

  5. ५

    Finish

    Edges trimmed, sealed, scanned at 600 dpi, packed flat.
