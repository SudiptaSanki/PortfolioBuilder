About · Stall 14, East Masi Street

My grandmother strung for the temple street. I string for the whole city.

I learned the knot at my grandmother's knee in Madurai, sitting on a banana-leaf mat with a basket of marigold between us. She strung for one temple street. Nineteen years ago I took the corner that is now Stall 14 in the East Masi Street flower market, and I have kept the same hours she kept: up at three, at the market by twenty to four, stringing by half past five.

Madurai is a jasmine city. Our malli is famous because the buds are tight, white and heavy with scent, and because the women who string it are fast and honest. In Chithirai month, when the Meenakshi temple celebrates the celestial wedding and the whole city is on the street, my stall strings from dark to dark. The rest of the year is weddings, temple festivals, and the quiet one-foot pooja garlands that start a thousand houses' mornings.

Two things I will not do. I will not string jasmine that was picked after rain — it bruises by noon, and I will not sell it to you however early you queue. And I will not touch plastic flowers. A plastic garland at a wedding is a photograph lying; the real one wilts by nightfall, and that is exactly what it is for.

Why the price is per foot, and per flower

Visitors ask why a jasmine garland costs nearly twice a marigold one of the same length. Because the foot and the flower are two different bills. The foot counts my work — a foot of jasmine is 65 small buds and sixteen minutes of stringing; a foot of marigold is 45 flowers and ten minutes. The flower counts the market — jasmine after a dry week can double by Friday, and the rate card moves with the dawn price, never after your order is placed.

Wedding garlands run longer than temple ones — four feet against six is wrong way round in most people's heads, so here it is plainly: a temple garland is long because it must fall well down a deity's chest or a doorway; a wedding garland is shorter because two people must wear them, move in them, and be photographed in them for hours. Hair strings are two feet. A pooja garland is one foot and is the best value in the market.

Keeping a garland fresh

What I tell every customer at the stall, written down. A garland is cut flowers with no water — its freshness is borrowed time, and these are the terms.

  • Hang it, never pile it. Air through the flowers is the whole secret. A garland left in its bag sweats and browns.
  • Banana leaf, not plastic. The leaf breathes and holds one misting of water against the petals for hours.
  • Never the fridge for jasmine. Cold burns the buds brown. Jasmine waits in a cool, dark corner, in leaf.
  • Tuberose wants water. Stand the ends in a wet cloth; it is a stem flower wearing a garland's shape.
  • Mist once, lightly. A wet garland is a heavy garland, and heavy garlands slip their knots.
  • Order for the hour, not the day. Tell me the ceremony time and I will count backwards from it — that is what the planner on the home page does.
  • Marigold forgives; lotus does not. Marigold holds a full day. Lotus buds get three hours of grace, so they are strung last.
  • After the ceremony, temple flowers go to the compost cart, not the bin. The market takes back every leaf and stem.
