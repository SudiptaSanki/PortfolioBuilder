The hand

Four years to draw
a straight line that
belongs to something.

Zoya Farrukh, born in Lahore in 1986. Four years at the National College of Arts, two of them mostly in the miniature studio. Eighteen years painting on wasli, six folios a year, three students on Fridays.

The first thing they take away from you at NCA is the eraser. In the miniature studio you draw in light red on wasli, and a wrong line stays under everything you put on top of it. My first teacher, Saira Ahmed, said that this was not a punishment but a lesson in looking before the hand moves. I drew the same cypress for six weeks.

What the college could not teach

The technique came from the studio: how to paste five sheets, how to burnish with an agate, how to lay a flat wash without a tide mark. What the college could not teach was why a page should exist. That came later, from looking at the Lahore Museum's Mughal folios on Sunday mornings, and from the slow realisation that most of them were made for a person, not for a wall.

I still begin every commission by asking who the page is for. If the answer is “the drawing room”, the painting will be decorative and probably fine. If the answer is a name, or a room someone remembers, the painting has somewhere to go.

The wasli

Five sheets of handmade wasli paper, pasted with flour paste cooked in the studio, pressed under a marble slab for three days, then burnished with an agate until the surface closes. A good sheet rings faintly when you tap it. A bad one drinks the first wash and buckles, and there is no rescue; it becomes a practice sheet for students.

I make about forty sheets a year, in February when the air is dry enough. It is the least interesting part of the work and the part I would miss most if I stopped.

“A miniature is not small because the paper was expensive. It is small because the eye of the person holding it was meant to come close.”Zoya Farrukh, lecture at NCA, 2023

The pigments

Lapis from Badakhshan, still sold in Peshawar by weight; vermilion, which I buy ready-ground and keep sealed; orpiment, in a jar with a warning written on the lid; indigo, malachite, chalk, and a small amount of saffron for the yellows that are meant to fade. Gold is 23 carat leaf, laid on a gum base and burnished with a tooth.

I keep a swatch book of every mix with a date on it. The oldest swatch is from 2009: a vermilion that has darkened to the colour of dried brick. It is a useful document and a slightly embarrassing one.

Why the borders matter

The hashiya — the ruled and flowered border — is not a frame around the painting. It is the room the painting sits in, and it controls how close the eye comes. A wide border with a narrow band of gold tells you the page is formal. A thin border with flowers running into it tells you the page is a garden. I spend as long on the border as on the sky.
