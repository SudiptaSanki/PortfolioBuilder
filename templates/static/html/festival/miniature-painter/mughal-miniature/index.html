I

Grind two pigments, then paint the folio

Every colour in a miniature is a decision about permanence. Lapis will outlive the paper; saffron will fade in a decade and is used anyway, because nothing else is that yellow. Choose two and see what the page does.

A mix is never only a colour. It is a drying rate, a price, and a guess about how the page will look in fifty years.

III

Four months, in four steps

The part everyone forgets is the paper. Wasli is made, not bought, and it takes three weeks before a brush touches it.

01

The wasli

Five sheets of handmade paper pasted with flour paste, pressed under a stone, burnished with an agate. Three weeks, minimum.

02

The underdrawing

A line in light vermilion, corrected until the proportions are right. No white correction is possible on wasli, so the line is decided before it is made.

03

The colour

Gouache in thin layers, back to front: sky, architecture, figures, foreground. Gold last, burnished with a tooth.

04

The border

Gold rules, a hashiya of flowers, and the small signature in the lower margin. Then six weeks face-down under a board.

Notes from the margin

I keep a notebook of pigments with a swatch of every mix, the date, and what happened to it after a year on the wall. It is the least romantic object in the studio and the most useful.

The lapis in the sky of “The pavilion at evening” came from a dealer in Peshawar who sells it by the ounce, wrapped in newspaper. It took four washings to lose the grey. The gold is 23 carat, beaten thin enough to breathe through, and it still costs less than the blue.
