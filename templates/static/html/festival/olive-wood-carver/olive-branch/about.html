The carver

Yusuf Handal has been at the same bench since he was nine.

Fourth generation. He learned from his father, who learned from his father, who opened the workshop in 1948 with two gouges and a borrowed lathe. Mariam, his wife, runs the finish room and answers the letters.

The first thing my father put in my hand was not a gouge. It was a broom. For a year I swept the shavings and stacked the blanks, and only then was I allowed to cut, because a boy who has not swept does not know how much wood he is about to waste.

I was nine. The workshop was two rooms then, with a corrugated roof that made the whole street sound like rain in summer. My grandfather Yusuf — I am the second Yusuf — sat at the window because the light was best there, and he carved the same six things for forty years: a bowl, a spoon, a camel, a nativity, a box with a lid, and a small cross for the tourists. He did them well and without complaint, and he told me that repetition is not the enemy of craft; haste is.

I have tried to keep the six things. I have also, since 2004, taken commissions for pieces nobody in the family had made before: a board for a restaurant in Haifa, a set of mezuzot for a synagogue in Pittsburgh, a bowl with a crack left in it because the customer's mother had a bowl with a crack in it.

“The tree spent forty years making that dark line. If I cut it out, I have thrown away forty years to make something smoother. I would rather leave it and tell you where it came from.”

Where the wood comes from

Olive trees in this region are not cut for timber. They are pruned every February, and the prunings are burned in the street unless someone buys them. I buy them by weight from four families in Beit Sahour and Beit Jala, in cash, at a price agreed before the pruning. In a good year I take about two tonnes. In a bad year, a third of that.

The rest comes from storm fall and from old roof beams pulled out of houses being repaired — beams that are often more than a century old, dry as bone, and full of nail holes that have to be worked around. The nail holes are usually the best part.

I do not buy from orchard clearances, and I have been offered them. An olive tree takes decades to bear; a carver who buys cleared orchard wood is buying the end of his own supply.

What I will not make

I will not carve a crucifix for a tourist shop at a price that requires me to use unseasoned wood and paint over the cracks. I will not make a piece that pretends to be something it is not — machine-turned bowls sold as hand-carved, or wood stained to look like older wood. And I will not reproduce a design that belongs to another workshop, even when the customer brings a photograph of it and says it is only a small thing.

I have also refused, twice, to make a piece from a tree the customer wanted cut down in their own garden. I have offered instead to make something from the prunings, while the tree is still alive. One of those customers came back three years later and did exactly that.

Mariam, and the finish room

The finish room is the smallest room and the slowest work. Mariam mixes the oil herself — flax, beeswax, a little citrus — and keeps a ledger of every piece, the date it was oiled, and the date it can be handled. She will not let a piece leave before its time, and she has overruled me on this more than once.

She also answers the letters, which is why the replies are better than mine would be. If you write to the workshop, you are writing to her.

The street now

There were eleven carving workshops on Star Street when I took over. There are three. The others closed when the tour buses stopped coming, and the men who worked in them went to the settlements for building work or left the country. I am not telling you this to sell a bowl. I am telling you because a workshop is not a brand; it is a room with a roof and a person in it, and when the room goes quiet it does not come back easily.

What keeps this one alive is not souvenir shops. It is people who want one object, made once, and are willing to wait for it. That is a small market, and it is enough.

Things people ask at the bench

  • Is it really hand-carved? Yes, except the roughing out, which is done with a band saw. Everything after that — adze, gouge, scraper, sanding — is by hand. I can show you the tool marks.
  • Will it crack? Not if you keep it out of a dishwasher and direct sun. If it does crack in the first year, send it back and I will repair it or replace it.
  • How old is the wood? Between five and seven years in the shed, plus however old the tree was. The rings on the bench card are the tree's, not mine.
  • Can you put a name on it? Yes, carved, not burned, for a small extra charge. It takes a week and I will send a photograph before it ships.
