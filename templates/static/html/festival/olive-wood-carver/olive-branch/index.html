Olive wood does not want to be straight. It turns where the branch turned, darkens where the tree was wounded, and splits if you rush it. Everything in this workshop is cut from trees that were pruned, felled by weather, or pulled out of an old roof — never from an orchard cleared for wood.

What the wood decides

Three grains, three arguments

01

Straight grain

From the trunk, cut along the axis. Predictable, pale, and strong. It is what I use for boards that will be cut on and washed, because it will not tear out under a knife.

Boards · spoons · mezuzot

02

Figured grain

From the crotch where a branch met the trunk. The wood folds into itself and catches light in bands. Beautiful, and about thirty per cent more likely to move while drying.

Bowls · platters · pendant faces

03

Burl

A swelling on the trunk, full of dormant buds. Impossible to split cleanly, so it is cut thin and stabilised. One burl yields three or four small pieces and no two look alike.

Small boxes · jewellery · inlay

From tree to bench

Seven years in a shed before I touch it

Olive is dense and full of oil. Carve it green and it will crack along the pith within a month. So the blanks are cut oversize, sealed on the end grain, and stacked in sawdust in the shed behind the workshop for at least five years, ideally seven. I mark each stack with the year and the village it came from.

Only then does it come to the bench: axe, adze, gouge, scraper, then sandpaper from 80 to 800. The final cut is by hand because a machine burns the oil in the wood and the smell never leaves.

  1. Yr 0

    Pruning season

    February. The trees in Beit Sahour and Beit Jala are pruned by the families who own them; I buy the limbs by weight, in cash.

  2. Yr 0–1

    Sealing and stacking

    End grain sealed with wax, blanks stacked in sawdust, labelled with village and year.

  3. Yr 5–7

    The bench

    Shaped with hand tools, hollowed to a consistent wall, sanded through eight grits.

  4. Yr 7+

    Finishing

    Food-safe oil or beeswax, cured for three weeks, then buffed and wrapped in cotton.
