A calavera is not a mask. It is a portrait of a person who is still here, made for a day when the dead are invited back to the table.

The first face I painted was my cousin's, the night before Día de Muertos in 2009, with a white cream that cracked within the hour. She kept it on for the whole procession anyway.

Where the craft came from

My grandmother made alfeñique in the kitchen: sugar and lemon, pressed into little skulls and sold from a table in the market. I grew up thinking a skull was a sweet thing, a gift you gave to a child with their name on the forehead. That is the image I carry into every sitting, and it is why I wince at the imported plastic version of the same idea.

Face painting came later, through the comparsa. The dancers needed to be ready before the procession, in the street, in the cold of five in the morning. There was no proper light and no time to be nervous. You learn brushwork when twenty people are waiting and the sun is coming up.

How a sitting runs

  1. Skin test and base. A patch behind the ear first, then a thin white base in two passes. The face is a surface with a temperature; the base has to move with it.
  2. Symmetry. I mark the centre line in pale pencil and place the eyes and the mouth from it. Every flower after that is placed twice, once on each side, so the face reads as one design and not two halves.
  3. Colour. Magenta petals first because they hold the light, then marigold centres, then verdigris for the small leaves at the temple. Nothing is filled in flat; two tones per shape, so it reads at a distance.
  4. Line and set. The fine line goes on last: rays around the eyes, the teeth, the stitching at the mouth. A setting mist, ten minutes of drying, then the mirror.

Why it takes ninety minutes

Thirty minutes of it is conversation. The design is a decision the person makes about how much of themselves they want to show, and I do not rush that. Some people want the full catrina with the high brow and the tear; some want three flowers and their own eyes. Both are correct.

The rest is drying time, which cannot be hurried in humidity. In September the base needs twelve minutes; in the dry heat of April, six. I have learned to read the weather on the walk to the studio.

What the day is for

On the first of November the streets fill before dawn. Families carry cempasúchil to the graves, the comparsas dance, and by noon half the town is painted. A face that took ninety minutes may only be photographed twice. That is not a waste; the point was never the photograph. The point is that for one day, a person walked through their own town as a calavera and was greeted as one.
