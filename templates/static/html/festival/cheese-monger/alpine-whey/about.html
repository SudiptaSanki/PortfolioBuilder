Cave & milk · Dorfstrasse 14

A cellar cut into the hill, and seven dairies above it.

Cheese is made in the dairy and finished in the cave. I do the second part: buying raw wheels at three days old, salting, turning, brushing and waiting. The cave was blasted out of the hillside in 2016 and stays at 9 °C without a chiller, which is the reason the wheels taste the way they do.

The cave

Granite holds a temperature better than any machine.

The cellar is 24 metres long and 7 metres wide, with a ceiling of raw granite and a floor of the same. It sits under the shop and the yard, entered through a steel door that takes both hands. In summer the rock pulls the room to 9 °C; in winter the door stays shut and the wheels keep their own weather. The humidity comes from the floor, which is hosed twice a week and never quite dries.

There are 1,240 wheels in it at capacity, on spruce boards in seven rows, each row a different dairy so that a problem can be traced. The boards are scrubbed every quarter, the wheels turned by hand, and the rind rubbed with a brine cloth every ten days. Nothing is waxed, nothing is vacuumed, and nothing leaves before I have cut into a sample from the same batch.

The cave has no windows and one thermometer, a mercury one from 1962 that I trust more than the digital probe beside it. When the two disagree I believe the mercury. It has never been wrong in a way that mattered.

The milk

Ten and a half litres for a kilo of cheese.

Seven dairies, twenty-two kilometres

All seven are family farms between 760 and 1,240 metres, and all of them keep their cows on grass from May to October. The milk comes up in the morning and is in the vat by nine. In summer the pasture is high and the milk is thin and herbal; in winter the cows are on hay and the cheese is yellower, sweeter and slower to open. Both are in the cave, and the labels say which.

I pay by the litre, not by the kilo of cheese, which means the dairy carries the risk of a bad vat and I carry the risk of a bad cave. It is a slower arrangement than a commodity contract and it keeps the farms small. Two of the seven have fewer than twenty cows.

Raw milk, and why

Raw milk carries enzymes and a bacterial flora that pasteurised milk does not. In a long-aged alpine wheel those are the difference between a cheese that tastes of hazelnut and hay and one that tastes of nothing much. The trade is that the milk has to be impeccable and the cave has to be cold and clean. I visit each dairy twice a year, look at the bulk tank, and taste the milk warm. If either is off, the relationship ends that week, not at the end of the season.

The alpine year

  • May — the herds go up to the high pasture. The first summer wheels are small and pale.
  • July — the richest milk of the year; the Alpkäse made this month is what I keep longest.
  • September — the descent. The last high-pasture wheels come down and the cave fills to capacity.
  • November — hay milk begins. Softer paste, deeper colour, a shorter cave.
  • March — the cave is nearly empty and the oldest wheels are cut. This is the best month to visit.

The affineur

Matthias Brunner, Glarus, since 2014.

I grew up twenty kilometres from here, in a house where the cheese came from my uncle's dairy and was kept in a wooden box under the stairs. I apprenticed as a Käser at sixteen, spent six years in the Gruyère country learning to press a wheel, and came back in 2014 with a van and a loan. The cellar took two more years to cut.

The work is repetitive in a way I like: turn, brush, taste, note, wait. A wheel is judged by the sound of the tapping, the smell at the rind, and a plug taken with a trier. I write every tasting in a paper book, one line per wheel per month, and the book is the actual archive of this place. The website is only the shop window.

I am not a maker. The dairies make the cheese; I decide what it becomes. That is a strange job to explain at a dinner, and a simple one to do: keep the room cold, keep the rind clean, and cut nothing before its time.

At the bench

What the affinage bench is for.

The bench on the front page is the arithmetic I do at the start of every wheel's life, made visible. Weight loss, rind thickness and turning rhythm are the three things that decide whether a wheel opens well, and all three follow from the weight of the wheel and the weather of the cave. It is not a substitute for tasting; it is what keeps me from wasting two years to find out I had the humidity wrong.

If you are ageing your own wheel in a fridge, the numbers still work. Set the temperature to 9 °C, the humidity to 90%, and be honest about the weight. A fridge that cycles on and off will dry the rind faster than the bench predicts, so check the wheel every week for the first month and trust your hands over the percentages.
