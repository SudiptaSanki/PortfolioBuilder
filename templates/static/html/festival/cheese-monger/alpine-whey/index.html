Cheese monger · Glarus, Switzerland

Wheels that spend two winters in granite before they reach the counter.

I buy raw milk wheels from seven mountain dairies within twenty-two kilometres and age them in a cellar cut into the hillside. No wax, no shortcuts, no cheese that leaves before it is ready. The cave is at 9 °C and 92% humidity, and it decides the calendar, not me.

Affinage bench

Every wheel is a subtraction.

Cheese loses water from the day it is pressed, and what is left is the cheese. Set a wheel and a cave and the bench will tell you how much weight leaves, how thick the rind grows, how often it needs turning and what the paste will taste like at the end. The constants come from the cave log: 0.55% a week at 10 °C and 85% humidity, adjusted for both.

What the cave is doing

Patience and a cold, damp room. The paste turns from lactic to hazelnut to broth, the rind thickens until the wheel can be handled without marking, and the salt moves inward about a millimetre a week.

  • Hazelnut and hay
  • Brown butter, a little onion
  • A long, mineral finish

Turn daily for the first fortnight, then every third day. Wipe the rind with a brine cloth every ten days; the cave does the rest.

The everyday wheel: firm paste, small eyes, a clean walnut finish. It melts without oiling off, which is why the restaurant on the corner takes two a week.

Raw cow · 12 weeks · 1,100 m

A small, rindless wheel sold at ten weeks and eaten fast. Mild, lactic and springy, the cheese to put on a plate with radishes and a cold beer.

The counter

Come and taste before you buy.

The shop is the front room of the cellar, on Dorfstrasse, with a marble counter and a window that looks at the mountain. I cut to order, wrap in paper and write the dairy and the date on the label. Bring a bag; the paper is not a bag.
