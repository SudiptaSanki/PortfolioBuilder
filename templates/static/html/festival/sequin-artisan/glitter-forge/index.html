A sequin is a stitch
that will not sit still.

I am Nafisa Qureshi. Since 1996 I have stitched sitaara, katori and badla metal work into the costumes of Ramlila companies, Durga Puja tableaux and wedding processions — six hundred thousand sequins a season, one at a time, by the light of a bench lamp in Aminabad.

Nothing here is embroidered by machine. Nothing leaves the bench until it has been held up to a lamp and turned.

Four materials, and what each one costs the eye

Every commission is priced on stitch count, not on the size of the cloth. The four things I work with behave differently under a lamp, and that difference is the whole design.

How the count is worked

Coverage is the share of the cloth the sequins occupy: scattered is roughly a fifth, solid is almost all of it. A skilled hand seats about 900 sequins an hour, which is where the bench hours come from.

Katori throws light further than sitaara. Tikkadi is for a crowd, not for a family photograph.

What leaves the bench

  • Ramlila and nautanki costume

    Crowns, chest armour, shoulder capes and footwear for a company of twelve to forty actors, built to survive a month outdoors.

    Katori on canvas-backed cotton · 6–14 weeks · from ₹38,000 a set

  • Durga Puja and Ganesh mandal tableau

    Large drape panels, dhak-stand covers and backdrops that must read from thirty metres, in the rain, under floodlight.

    Tikkadi and mirror on tarpaulin · 4–8 weeks · from ₹52,000 a panel

  • Wedding and Teej cloth

    Sehra bands, dupatta borders and ghagra hems, worked closely enough to be read by the family, not by the crowd.

    Mukaish and 3 mm sitaara on silk · 3–6 weeks · from ₹21,000 a border

  • Repair and re-seat

    Old mukaish panels restrung on fresh badla, missing sequins matched by size and tarnish, loose stitches picked back to sound cloth.

    Worked from photographs first · 1–3 weeks · from ₹6,500
