The cloth is old.
The work is slow.
Both of those are the point.

Mukaish badla has been drawn, cut and stitched in Lucknow since the eighteenth century. I work with the same four materials the trade has always used, at the same pace, for the same reason: a sequin that has been put on by hand moves with the body underneath it.

What mukaish actually is

The word most people know is chamki, the shine. What we call the work is kamdani: metal wire, badla, flattened between rollers until it is a ribbon a little narrower than a matchstick, then cut into short lengths and stitched down so that the cut ends catch the light. Before there were plastic sequins there was only this — silver, sometimes gilt silver, drawn by a badla-wala from a bar of metal the thickness of a pencil.

Sitaara is the later, cheaper cousin: a flat disc with a single hole, sewn through the centre. Katori is the cup sequin, domed, so it holds a reflection longer and throws it further. A tikkadi is the big flat disc you see on processional cloth, often with a mirror set into it. On any one panel there may be three of these together, and each behaves differently under a lamp, which is why a costume looks alive at ten metres and muddy at one.

Zardozi — zar, gold, and dozi, sewing — came into Awadh with the Mughal court. Kamdani came after it and stayed after the court left, because it was cheaper and it survived washing. By the 1900s it was the trade of lanes like this one: a badla-wala at one end, four or five households stitching at the other, and a trader in between who took the profit. I still buy my badla from a family in Chowk who have drawn it for four generations, and I pay them before the cloth is cut.

Why the work is done with a hook

Almost nothing here is done with a needle. The stitch is made with an aari, a hook with a wooden handle, held in the fist and pushed through the cloth from above while the left hand holds the frame underneath. A needle has to be turned over for every stitch; a hook does not. That is the whole reason a karigar can seat nine hundred sequins in an hour and still keep the tension even enough that the cloth does not pucker.

The frame matters as much as the hook. The cloth is stretched on a wooden adda and held by four people or by rope, tight enough to sound like a drum when tapped. Slack cloth is where cheap work shows: the sequins drift off the drawn line, and in two seasons the panel hangs crooked.

What I will not do

I will not glue a sequin. I will not use a machine that stamps a motif and calls it embroidery. I will not copy a living karigar’s pattern from a photograph and sell it to a costume house as my own. And I will not take an order I cannot finish by the date the cloth is needed, because a Ramlila crown that arrives on the eleventh night is not late — it is useless.

How a panel is made

Six stages, in this order. If a client asks to skip the third, the answer is no — the pattern is the panel, and everything after it is labour.

  1. Cloth and sample

    We agree the cloth, the backing and the light it will be seen in. I ask for a 15 cm square of the actual cloth, not a photograph of it.

  2. Khaka

    The motif is drawn full-size on tracing paper, then pricked along every line and pounced with chalk so the pattern can be transferred to the cloth without a pen mark.

  3. Seating the frame

    The cloth goes onto the adda and is pulled until it rings. The chalked pattern is checked against the khaka before a single sequin is opened.

  4. Line work

    Badla and the larger discs go down first, because they set the geometry. A mistake here is cheaper to fix now than after the fill.

  5. Fill

    Sitaara and katori are seated from the centre outwards, in rows that follow the curve of the motif. This is the long part: most of the hours and most of the sequins.

  6. Lamp check

    The panel is held to a lamp, turned, and inspected for dark patches, crooked rows and sequins seated on the wrong side of a chalk line. Then it is signed on the back.

Questions that come up

Can a sequin panel be washed?

Dry clean only, and tell the cleaner what it is before handing it over. Badla tarnishes in water and the cut ends will catch on any rough lining. Between seasons, store flat in acid-free tissue, never on a hanger, and never in a plastic bag — the cloth needs to breathe.

How close to a deadline can you work?

A crown front is three weeks. A full Ramlila set for twelve actors is ten to fourteen. Tableau drapery is four to eight. I build in a week of slack before any festival date, because a delay at my end is not something the client can fix.

Do you work from a photograph of an old piece?

Yes, for repair and for re-seating. Send daylight photographs of the whole panel and of one damaged section at close range, and I will tell you whether the badla can be matched or has to be replaced. Replaced sections are always noted on the back.

Is the price on the workbench final?

No. It is an estimate built from sequin count, weight and bench hours at the rates shown. The final figure is confirmed after I have the cloth sample, because a heavy silk seats differently from a cotton canvas and takes more or fewer hours for the same count.
