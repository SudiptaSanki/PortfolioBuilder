One table, eight stories, and a room that has to agree to sit still.

I am a Grade 3 teacher who tells stories in December. The practice is small on purpose: one teller, one candle table, no amplification, and a repertoire narrow enough to know by heart.

Where the stories came from

My grandmother told stories in her kitchen in Dollard-des-Ormeaux, in French and Yiddish, with a tea glass and a plate of mandelbrot. She had six stories and told them in a different order every year, which is how I learned that a repertoire is a set of parts, not a script.

I started telling them to my own Grade 3 class in 2008, in English, because a class of twenty-eight children cannot follow a Yiddish line and I was not going to pretend otherwise. A parent asked me to tell them at a library branch the following December. The year after that there were four bookings, then nine, and at some point the December calendar stopped being a hobby.

I still teach. The classroom is why the sets work: I know exactly how long a seven-year-old can hold a question, how a room changes when the lights go down, and what a story sounds like when forty people are breathing in the same place.

What a night actually looks like

The host and I talk once on the phone, eight to ten weeks before the date. I ask four things: how many people, how old the youngest is, whether anyone in the room has hearing or sight needs, and where the candles can go without a fire hazard. Everything else follows from those answers.

On the night I arrive twenty-five minutes early with two plastic crates. One holds the candle table, the cloth, the tray and the props for that night's story. The other holds spares: a second shamash, extra matches, a small torch, a folded sheet in case the room has no table at all.

The set runs without a script. I know the shape of it and the five fixed points: the blessing, the first line, the turn, the closing question, and the moment I stop talking and let the room answer. Everything between those points can move.

What I will not do

No amplification unless the room genuinely needs it, because a microphone flattens the quiet parts. No backdrop, slide deck or sound cue. No retelling of the Maccabean battles with weapons for an audience of small children. And no Santa, no snowman, no "holiday season" blurring: this is a Hanukkah set, and December is crowded enough without pretending otherwise.

I do tell the Judith story to adults, which surprises people. It is a story about a clever hostess and a very tired general, and it explains the cheese.

What hosts provide

  • A table 70 to 90 cm high, or the promise of one.

  • Chairs in an arc, not rows, where the room allows it.
  • A switch for the overhead lights.

The twenty-five minutes before

The setup is part of the performance. People watch it happen and settle down while it does.

  1. Find the light switch

    Before anything else. A room with no switch gets a set with the lights on and the candles doing less work.

  2. Build the table

    Cloth, tray, eight candles and the shamash, the night's props laid left to right in the order they are needed. Nothing in a bag by the third line.

  3. Place the chairs

    A shallow arc if the room allows it, with a gap at the front for children who need to sit on the floor. The front row should be close enough to see the wick.

  4. Greet the host, then disappear

    I ask the host to introduce me in one sentence and then sit down. Long introductions turn a teller into a lecturer before the first word.

  5. Light the shamash and wait

    I light it, put the match down, and let the room go quiet on its own. It takes about eleven seconds. Nobody has ever needed prompting.

Training and the people who taught me

Nobody accredits a holiday storyteller, so here is the honest version.

My grandmother, Rivka Adler, taught me six stories and the rule that the telling is not finished until someone in the room answers a question. I studied English and drama at McGill, then a Bachelor of Education at OISE, where I took a course in oral history that changed how I ask questions.

Between 2012 and 2017 I worked with the Toronto Storytelling Festival as a volunteer teller and then as a host, which is where I learned to listen to a room while standing in front of it. I have taken workshops with the Jewish Storytelling Coalition and with the Ottawa Storytellers, and I read the sources: Maccabees, the Judith material, and the rabbinic argument in Shabbat 21b about where the lamp belongs.
