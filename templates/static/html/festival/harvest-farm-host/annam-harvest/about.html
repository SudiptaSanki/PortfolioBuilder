The farm

Three acres, one canal,
and a family that stayed

My grandfather bought this land in 1948 with money from a coconut harvest and a wedding that did not happen. It has been paddy ever since. The canal has been good to us and, twice in my lifetime, has not come at all.

Who is here

I am Meenakshi Sundaram. I farm with my husband Karthik, my mother-in-law Lakshmi, and two men from the village, Selvam and Arumugam, who have worked this land longer than I have. My son is in Coimbatore learning to be a mechanic and comes home for Pongal, which is the correct order of priorities.

We are not a homestay business that happens to have a field. The field is the business; the table is how we have always eaten, and the two rooms came later because visitors kept asking to stay the night after a Pongal breakfast and there was nowhere in the village to send them.

Patta 1187/2. The land record names my grandfather, then my father, then me. Three names in seventy-eight years is unusual here; most of the neighbouring plots have been sold twice.

The water decides everything

Our water comes from the Grand Anicut canal, which fills from the Kaveri when the Mettur dam opens. The dam usually opens in the second week of June, and the whole delta waits for that announcement the way other places wait for rain. When it opens late, the nursery is kept alive by hand pump and the transplanting is pushed into August.

In 2017 the canal carried nothing until October. We planted one acre instead of three, let the other two lie fallow, and bought rice for the first time since my grandfather's day. I do not romanticise a dry year. It is the reason we keep a second crop of black gram, and the reason the kitchen garden is not decorative.

The kolam, before anything else

Every morning my mother-in-law sweeps the ground in front of the door and draws a kolam in rice flour. Not rangoli, which is coloured and for festivals; a kolam is a single line looped around a grid of dots, drawn from memory, and it is finished in four minutes. The rice flour is food for the ants. That is not a charming detail added later; it is the point.

On Pongal the kolam is larger and the whole front yard is washed the night before. In 2021 a photographer from Chennai asked her to draw one slowly so he could film it. She refused, drew it at her usual speed, and told him he could film the ants instead.

The dots. A kolam begins with a grid of dots, and the line must pass around every one without lifting from the ground. Ours is a four-dot pattern, which is a child's pattern. She has never seen the need to make it harder.

The kitchen

The kitchen is the oldest part of the house and the only room with a chimney. It runs on firewood, a stove for the morning coffee, and a grinding stone that is used for idli batter twice a week. The rice we eat is our own, milled at Kallanai and brought back in sacks with the bran still in it.

Meals are simple because the day is long: idli and sambar at eight, rice with kootu and rasam at half past twelve, and something light at eight in the evening. On Pongal the order reverses and the sweet comes first, which the children have never once objected to.

What we do not serve. No filter coffee after four in the afternoon, no food on banana leaf for dinner (my mother-in-law considers it a waste after dark), and no rice that is not ours.

What we ask of a guest

Very little, and it matters. Take your shoes off at the step. Do not photograph the family without asking, including the children, including the cattle. Do not bring sweets from a shop; there is a pot on the fire. If you are here at Pongal, be awake by half past five, because the milk will not wait for you.

In return you will be fed properly, shown the fields, and given the mat on the thinnai where the breeze comes through at night. If the power fails, and it will, the conversation continues.
