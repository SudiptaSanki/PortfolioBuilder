01 — The chandas desk

Read the weights, and the metre names itself.

Sanskrit metre is counted, not guessed. The desk scans each syllable as laghu or guru, groups the syllables into gaṇas, and matches the result against the metres a reader meets first. Nothing here is sent anywhere.

Scan a verse

Paste a verse in Devanagari, or choose one of the four examples. Dāṇḍas are optional; the scanner finds the pādas from the syllable count.

No audio · no network

Laghu is one mātrā (⏑), guru is two (–). A syllable is guru if its vowel is long, if it is followed by anusvāra or visarga, or if a consonant closes it.

02 — Three kinds of work

Text, metre, language

The three cannot really be separated, but they are taught separately because a student can only hold one problem at a time. Eventually the problems become one problem, which is reading.

छन्दस्

Chandas

Metre taught as listening before naming: tap the laghu, hold the guru, feel where the pāda wants to break. Students who learn the names first tend to hear nothing at all. The desk on this page is the same exercise, done with the eyes.

पाठ

Text

Critical editions built from manuscripts rather than from previous editions. Variant readings stay visible in the apparatus; I do not smooth a text into a single confident line when the copies disagree. The disagreement is often the most interesting thing in the room.

भाषा

Language

Sanskrit for readers of English, from the first declension to the long compounds of the Mahābhārata without panic. Grammar is taught as a way of noticing, not a list to be memorised before the reading can begin.

03 — Method

What a critical edition actually involves

Less romantic than the word “manuscript” suggests, and slower. Most of it is photographing folios, comparing scribal habits, and deciding when a mistake is a mistake.

Witnesses
Every surviving copy is a witness, including the bad ones. A late, careless manuscript can preserve a reading that the careful copyist corrected away, so the careless copy is not discarded; it is dated, described, and given a siglum.
Collation
I read the witnesses line by line against a base text and record every difference, including spelling. The apparatus is not a decoration at the foot of the page; it is the evidence for every choice in the text above it.
Conjecture
When no witness is right, the editor proposes a reading and says so. A conjecture that fits the metre and the sense is still a conjecture. I mark them, and I keep a list of the ones I have withdrawn.
Metre as evidence
Chandas is the editor’s cheapest test. If a proposed reading breaks the metre in a line that is otherwise regular, the reading is probably wrong. This is why the metre desk sits in the middle of the work, not at the edge of it.
