I came to Sanskrit through a radio, not a classroom.

A small transistor on a kitchen shelf in Nagpur, a programme of verses at six in the morning, and a neighbour who could explain why a line sounded the way it did. Everything since has been an attempt to make that explanation exact.

Nagpur, 1972–1990

My father was a clerk in the Central Railway and my mother sang bhajans at the neighbourhood temple on Tuesdays, badly and with great conviction. The radio was hers. At six in the morning All India Radio played a programme of Sanskrit verses, and because the kitchen was small and the transistor was loud, I learned several of them by sound before I could read a single letter of the script.

The neighbour was a retired schoolteacher named Deshpande, who had taught Sanskrit for thirty-one years and had opinions about every metre. He did not begin with grammar. He made me say the Gāyatrī and tap the table, and told me which syllables were heavy. When I asked why, he said, “Because the poem is walking, and you are listening to the feet.” I was eleven. It took me another twenty years to understand that this was a complete account of prosody.

तत्सवितुर्वरेणियं भर्गो देवस्य धीमहि ।
धियो यो नः प्रचोदयात् ॥

tat savitur vareṇiyaṃ bhargo devasya dhīmahi / dhiyo yo naḥ pracodayāt — Rigveda 3.62.10, twenty-four syllables in three pādas of eight.

The long detour through grammar

I studied Sanskrit at Savitribai Phule Pune University because a teacher there, Mrs Kulkarni — no relation, a coincidence we both found funny for four years — refused to let me specialise in poetry until I could parse a compound without guessing. She was right and I resented it for a decade. The grammar is not the enemy of the verse; it is what lets you see the joins.

My doctoral work was on the manuscript tradition of the Mṛcchakaṭika, which sounds grander than it was. Mostly it meant travelling to small libraries with a camera and a notebook, photographing palm leaves that had been stored in tin trunks, and comparing the handwriting of scribes who had copied the same play two hundred years apart. I learned that a copyist’s mistakes are more informative than his corrections, because mistakes are unconscious.

Twelve years of cataloguing

For twelve years I worked on a catalogue of Sanskrit manuscripts held in Deccan collections: 4,600 entries, most of them never described in print. The work is unglamorous and it changed how I read. When you have held four copies of the same text and seen how differently four people wrote the same word, you stop believing in a single correct text. You start believing in a text with a history.

I still keep the catalogue cards for the manuscripts I could not identify. There are 63 of them. Two turned out to be fragments of a ritual manual in a very bad hand; the rest are still waiting, and I look at them on quiet Fridays.

“A manuscript is not a message that survived. It is a person’s afternoon, preserved by accident.”

Teaching, and why metre comes first

I teach chandas before I teach declension, which upsets people who prefer a syllabus. The reason is practical: metre gives a student a way to hear that something is wrong before they can say what. A verse that breaks its metre is usually a verse that has been misread, and a student who can hear the break will go back and find the sandhi.

My Monday class has eleven regulars and a changing population of listeners. Two are retired; one is nineteen and wants to read Kālidāsa in the original; one is a software engineer who comes because the counting calms him. We read slowly. In a good term we finish two cantos.

What I am slow at

Computers, self-promotion, and deadlines set by other people. The scanner on the home page was built with a former student over four months, mostly because I kept insisting that the final syllable of a pāda should not be counted against the pattern. He kept saying the machine could do that. It could. I was wrong about the difficulty and right about the principle.

I answer letters on Tuesdays and Fridays. If a thing is urgent, say so in the first line; I will not be offended, and I will not pretend that slowness is a virtue when someone needs an answer.

A note on the method

Three things I will not do

They are worth stating because people ask for them politely and often.

Invent a reading
If the manuscripts disagree, the edition shows the disagreement. I will conjecture when a line is metrically impossible, and I will mark the conjecture as mine. I will not quietly choose the reading that makes the best sentence.
Translate without the metre
A translation that ignores the metre is a summary. I try to keep the syllable count where English allows it, and where it does not, I say what has been lost in the note rather than hoping nobody notices.
Call the work easy
Sanskrit is hard, and the difficulty is part of what it has to teach. I would rather a student leave after one term knowing ten verses properly than a year knowing a hundred approximately.
