The work

Walls I have painted, and what came of them

Every wall here was asked for by somebody, for a reason. I have written down what they asked, what I did, and what happened — because a painting you cannot explain is only decoration, and Warli was never decoration.

The wedding chauk at Dahanu

Wall:
14 × 9 ft courtyard wall, facing the gate
Scene:
wedding — the lagna chauk, Palaghat at the centre
Time:
15 days — 2 preparing, 13 painting
Price:
₹69,300 (126 sq ft at the wedding rate)

What they asked. A family in Dahanu was marrying their daughter from home, and wanted the chauk painted on the courtyard wall where the guests would arrive — not on paper, not small, on the wall itself, the way it was done when the grandmother married. The wedding was six weeks away.

What I did. I prepared the wall, then drew the lagna chauk first, before any figure, and the bride's mother checked it line by line — the chauk is the goddess's seat, and it is not mine to improvise. Only when she nodded did the procession, the drummer and the guests go up around it, working outward for thirteen days.

What came of it. The wall was finished four days before the wedding. The family has since refused two offers to have it "touched up brighter" by a town painter. It stays as it is, and the daughter's photograph in front of it is the one they framed.

"She drew the chauk on the first day and my mother stood and checked every line before she went further. On the wedding morning the wall was the first thing our guests photographed — before the bride, my daughter says, and she is not wrong."

Kavita Patil, Dahanu — the bride's mother

The harvest wall on the godown

Wall:
24 × 10 ft, the farmers' collective grain godown outside Talasari
Scene:
harvest — Kansari's chauk, reapers, about 720 figures
Time:
14 days — 2 preparing, 12 painting with two painters
Price:
₹1,29,600 in all (240 sq ft at the harvest rate, plus Datta's 12 days)

What they asked. The collective's godown wall faces the road, and for years it carried only a faded painted sign. The members voted to spend part of the year's surplus on a harvest wall — Kansari over the door end, the reaping going on along the whole length — to be finished before the new grain came in.

What I did. Two hundred and forty square feet is not a one-painter wall against a harvest deadline, so my cousin Datta painted beside me — his reapers march from the right, mine from the left, and where we met in the middle we drank tea and argued about whose sheaves stood straighter. The chauk I drew alone; that is not shared work.

What came of it. The wall was done nine days before the first carts arrived. The collective now weighs the new grain under Kansari's chauk, which is where the weighing should happen, and the school bus slows down at the bend so the children can look.

"We argued in the meeting whether a painting was a proper use of the surplus. After it was finished nobody argued. The wall does the work our signboard never did — it says whose grain this is."

Anil Vaze, secretary, Talasari Farmers' Collective

A tarpa for the school hall

Piece:
24 × 36 in on geru-washed handmade paper, framed by the school carpenter
Scene:
tarpa circle — fourteen dancers and the player
Time:
five evenings
Price:
₹7,500, the paper rate — the school paid from its own fund, in full

What they asked. The headmaster of the Zilla Parishad primary school in Talasari wanted the children to see their own dance somewhere in the school that was not a textbook photograph. His words: "Our children dance the tarpa every harvest. Let them walk past it every morning."

What I did. I painted it on paper, at the big size, over five evenings after the haat days — a full ring of dancers with the tarpa player in the middle, and the gap left in the circle where the next dancer joins, the way the dance itself is danced.

What came of it. It hangs in the school hall. The children have counted the dancers — fourteen, and the player makes fifteen — and a Class 4 girl informed the headmaster that the gap in the circle is "for whoever is late". She is exactly right, and I have quoted her since.

"Visitors ask where we bought it. The children correct them before I can: it was not bought, it was painted for us, by Sunita-tai, whose niece sits in Class 3."

Suresh Bhoir, headmaster, ZP Primary School, Talasari

Devicha Dongar, on cloth

Piece:
5 × 3.5 ft on geru-dyed cotton cloth
Scene:
Devicha Dongar — the hill of the goddess, the climb to her shrine
Time:
7 days — 1 stretching the cloth, 6 painting
Price:
₹9,500

Sold

What she asked. A schoolteacher from Boisar, whose father's people come from a village under Devicha Dongar, asked me to paint the hill for his front room — the shrine at the top, the path, the climb. "He cannot climb it any more," she said. "Bring the hill to him."

What I did. Cloth, not paper — a hanging can be taken down, carried and re-hung when a family moves, and this one will move when he does. I painted the path the way it is walked: it doubles back on itself, and it is steepest just before the shrine, which anyone who has climbed it will tell you is true.

What came of it. It hangs where she said it would. Her father, she writes, touches the little shrine at the top of the painting every morning before his tea. I did not paint it for that, but I am glad it is used that way.

"My father shows it to every visitor and walks them up the path with his finger, naming the turns. The painting is the hill, for him. That is all I asked for and it is exactly what came."

Meena Gharat, Boisar — who commissioned Devicha Dongar
