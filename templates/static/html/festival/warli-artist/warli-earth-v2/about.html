About

My grandmother's wall is where I learned

In our villages, a house wall is not finished when the plaster dries. It is finished when it is painted — for a wedding, for the new rice, for the goddess on the hill. I learned standing on a stool beside my grandmother, holding the rice paste while she drew.

How I came to paint

From our walls to yours

I am Sunita Bhoye, forty-one years old, from a village outside Talasari in Palghar district. The Warli are my people; this painting is our way of writing. Before we had paper in our houses we had walls, and the walls carried everything — the harvest, the weddings, the gods, the animals, the year.

My grandmother painted the inside walls of our house, and my mother after her. The walls were replastered and painted again for every wedding and every good harvest, so the paintings I grew up inside were never the same twice. I was fourteen when my grandmother first let me draw the guests at the edge of a wedding wall — the small figures, where a shaky hand does the least harm. I have been moving toward the centre of the wall ever since.

In my mother's time, painters from these villages began working on paper for buyers from the towns, and the painting learned to travel without leaving home. I sell at the Dahanu haat and by commission. Nineteen harvests now. The walls still come first — a season with no wall in it feels unfinished, whatever the paper sales say.

Materials, told honestly

Rice paste, geru, and one modern compromise

The ground. Traditionally the wall is plastered with mud and cow dung, smoothed, and washed with geru — red ochre earth — which gives the deep red-brown you see everywhere on this site. On paper and cloth I wash the same geru colour into the ground before I begin.

The white. The white is rice paste: rice ground fine, mixed with water, a little tree gum to help it hold. The brush is a bamboo stick, chewed soft at the end. For our own walls and our own festivals, this is still exactly what I use, ground the same morning I paint.

The compromise. Rice paste on paper that must travel, be framed, and live in a town flat will crack in a dry winter and interest insects in a wet one. So for paper and cloth that leaves the district, I paint with a white pigment that behaves like rice paste and lasts like paint. I will always tell you which white your painting is before you pay — ask, and I will show you both.

Care. Keep the painting dry and out of hard sun. Dust it with a dry cloth, never a wet one. A wall painting should never be washed; if a corner flakes, that is a repair, and the first one is mine to make.

Reading the painting

The figures and what they carry

Nothing in a Warli painting is there only to fill a corner. These are the signs you will meet in my work, in the order a child here learns them.

The two triangles

Every person is two triangles joined at the waist and a circle for a head — shoulders above, a skirt below. A child here can draw a person before she can write her name. It is our first letter.

The tarpa

The harvest dance. The player stands in the middle blowing the tarpa, a pipe made from a gourd, and the dancers circle him holding each other, spiralling wider as the night goes on. I never close the circle, and I never paint it without him.

The chauk

The square is sacred ground. The dev chauk is drawn for the gods; the lagna chauk for a wedding, with Palaghat, the mother goddess, at its centre. The chauk is drawn first, and at a wedding the family checks it before one more line is painted.

Kansari and the grain

Kansari is the goddess of the grain. When the new rice comes in, the first sheaves are hers, and in a harvest painting she sits in her chauk with the sheaves around her while the reapers work under her eye. That is the order of things.

Devicha Dongar

The hill of the goddess. Every village here has its hill, and she sits on top of it. We climb to her with offerings; the path doubles back on itself, and it is steepest just before the shrine — anyone who has climbed it will tell you so.

The creatures

The pointed tree, the sun, birds over the fields, fish in the pond, and the peacock, who thinks very well of himself. A painting holds the whole living world of the village, or it is not finished.

My position, since you will ask

What I will not paint

You can buy a cushion cover printed with a tarpa circle, the dancers cut off at the edges where the cloth ran out. That is not Warli; that is wallpaper wearing our clothes. In a real tarpa the circle is a living thing — it opens to let the next dancer in. Cut it at the edge of a cushion and you have killed the one thing it means.

And I will not paint a wedding chauk for a hotel lobby or an office. The lagna chauk is a seat made ready for the goddess at a real wedding, in a real family's house. It is not a pattern for a wall behind a desk. Ask me instead for a harvest, a tarpa, the hill of the goddess — those belong to everyone who asks with respect, and I will paint them gladly, and well.

Teaching

The painting should not end with me

Every year I teach a small group of girls from the villages around Talasari — my niece among them — the way my grandmother taught me: small figures at the edge first, the chauk last, when the hand is steady and the understanding has come. Two of my students now sell their own paper paintings at the haat.

If you commission a wall, your children are welcome to hold the bamboo sticks and watch, and if a daughter of the house wants to draw the guests at the edge, as I once did, I will make room for her.
