Kirtan Lamplight·The leader

I was taught to sing one bhajan for six months.

Radhika Sharma has led kirtan in Vrindavan since 2012. She learned the harmonium from her grandmother, the ragas from a teacher who did not believe in hurry, and the room from two hundred people who sing back.

I grew up in a narrow house off the parikrama path, where the morning began with my grandmother's harmonium and the evening ended with her telling me to stop singing and listen. She played by ear and never named a raga. It was only later, under my teacher, that I learned what she had been doing all along.

The six months

My teacher, Pandit Shyam Lal Mishra, accepted me on a Tuesday and gave me one bhajan: Achyutam Keshavam. For six months I sang it in a small room with the door closed. No ornament, no taan, no change of tempo. If I added a flourish, he would raise one hand and we would begin again. I was nineteen and furious for most of it.

At the end of the sixth month he asked me to sing it in front of eight people. I did, plainly, and one of the eight began to cry. He told me afterwards that the tears had nothing to do with my voice. They came because the bhajan had been allowed to be itself. That is the lesson I still teach: the raga does the work, if you let it.

“A kirtan leader is not the best singer in the room. The leader is the person who makes it possible for everyone else to sing.”

Pandit Shyam Lal Mishra, first lesson, 2006

What I actually do

I choose the raga for the hour, the bhajan for the room, and the tempo for the energy I can feel at the door. I teach the refrain line by line before we begin. I keep the kartal steady when the room speeds up and slows it when the room is rushing. I stop a kirtan that has become a performance and start again softer.

For a public night I do not use a microphone under a hundred voices. The harmonium is acoustic, the kartal are loud, and a room that has to listen is a room that sings better. For larger halls I use one condenser microphone on the harmonium only, so the voices can still fill the space unamplified.

Ragas and the hour

The raga is chosen for the time of day, not for the mood of the singer. Yaman belongs to dusk, Bhairavi to the morning and to the end of a long night, Malkauns to the small hours, Durga to the early evening, Kafi to the night. Singing Malkauns at six in the evening is not a sin, but it is a singer showing off, and the room can tell.

Teaching, and the Tuesday group

On Tuesday mornings I teach a group of eleven women in the haveli courtyard. Most had been told at some point that they could not sing. The first month is spent proving that untrue, the second on breath, the third on the refrain. They now lead two kirtans a year themselves, which is the point of teaching.

I do not take beginners as private students unless they can come weekly for six months. Half-learned kirtan is worse than none, and I am not a patient teacher on a quarterly schedule.

Money and the lamp

Public kirtans at the haveli are free; the tea is by contribution and the lamp oil comes out of it. Private satsangs and festivals pay a fee that covers my travel, the harmonium's hire and a day of teaching lost. Schools in the district pay half. Nobody has ever been turned away from the courtyard for money, and that is a rule I keep.

Training

Harmonium and bhajan from my grandmother, Kamla Devi, from the age of six. Ragas and voice from Pandit Shyam Lal Mishra, 2006–2014, in the guru–shishya manner: one bhajan at a time, no shortcuts.

I still practise for an hour before dawn, which is the only time the lane is quiet.

What I bring

A harmonium (rented in Vrindavan), two pairs of kartal, a small oil lamp, and a notebook with the refrains written out in Devanagari and transliteration for anyone who asks.

For halls over a hundred and fifty, one microphone for the harmonium. Nothing else.

Lineage, named

The bhajans are traditional, and the versions I sing come from my grandmother and my teacher. Where a composer is known, I name them at the start of the bhajan: Tulsidas, Narsinh Mehta, Surdas, Mirabai.
