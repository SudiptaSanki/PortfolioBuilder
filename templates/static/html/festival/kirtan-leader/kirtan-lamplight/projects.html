Kirtan Lamplight·Repertoire

Five ragas, eight bhajans, one steady kartal.

A night is built from what the room can sing, not from a fixed set list. These are the ragas I keep in practice and the bhajans the rooms already know, with the taal and the length they usually take.

The ragas

Chosen for the hour, not the mood.

Yaman

Dusk · 6–9 pm · Kalyan thaat

The first raga most singers learn and the one that never quite leaves. Tivra Ma gives it the lift; the vadi is Ga. Good for a room that is still arriving, because it forgives a little chatter.

सा रे ग म॑ प ध नि सां

Bhairavi

Morning, and the end of night · Bhairavi thaat

All four komal notes, and the raga that closes a long kirtan. Singing it at the end is a way of saying the night is over without saying it. The vadi is Ma.

सा रे॒ ग॒ म प ध॒ नि॒ सां

Malkauns

Midnight · 12–3 am · Bhairavi thaat

Five notes, no Sa in the ascent until the top, and a gravity that makes a room go quiet. Not for a family crowd at seven in the evening. The vadi is Ma.

सा ग॒ म ध॒ नि॒ सां

Durga

Early evening · 6–9 pm · Bilawal thaat

Five notes, bright and open, with no Ga and no Ma. It suits a kirtan with children in the front row, and a harmonium that is slightly out of tune.

सा रे प ध नि सां

Kafi

Night · 9 pm–midnight · Kafi thaat

The raga of the bhajans everyone already knows. Komal Ga and komal Ni, a folky lilt, and a tendency to turn into a jhala if the kartal are allowed to speed up. The vadi is Pa.

सा रे ग॒ म प ध नि॒ सां

What I will not do

A note on requests

No film songs, no recorded tracks, no amplification that drowns the room. If you want a kirtan that sounds like a concert, there are wonderful singers for that; it is simply not this practice.

Lengths are what the bhajan usually takes once the room is singing, not a fixed time. The mahamantra has no length: it ends when the room has arrived.

The shape of a night

Build, slow, close, tea.

A public kirtan runs about ninety minutes. We open with a short invocation in Bhairavi or Yaman, teach the first refrain, then build through two or three bhajans in Kafi or Durga. The tempo rises, the kartal get louder, and then we stop before the room is exhausted.

The slow bhajan comes when the room is full and warm, usually Mirabai in Bhairavi. Then one final refrain everyone knows, standing, and the lamps are carried out. Tea on the steps. The singing stops before the talking starts.

For a private satsang I ask the host three questions: how many people, what time of day, and whether there are children. The answers choose the raga and the length. A housewarming at four in the afternoon is not the same kirtan as a death anniversary at seven in the evening, and it should not be.

Requests are welcome if they are in the repertoire. If a bhajan is requested that I do not lead, I will say so at the start rather than pretend my way through it.

If you have a date in mind, tell me the hour and the number of people. I will send back a raga, a set and a plain fee.
