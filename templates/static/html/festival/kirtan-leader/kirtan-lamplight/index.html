01 · The practice

The leader starts. The room decides how it ends.

Kirtan is not a performance with an audience. It is a call and a response, repeated until the refrain stops needing to be led. I sing a line; the room sings it back. On a good night, by the ninth round the harmonium is almost unnecessary and the kartal are keeping time for two hundred people who have forgotten they were shy.

I was taught by my grandmother in a house with a low ceiling, and later by a teacher in Vrindavan who made me sing the same bhajan for six months before he let me add a single ornament. That patience is the whole training. The raga is not a decoration on the words; it is the room the words live in.

A lamplit kirtan has a shape: an invocation that names the tradition, three or four bhajans that build in tempo, a slow one when the room is full, and a final refrain that everyone already knows. Tea afterwards, outside, sitting on the steps. The singing stops before the talking starts, which is the sign of a good evening.

I lead in Hindi and Braj, with English explanations for the parts that need them. Nobody is asked to know the words before they arrive. The refrain is taught in the first two minutes, line by line, and then we begin.

Sargam is the ladder a singer climbs and comes back down. Lowercase re, ga, dha and ni are komal, a semitone lower; the capital letters are shuddha.
