The workshop · Naya, Pingla

One room, two tables, four brushes

The painting room faces the courtyard so the cloth dries in shade and the door can stay open. It has been the same room since 1998; the tables are newer.

The village paints

Naya sits about fourteen kilometres from Kharagpur, in the red-soil belt of Paschim Medinipur. Around eighty families here paint pats; most of them also sing. It was not always a livelihood. Through the 1980s, pats sold for a few rupees at village fairs and the songs were sung for rice. What changed was the road, and the museum crowd, and a handful of families who kept painting through the years when nobody was buying.

My grandmother Meenakshi painted through that time. She worked sitting on the floor with the cloth stretched on a wooden board, and she taught in exchange for a bowl of rice. I learned by grinding her colours, which is the honest way: you cannot paint red until you know how long vermilion takes on the stone.

A pat has two halves. The painting is the quiet half. The singing is the half that makes a room go still.

The singing is not decoration. The scroll is unrolled panel by panel while the singer stands and names what is coming. My uncle sings the Manasa pats in a cadence that is closer to argument than to melody. My mother sings Chandi in a higher, flatter line. I sing my own scrolls badly and I keep practising.

Fourteen years, in order

  • 2012First paid scroll, a three-panel Manasa for a school in Midnapore. ₹600 and a bus fare home.
  • 2015Started painting full time after a year of grinding colours for my grandmother and uncle.
  • 2018First exhibition outside Bengal, in Delhi. Sold four scrolls and learned that a city wall wants panels, not length.
  • 2020The village shut. Painted twenty small pats and sent them out by post, one a week, to people who wrote to ask.
  • 2022Nine-panel Ramayana, the largest single commission so far, for a family in Toronto.
  • 2024Began teaching the song alongside the painting in the village workshop, four students at a time.
  • 2026Sixty-two scrolls painted, thirty-one of them still in the room or in the village.

What is in the room

The materials are ordinary and local. Most of the cost of a pat is the weeks it takes, not the pigment.

Cotton

Mill cloth from Kharagpur, 60 counts, washed twice in the pond. About 1.1 m wide; a scroll is two or three widths stitched with a flat seam.

Tamarind size

Seed kernels soaked overnight, pounded and strained, mixed with chalk. This is what makes the cloth take paint without bleeding.

Conch white

Shells bought from a trader in Digha, broken and ground with water on a flat stone. Two hours of grinding for one small pot.

Vermilion

Stone vermilion, not the powder dye. Ground fine, it sits on the cloth like a thin skin and darkens over years.

Indigo

A cake from the market in Midnapore, dissolved and strained through cloth. It is the colour that fades first; keep the pat out of the sun.

Lampblack

Soot from a mustard-oil lamp held against a brass plate. Mixed with bel gum so it does not dust off the cloth.

Brushes

Goat hair bound to a bamboo stick, cut short for outlines and long for the border flowers. Four brushes do a whole scroll.

The board

A teak plank, 2.2 m, with the cloth pinned at the edges. It lives against the west wall when not in use.

Come and paint

The village workshop runs from November to February, when the air is dry enough for the size to cure. A day begins at seven with the cloth, and by four you will have drawn a border and blocked in one figure. Materials, lunch and the loan of a brush are included. Children over ten are welcome with an adult.
