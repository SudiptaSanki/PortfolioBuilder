01 The studio · Bondegatan 42

I came for the flowers and stayed for the space.

Twelve years of study, one long table, and a courtyard where the maple turns earlier than the one on the street. This is how the work is made and why it looks the way it does.

From a florist's van to a Kyoto classroom

I trained as a florist in Malmö and spent four years arranging flowers for weddings and hotel lobbies. I was good at it, and it made me unhappy. A bouquet is finished when it is full; the more you add, the more generous it looks. I kept having the opposite instinct — that the interesting thing was the gap between two stems, and the way a branch made you look at the wall behind it.

In 2008 I went to Kyoto for a six-week course and stayed four years. I studied with the Sogetsu School, which is the least reverent of the classical schools and the one that suited me: it insists on the classical forms, then asks what they mean in a room that did not exist when they were written. My teacher, a woman in her seventies who had arranged for the imperial household, corrected my work mostly by removing things.

“You are not making a garden. You are making a sentence.”
Ikebana is a decision about what to leave out. The rest is material.

What the studio is

A room on the ground floor of a courtyard building on Bondegatan, with a north-facing window, a long oak table, and a concrete floor that can take water. There is a cupboard of vessels — perhaps sixty, most of them picked up secondhand — and a shelf of kenzan, the lead pinholders that hold the stems. On class days the table is cleared to the wall and six places are set with a mat, a basin and a pair of secateurs.

The materials come from three places: a florist wholesaler at Årsta for flowers, a garden in Nacka for branches and grasses, and the courtyard itself, which gives me maple leaves in October and bare twigs in February. In a Swedish winter there is no cherry blossom; an arrangement has to be made of what is actually outside, or it becomes costume.

Ma, and the room

Ma is usually translated as negative space, which makes it sound like something missing. It is better understood as interval — the pause that makes a phrase legible. In an arrangement, ma is the air the longest line needs in order to lean. It is also the distance between the arrangement and the wall, and the reason I ask where a piece will stand before I make it.

This is why the studio work is always shown in a corner, on a tokonoma-like shelf, or on a plinth with nothing else on it. An arrangement placed beside a lamp, a book and a bowl of fruit has no ma left; it becomes decoration, which is a different job.

How a class runs

  • Look first. The theme of the evening is written on the board; everyone looks at the materials for five minutes before touching them.
  • Build alone. No talking for the first hour. I walk around and ask questions rather than give answers.
  • Take one thing out. Before adjusting an angle, every student removes one element. It is usually the right change.
  • Look at everyone's. The last half hour is spent at the table with coffee, saying what each arrangement is doing.
  • Photograph, then dismantle. The materials go back into buckets and are used again; nothing is kept unless it is for a commission.

The room North window, 4.2 m × 5.1 m, concrete floor, one long oak table built by a carpenter in Gnesta. Capacity: six students and one teacher who moves.
Vessels Around sixty, mostly secondhand: suiban basins, cylinders, bamboo sections, one cracked stoneware jar that is only used with dry branches.
Materials Wholesaler at Årsta, a garden in Nacka, and the courtyard. Nothing imported out of season; in February that means pine, willow and forced tulips.
On the walls Three photographs of my teacher's arrangements, a calendar from a Kyoto paper shop, and a hook where the apron goes.
