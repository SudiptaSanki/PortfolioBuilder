زر دوزی · gold work on velvet

Gold, laid by hand, one stitch at a time

Zardozi does not print, weave or glue. It lays metal thread on cloth and holds it there with a needle, which is why a bridal lehenga takes eleven hundred hours and why the underside is as beautiful as the front. The atelier is on the first floor above a cloth shop in Chowk, and it has been in the family since 1948.

The sampler

Five stitches, one hoop

Choose a stitch and draw on the velvet. Every mark is laid the way a karigar lays it: the needle through, the metal pulled flat, the next one a hair's width away. The guide is a paisley, the same one a new apprentice traces for three months before touching a real order.

Why it costs what it costs

Eleven hundred hours, and a name on every piece

i

The metal is not thread

Badla is silver wire, flattened or coiled, and it does not bend twice. A stitch laid badly cannot be unpicked without marking the velvet, so the karigar decides before the needle goes in.

ii

The cloth is the ground, not the page

Velvet, raw silk, organza. Each holds metal differently: velvet swallows it, silk throws it back. The design is drawn for the cloth before it is drawn for the client.

iii

Twelve pairs of hands, named

The atelier pays by the piece, not by the hour, and every finished garment carries the names of the karigars who worked on it on a small card. That is unusual. It should not be.

In the atelier now

A bridal lehenga, eleven months in

Commissioned in November for a wedding the following October: a deep wine velvet lehenga with a kamdani ground, salma borders and a scatter of sitara that reads as frost under the lights. Four karigars, rotating because the eyes go first, at about fourteen hundred hours by the time the dupatta is finished.

The motif is a paisley the bride's grandmother wore in 1974, redrawn from a photograph that had faded to the colour of tea. Saba redrew it at four times the size, traced it onto butter paper, and pricked the outline with a needle so the chalk would pass through.
