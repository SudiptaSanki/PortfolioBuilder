Taught at eleven, trusted at twenty-five

IThe first needle

A workshop above a cloth shop

The atelier was founded by her grandfather, Nazir Ahmed, in 1948, in two rooms above a cloth merchant's shop in Chowk. He had come from Agra with a box of needles and a reputation for borders that did not pucker. The shop still has his brass measure, worn smooth at the thumb, and Saba still uses it to check the width of a salma border.

She was eleven when her mother put a scrap of velvet in her hands and told her to lay a straight line of salma. She laid it crooked. Her mother unpicked it with a needle and gave it back. That went on for a week. What she remembers is not the frustration but the lesson underneath it: the metal is soft enough to shape and too soft to forgive. You decide before the needle goes in.

“You cannot hurry a stitch. You can only decide it faster.”

IISeven years

The apprenticeship, and what it did not teach

Formal training lasted seven years, from 1990 to 1997, under her mother and two senior karigars. It covered the five stitches in the sampler on the front page, the drawing of motifs on butter paper, and the trick of pricking an outline so the chalk dust passes through it. It also covered cloth: which velvet takes metal, which silk refuses it, why organza needs a backing before anything can be laid on it.

What it did not cover was money. When she took over the workshop in 2004, she found that the karigars were paid by the piece at rates set in the 1990s, that two of them had left for a factory in Noida, and that the atelier's reputation was doing better than its accounts. Her first change was a rate card, printed and pinned to the wall. Her second was to put the names of the karigars on the card that goes out with every finished garment.

Both were unpopular for about a year. Both are now the reason the workshop still has twelve pairs of hands, four of them women who began as apprentices and stayed.

IIIThe work

What a zardozi piece is, physically

Zardozi is metal embroidery: silver wire, often gilded, drawn, flattened or coiled, then stitched onto a ground cloth with a needle and a thread that never shows. The five stitches have different jobs. Salma is the flattened strip that draws a line. Sitara is the sequin that catches light. Dabka is the coil that gives relief. Nakshi is the chain that fills a curve. Kora is the thin ribbon used for fine detail, and it is the hardest to lay without twisting.

A bridal lehenga is not one design but four: the skirt, the blouse, the dupatta and the border, each with its own density. The skirt takes the heaviest work because it is seen from a distance and moves; the blouse takes the finest because it is seen close and does not. The dupatta is where the motif is allowed to breathe.

  • Ground cloth is stretched on a wooden frame, not a hoop, for large pieces.
  • A second cloth is laid underneath so the metal does not tear the velvet.
  • The design is drawn in reverse on the back for pieces worked from the underside.
  • Nothing is glued. Ever. Not even the sequins.

VRestoration

Old metal, new thread

About a fifth of the atelier's work is restoration: nineteenth-century shawls, a 1930s sherwani, a velvet panel from a demolished haveli. The rule is the same as in a museum. Do not match the colour if the colour is gone; support what remains and make the repair visible on the reverse. Saba keeps a notebook of every restoration — the ground, the stitch, the metal, the date — because the next person to open the piece should not have to guess.

She will not restore a piece she cannot identify, and she will not "improve" one. Twice she has declined a commission because the owner wanted the metal replaced with something brighter. Both times the piece went elsewhere. Both times she wrote down why.
