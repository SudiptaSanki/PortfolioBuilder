Hanukkah · Dec 4–12, 2026 · The first candle is a Friday

Eight nights. One pan. No soggy latkes.

I'm Shani Weiss. In Crown Heights, Brooklyn, Hanukkah is not a garnish on December — it's eight nights of oil, and I fry all of them. Latkes to order from my small kitchen off Eastern Parkway: grated by hand, squeezed dry in a towel, fried at 190 degrees, and eaten while they still crackle.

A latke should shatter a little at the edge. If it bends, it steamed — and steaming is what happens to other people's latkes, in covered trays, in lukewarm oil. Mine go from pan to rack to table, and if you order them, they go from my pan to a hot box to your door inside the hour.

Technique

Why they stay crisp

Four rules. They are the whole difference between a latke and a potato pancake that apologises.

1

Grate coarse

The big holes of a box grater, or the shredding disc — never the fine side, never a blender. Long shreds are what lace together into edges that crisp. Mush makes pancakes; shreds make latkes.

2

Squeeze like you mean it

The grated mix goes into a clean tea towel and gets wrung over the sink until it stops dripping — then wrung again. Water is the enemy. In the pan, water becomes steam, and steam is how a latke goes soggy from the inside.

3

Oil at 190°C (375°F)

Hot enough that the edge seals on contact and the latke fries instead of drinking. I use a thermometer. A bread cube browns in the same oil at the wrong temperatures too often to be a witness.

4

Drain on a rack, never paper

Paper towels steam the underside you just crisped. A rack over a tray, a pinch of salt while they're glossy, and straight to the table — or into a 100°C oven, uncovered, if the crowd is still arriving.

From The Work

The third-night fry

The ask. A Park Slope synagogue, 150 people at its third-night party, wanted latkes served hot for two hours — not a chafing dish of them going soft under a lid.

What I did. Two cooks, four pans, and a runner between the kitchen and the hall. Classic and sweet potato in waves of two dozen; finished latkes held on racks in a low oven for minutes, never longer.

The outcome. 312 latkes across the night — twenty-six dozen — and the last tray crackled like the first.

I've chaired this party for six years. It's the first time the latkes were the thing people talked about on the way out.
— Rina Goldfarb, events chair, on the third-night fry
