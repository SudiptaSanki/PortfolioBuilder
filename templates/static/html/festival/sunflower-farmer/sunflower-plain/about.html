The almanac, month by month.

A hundred and forty acres of sunflower is really a year of small decisions. This is the order we make them in, and the month each one has to happen whether or not it is convenient.

My father bought this land in 1994 with a bank loan he was told he would not get, and planted sunflower because his father had planted sunflower, and because the oil mill in Chikkaballapur was buying that year at a price that made the loan look possible. The first crop failed. The second one did not, and the farm has been in sunflower ever since, with ragi in between because the ground will not do the same thing twice.

What follows is not the calendar from a seed company. It is the calendar we actually keep, including the fortnight in April when nothing happens and the three weeks in December when nobody sleeps.

The year

The farming year, month by month
JanuaryHarvest Plot C, then Plot D. Heads cut and dried on tarpaulins for four days before threshing. The press runs every Thursday from the middle of the month.
FebruaryPlot D is open to visitors until the 8th. Then the stalks are cut and the field is left to the cattle for a fortnight — the village herd, in exchange for a share of the cake.
MarchSoil test on all four blocks. The report decides how much of the year will be ragi. Nothing is planted.
AprilThe hottest month, and the quiet one. Fence repairs, the tank desilted, the press stripped and rebuilt by a man from Tumkur who does nothing else.
MayFirst rains, if they come. Horse gram sown as green manure on the block that will take sunflower next. Ploughing begins when the soil will turn without smearing.
JuneRagi on Plots A and C. This is the farm's food crop and its insurance; sunflower is the cash.
JulyWeeding, twice. The tank fills. Nothing else.
AugustThe green manure is turned in. Seed is bought from the university station at Hebbal, not from the shop in town, which sells last year's seed with this year's label.
SeptemberSow Plot A on the 12th, Plot B on the 26th. Ridges, 60 cm apart. Thinning at the two-leaf stage, which is the whole family on their knees for two days.
OctoberSow C on the 9th and D on the 24th. Hives arrive at the edge of B. This is the month the rain decides the year.
NovemberPlot A in flower. Parakeets arrive with it. Someone walks the rows at dawn every day, which is also when visitors are allowed in, so the two jobs are the same job.
DecemberB and C flower. Heads turn down and the backs go yellow, which is when we stop watering and start watching the sky. Harvest begins on the 19th, weather permitting.

Water, honestly

Sunflower is sold as a dryland crop and it is, up to a point. We have a borewell and a tank that holds four days of irrigation, and we use both at two moments only: at sowing, to get the seed up, and at flowering, when the plant is making the seed it will be judged on. Everything in between is rain. In a year when the October rain fails, the yield drops by a third and no amount of water from the borewell brings it back, because the borewell cannot cover forty acres.

Drip was quoted at ₹68,000 an acre in 2022. We did not take it. On this soil, with this crop in rotation, the arithmetic did not work, and I would rather say that than pretend we are more modern than we are.

The press

The press is a cold screw press, Indian-made, bought second-hand in 2011 from a mill in Kolar that had closed. It takes 40 kg of seed an hour and gives about 15 litres of oil, and the cake that comes out of the other end goes to the cattle and to a soap maker in Bengaluru. Cold pressing leaves more of the lecithin and the smell in the oil, which is why ours is darker than the refined oil in the shop and why it goes cloudy in the cold months. That is not a fault.

We do not filter it beyond a cloth. Sediment settles in the bottle over three weeks; pour carefully, or shake it, and nobody has ever complained twice.
