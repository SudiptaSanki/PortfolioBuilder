One hundred and forty acres of flowers that all face the same way.

We grow sunflower for oil and for seed on the red soil north of Nandi Hills, in four plots that rotate through ragi and cowpea so the ground does not tire. The harvest goes to a small press in the yard, and the oil is sold in bottles from the same table we use for paperwork.

The plain, plot by plot

Sunflower takes ninety to a hundred days and does not like to be hurried. We sow four blocks ten days apart so that the press is not asked to handle forty acres in one week, and so that if a hailstorm takes one block, it does not take the year.

Why four sowings

A single sowing means one week of flowering and one week of combining, and a fortnight of work for eight people in a year. Spreading it out also spreads the risk: the October block is the one that usually gets the rain, and the September block is the one that usually does not need it.

What follows sunflower

Ragi on A and C, cowpea on B, and a bare fallow on D with a green manure of horse gram. Sunflower takes a lot out of the topsoil and the roots do not hold the red earth the way ragi does, so the second year is always a soil year.

The problem with visitors

People walk into a field of flowers and pick one to photograph, and a picked head is a lost head — the plant will not make seed from it. The February block is planted for visitors, and we ask that you stay on the cut paths. The paths exist so that you can.

The season, as a strip

Six days to come up, thirty-five to the bud, sixty to the first open flower, ninety-five to the combine. The flowering week is the one that decides the year.

What goes wrong, and what we do about it

Sunflower is a crop that looks after itself for about six weeks and then asks for everything at once. The head is heavy, the stem is not, and a plant that has been fed too much nitrogen will fall over in the first rain of December with a head full of seed and nowhere to put it.

We sow on ridges rather than flat, which costs a day of tractor time and saves the block in a wet October. We thin to one plant every thirty centimetres at the two-leaf stage, which nobody enjoys and everybody does, because two plants in a hill make two small heads instead of one good one. And we keep two hives at the edge of Plot B, borrowed from a beekeeper in Doddaballapur, because a sunflower that is not visited sets about forty per cent less seed.

Birds are the other problem. A flock of parakeets can take the edge off a block in a morning, and the only thing that works is a person walking the rows at dawn, which is why the field is open for walks between six and ten and why the walks stop before the seed sets.

Come and stand in it

Plot D flowers from the last week of December into the first week of January, and for those two weeks the gate is open from six in the morning until ten. There is no entry fee and no ticket; there is a box for the school in Chikkaballapur that we support, and you may put something in it or not.

Where — Nandi Road, four kilometres past the Nandi Hills turn, on the left after the tank. Look for the yellow gate.

When — 26 December to 8 January, 6 am to 10 am. The light is best before eight and the field is busiest at nine.

Bring — shoes you do not mind ruining. The red soil does not come out.
