Mapusa market, west wall

The vegetable market's west wall, 18.4 metres of cement render patched four times over thirty years. The register is a river of fish and baskets because that is what the market sells, and the palette comes from the laterite under the plaster rather than from a book.

Painted in three sections of six metres, each finished in a day, with the lime mixed at 6 a.m. in the shade of the loading bay. The fishmonger whose stall faces the wall asked for one fish with a spot on its tail; it is at 4.2 metres, near the drain.

Government primary school, Porvorim

A corridor wall 22 metres long, painted in the summer holidays over two years. The upper band is fresco: birds, a sky and the hill behind the school. The lower band is secco, because it is at the height of a seven-year-old's hand, and it is repainted every second year with a pot of the same red ochre.

The headteacher asked for the alphabet. We compromised: the letters are cut into the wings of the birds, small enough to find only if you look. The children found them all in a week.

Laterite compound, Aldona

A private compound wall on the road to the ferry, 9 metres of exposed laterite with the red still coming through. The design is kash grass and a low sun, drawn to sit in the stone's own colour rather than cover it.

This wall needed four coats before the lime stopped disappearing into the stone. It was the first time I mixed pigment into the first coat as well as the last, which turned out to be the right answer and is now what I do on all laterite.

Ferry terminal, Panjim

The waiting hall wall at the Panjim ferry terminal, painted in secco because the hall is air-conditioned and the wall is dry for most of the year. The design is the river at four o'clock: boats, nets, and the light on the water, drawn from the view through the ticket window.

Two hundred people a day wait in front of this wall. It is the only commission where the design was chosen by a committee, and the only one where I was glad of it: the ferry crews asked for the boats to face the right way, and they were right.
