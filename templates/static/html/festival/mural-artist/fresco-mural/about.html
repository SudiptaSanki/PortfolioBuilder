A mural is finished when the street starts using it.

I work in lime because it is the material the walls here are already made of. The practice is small: one painter, one assistant, a van, and a workshop that smells of slaked lime and wet sand.

Practice

Panjim, since 2011

I studied painting at the Goa College of Art, where I was taught to work on canvas and not to touch walls. The first wall I painted was a school corridor in Porvorim in 2011, in acrylic, because that is what I could buy. It peeled in two monsoons. The second one I painted in lime, because the caretaker told me that is what the building was made of, and it is still there.

Since then the practice has been an argument with the weather. Goa is hot, humid for four months, and then wet for four more. Lime is the only material I have found that moves with a wall instead of sitting on top of it, and it is the only one that lets me paint into the surface rather than onto it.

What I actually do

Most commissions are between 8 and 25 square metres, on a wall that belongs to a school, a market, a community hall or a family compound. I do not take gallery work, and I do not print designs onto vinyl and call it a mural. The drawing is made for the wall, at the wall, with the people who use it.

The process is slower than a painted panel and cheaper than people expect, because lime and sand are cheap and the expensive part is time. A 24-square-metre wall takes about five days on the wall, plus two days of drawing and a day of preparation. The wall then needs three weeks of curing before anyone touches it.

Materials, honestly

Lime putty
Slaked from quicklime in the workshop, aged at least three months. Bought from a supplier in Kundaim; never from a hardware shop's bagged "lime".
Sand
River sand, sieved to 2 mm, washed until the water runs clear. Sharp sand from the coast is wrong: it brings salt into the wall.
Pigment
Earth pigments from Mapusa: yellow ochre, red ochre, burnt umber, terracotta. Indigo is the one synthetic colour I use, and I say so on the wall notes.
Brushes
Lime brushes, hog hair, cut short. A 100 mm brush does most of the work; the rest is a sash brush and a spray bottle of water.
Scaffold
Hired bamboo, tied with coir. Steel scaffold marks the wall and costs more; bamboo is what the plasterers here use and it goes up in an hour.
Sealer
None. A fresco that needs a sealer was not painted as a fresco. If the wall is exposed, I change the design and the season, not the chemistry.

Working with the people who use the wall

Every commission starts with two afternoons on site without a sketchbook. The first is for measuring and photographing; the second is for talking to whoever is there: the caretaker, the shopkeeper opposite, the children who will use the wall as a backrest. Their answers change the design more than any reference image.

At the school in Porvorim the children asked for a fish that could be touched, at their height. That became a band of fish at 1.1 metres, painted in secco over the fresco because it would be touched and would need repainting. I go back every second year with a pot of the same colour.

What I will not do

No acrylic on exterior walls. No mural on a wall that has not been checked for damp. No design that requires the wall to stay clean: a public wall will be leaned on, sat against and written on, and the design should survive that or invite it.

I also will not start an exterior fresco after the first week of April. The monsoon does not negotiate, and I have watched a good wall come off in sheets because someone wanted to finish before a festival.

Rates

₹3,600 to ₹6,200 per square metre depending on surface and finish, including lime, pigment and labour. Scaffold and travel extra. Community walls and schools are quoted at cost plus a fee we agree before the first coat.

Season

Interior walls all year. Exterior lime between November and April. The monsoon is for drawing, teaching and repainting the school fish.

The caretaker at Porvorim told me the wall was made of lime and sand and that anything else would fall off. He was right, and it took me a ruined corridor to believe him.

Teaching

Two workshops a year

Teaching, and why it is part of the practice

Twice a year I run a three-day lime workshop at the studio: one day of plaster, one of drawing and pouncing, one of pigment. Six people, no more, because there is only one wall.

Half the people who come are architecture students who want to know how the material behaves; half are painters who have only worked on canvas. Both groups are surprised by the same thing: how fast lime goes off, and how much of the work is preparation rather than paint.

I also teach a short unit at the Goa College of Art every January on wall preparation and pigment, which is unpaid and the most useful week of my year.
