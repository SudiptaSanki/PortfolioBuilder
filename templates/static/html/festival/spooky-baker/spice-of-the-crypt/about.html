Counter / The baker

My grandmother baked for the dead and the living, and never wrote anything down.

Everything I know about weighing, resting, and not opening the oven door came from standing next to her and being told to wait. The book came later, because I kept forgetting.

East Oakland, and a kitchen with one window

My grandmother came from Usulután in 1979 and spent thirty years in a kitchen on 23rd Avenue with one window and a stove that leaned to the left. She made pupusas for money and pan dulce for us, and she kept the two kinds of baking entirely separate in her head: one was work, one was a language she spoke to the family.

The pan dulce changed with the season without anyone deciding it should. In November there were bones made of shortbread and a dark cake with burnt sugar; in December, a sweet bread with anise. She never called any of it Halloween. She called it “the things for the cold”.

When she died in 2014, the recipes went with her, which is the ordinary tragedy of families like mine. I had watched her for thirty years and could reproduce maybe four things. The rest I have spent a decade reconstructing badly and then better.

“She measured with a cup and a hand. I weigh everything, because I am trying to write down what she would not.” Notebook, 2018

Pastry school, and other people’s cakes

I went to a pastry programme in San Francisco in 2015 because I wanted to know why bread rises and cake does not, in that order. It was useful and it was not the education I needed. The useful part was the discipline: mise en place, temperatures, the refusal to guess. The useless part was the assumption that everything should look like a display.

After school I worked three years in restaurant pastry, which taught me speed and ruined my wrists for a while. I made a lot of plates with a lot of components and almost none of it was food I wanted to eat on a Tuesday. The turning point was a funeral: a colleague asked me to bake for her father’s wake, and I made a tray of shortbread bones because it was the only thing that felt honest. She cried at the table. I quit the following month.

The building

Crypt Bakery is on Telegraph Avenue in a building that was a mortuary from 1928 until 1985. The landlord kept the original sign in the basement, a wooden board with CRYPT painted on it in gold, and when I asked about the name for the bakery he said, “You may as well use it. The neighbours already do.”

I have kept the sign and the building’s cold room, which is now the proofing room and is the best thing about the lease. The oven is a 1948 double-deck gas model that takes ninety minutes to come up and holds heat like a stone. It is not efficient and it bakes better than anything I could afford new.

Why the book is in grams

Every recipe in the ledger is written in grams, with baker’s percentages beside it, because cups are a way of pretending that flour is always the same. It is not. A cup of flour can weigh 110 grams or 160 depending on the day, the mill, and how the bag was handled. When you bake the same thing four days a week, that difference is the whole problem.

The percentages also let me scale without thinking. On the home page the ledger does it for you; in the book it is a column on every page, and the columns are the reason I can train someone in a month instead of a year.

What I will not do

I will not use fondant. I will not put a colour in a cake that does not come from food. I will not make a dessert that is only a photograph, and I will not sell a tray for a wake at a wedding price. If someone asks for a cake that looks like a handbag, I will say no and mean it, and then I will suggest a cake that tastes like something.

I will bake for a funeral at cost if the family is local and the notice is short. That is not charity; it is the reason the shop exists, and it is the only part of the business I would not change if I were trying to make more money.

A NOTE ON THE NAME

The crypt is not a gimmick

People assume the name is a Halloween joke. It is a building, a landlord, and a sign that was already in the basement.

The mortuary closed two years before I was born. The cold room kept its tiles; the front room kept the light. I have not tried to make the shop spooky, because the building is already itself, and fake cobwebs would be an insult to a room that has genuinely held what this one has held.

The bones are shortbread because my grandmother made them. The dark cake is a chocolate cake because she made one. The name is the only part of this that is a joke, and it is the landlord’s joke, not mine. I kept it because it is on the building and because it makes people walk in.
