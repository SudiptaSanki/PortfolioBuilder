The practice

A classical form, used carefully, in an unclassical room.

Sharada Menon trained at Kalakshetra, performed for eleven years, and left the stage in 2014 to work with bodies that were not performing for anyone. She is a natya therapy coach, not a dance teacher and not a psychotherapist.

I performed my arangetram in 2004, in a hall in Mylapore with a bad air conditioner and my grandmother in the second row. For the next ten years I did what dancers do: toured, taught, injured myself, recovered, and slowly noticed that the most interesting part of the work was not the audience.

It was the students who cried in the corridor after class. It was the woman in a workshop who could not lift her arm above shoulder height and could not say why. It was my own body, which held my father's death in the right hip for two years before I noticed.

Bharatanatyam has a technical vocabulary for emotion that is more precise than most psychology textbooks. The Natyashastra lists nine rasas and the physical means of producing each one — the glance, the eyebrow, the set of the neck, the hand. A performer uses them to make an audience feel something. A therapist can use the same grammar in reverse: begin with the body's position and let the feeling that belongs to it arrive in its own time.

“A dancer asks: how do I make them feel this? A therapist asks: what is this body already feeling, and what would let it move?”

I do not teach Bharatanatyam in sessions. I borrow its alphabet. There is no repertoire, no item, no costume, and nobody is corrected for looking wrong.

What a first session is like

We sit on the floor, usually with tea, and talk for fifteen minutes. I ask what has been happening, what the body has been doing with it, and who else is helping — a psychiatrist, a counsellor, a physiotherapist. If the answer is nobody, and the situation sounds like it needs one, I say that before we begin.

Then we stand or sit, depending on what the body can do, and start with breath. Nothing happens until the exhale is longer than the inhale. From there I offer one gesture, then a short sequence built around the rasa we have chosen together. The person is never asked to perform it well. The instruction is always about attention, not form.

We finish with the gesture held for three breaths and released, and a minute of silence. People often cry in that minute, and that is not the goal — it is simply what happens when a body stops working to hold something in.

The ethics of borrowing a sacred form

Natya therapy is not mine to invent. It exists in Indian clinical practice in several forms, and I came to it through the drama therapy programme at HCU rather than through any proprietary training. I am careful about three things.

  • No performance. Sessions are closed. Nothing is shown, filmed or shared, and I do not use a client's material in teaching without written consent.
  • No spiritual claim. I do not present rasa work as treatment for disease, and I do not use the language of energy or chakras with clients who have come for something practical.
  • No borrowing without credit. The form belongs to a lineage of teachers and practitioners. I name my teachers, I pay for supervision, and I do not sell a certification.

Who it is for

Most of my work is with adolescents and with people in caring professions: school counsellors, nurses, special educators, dance teachers who have become unofficial therapists for their students. The common thread is not a diagnosis. It is that talk has stopped being useful, and the body is still carrying the conversation.

It is not suitable for someone in an acute crisis, someone who is actively psychotic, or someone who cannot tolerate being in a closed room with another person. Those are referrals, not failures, and I keep a list of people who are better at them than I am.

Questions I am asked in the first ten minutes

“Do I need to know dance?”
No. Nothing in a session requires training, flexibility or rhythm. If you can lift an arm and breathe out slowly, you can do this.
“Will I have to talk about my childhood?”
Not unless you want to. The work is mostly non-verbal. I will ask enough to be safe, and you can decline any question.
“Is this religious?”
No. The vocabulary comes from a temple tradition, but sessions are secular and no prayer, mantra or ritual is used unless you ask for it.
“Can I come with my therapist?”
Yes, with their agreement. I prefer to speak with them before the first session so the work is coordinated rather than parallel.
“What about touch?”
I do not touch a client in a first session. Later, if a gesture needs support, I ask each time and describe exactly where. A no is always accepted without discussion.
“How many sessions?”
Six is the usual block, with a review at the fourth. Some people come for a season; some come for two years. There is no package designed to keep you.
