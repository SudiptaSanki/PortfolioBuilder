About the leader

FORTY YEARS
IN THE ROWS.

I joined this choir at seventeen because my father stood in the low voices and the house rule was simple: in this family you sing. I stood beside him for nine years, learning the bass row's patience, before the old leader, Wendel Inauen, moved me up to the lead line and told me the truth I still teach: the yodel is not the loud part. The yodel is the honest part — the place where the voice changes its nature in front of witnesses, and you cannot fake it at any volume.

I have led the Echo vom Säntis for nineteen seasons. In that time the choir has been classified in the jury's third class and its first, buried four singers and baptised six of their grandchildren, and sung the same Naturjodel at all of it. That is what a village choir is: not a concert machine, a memory with harmony.

People from outside ask me to "do the funny voice" — at weddings, at the brewery, once at a petrol station. I always refuse, and I am not being precious. A party-trick yodel is a break done as a joke, and a break done as a joke teaches the throat that the flip is clowning. In my choir the flip is a tool: it is how a voice throws a melody across a room the way our grandfathers threw it across a valley, to say I am here, the herd is here, come home. You do not make a joke of a signal fire.

The other thing I am known for: no microphones in our hall. People assume it is nostalgia. It is arithmetic. A wooden hall of our size returns the choir's sound to the singers in time for them to tune to it; a loudspeaker returns it late, loud and flat, and the row starts singing to the boxes instead of to each other. In a wooden hall, a microphone is a confession that the room was built wrong — or the choir too quiet. Our room was built right. So we sing to the beams.

The craft, part one

THE BREAK,
TAUGHT ON PURPOSE.

Every voice has two registers. The chest voice is the speaking voice — heavy, low, the one you argue with. The head voice is the calling voice — light, high, the one that carries over a meadow. Between them is a seam. Most singers spend their lives smoothing that seam away. A yodeller does the opposite: we find the seam, we mark it, and we learn to step over it so cleanly and so fast that the step itself becomes the music. That step is the break, and it is a release, not a push — the throat lets go of the heavy register and the light one catches the note. Singers who push get a crack; singers who release get a yodel.

How I teach it, in order

First, the two notes. We find your chest E and your head E — the bench on the front page plays the pair I start from — and you sing them apart, slowly, until each one is steady on its own. Second, the siren: one breath, sliding up through the seam without stopping, so your ear learns where your own crack lives. Everybody's seam is in a slightly different place; a teacher who gives the whole row the same pitch is teaching his voice, not yours. Third, the placed flip: the slide becomes a step — chest note, head note, nothing between — first on "yo", then on the yodel syllables the old songs use. Fourth, the flip at tempo, inside a chord: because a break that only works alone in a kitchen is a parlour skill, and we are building a festival singer.

It cracks a hundred times before it lands. That is not failure; that is the tuition. The singers who stay are the ones who can laugh at crack number forty and sing number forty-one anyway.

The craft, part two

HOW A REHEARSAL
IS BUILT.

Tuesdays — the rows apart

The first hour, each row rehearses in its own corner with its row leader: new notes, hard intervals, the diction of the verses. The lead line works on the flips inside the festival Naturjodel; the second voices tune their thirds against a single lead voice, over and over, until the interval rings. The second hour we assemble — and a passage only counts as learned when it survives assembly.

Fridays — the whole choir

Full runs, standing in festival formation, in the order of the set. Friday is where stamina is built: four pieces back to back, then again. In the last six weeks before a festival, Friday ends with one full adjudication run — doors closed, no stopping, no apology mid-piece. What breaks in the run gets Tuesday's first hour.

What we sing

Naturjodel — the wordless form, the oldest: pure melody and break, handed down by ear, belonging to a place the way a dialect does. Jodellied — a song with verses, the choir singing the words and the lead line answering with the yodel refrain. Jodlermesse — the mass set to yodel composition, sung from the gallery; we sing one every St. Martin's Day, and it is the date the whole village plans around. A festival set is two of the first and two of the second — wordless and worded, so the jury hears both crafts.

A new singer's first season

You audition by ear — sing back my phrases, hold a harmony against my voice — then stand two trial evenings in the row you would join. If we take each other, you get a buddy in your row, the folder, and one job: your row's line, nothing else, until it is in your bones. Most new singers sing their first festival inside their first year. The season planner on the front page shows the honest arithmetic of that.

The craft, part three

HOW THE JURY
MARKS A CHOIR.

At a Jodlerfest — cantonal or federal — every choir sings its set in front of a jury of trained adjudicators, in a hall full of other choirs who know exactly what they are hearing. There is no audience easier to fool and none harder. The jury writes a report and marks the performance under five heads:

MarkWhat the jury is listening for
Sound cultureA unified, natural tone — one choir, not thirty-four soloists; vowels matched across the rows.
IntonationPurity of the intervals, above all the thirds and sixths between the lead line and the harmony rows.
RhythmTempo held together through the flips; no row dragging, no row running away downhill.
DynamicsThe marked louds and quiets actually happening — a Naturjodel that is all one volume is a Naturjodel half sung.
ExpressionWhether the performance says something: the overall impression, the honesty of it.

The marks resolve into a classification from Klasse 1 to Klasse 4 — Klasse 1 the highest, printed on a certificate and read out in front of the festival. There is no prize money and no league table. There is only the class, the written report, and the drive home. Choirs have wept over both ends of that scale, and both kinds of tears were about the same thing: whether the work showed.
