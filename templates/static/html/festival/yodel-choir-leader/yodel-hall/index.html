I The forms three layers, one breath

What a yodel actually is

A yodel is not a shout with decoration. It is a melody that lives in the seam of the voice: a phrase begins in chest voice, leaps, and continues in the thin head voice without a breath between them. The leap is the signature. Everything else — the syllable, the vowel, the room — is built around it. In the Bernese Oberland the old form has no words at all, only syllables that keep the throat open: ho-la-ri-di, ju-zä, di-ri-di-a.

01

Naturjodel

Wordless, three or four voices, handed down by ear rather than written. The first voice sets the call; the others answer in parallel thirds and sixths, never quite the same way twice. This is the core of the choir's book.

Listen for: the fifth leap, then the answering third

02

Jodellied

A strophic song with real words in the verses and a yodel refrain. Written for village evenings and harvest Sundays, it lets a room sing along on the verse and go quiet for the refrain, which is the point.

Listen for: the refrain arriving a fourth higher than expected

03

Juchzer

One voice, one long arc, thrown across a valley to say that someone is up there and all is well. A good Juchzer carries about a kilometre on a still morning. Ours are timed at rehearsal with a stopwatch, for fun and for lungs.

Listen for: the held top note before the fall

IV The leader Maria Bärtschi

Taught by her grandmother, then by the conservatory

Maria Bärtschi grew up in Grindelwald in a house where the evening ended with a yodel and not a song. She learned the Naturjodel from her grandmother Rosa, who had learned it from a hired hand on a neighbouring Alp, and only later spent four years at the conservatory in Bern learning why the old women never seemed to breathe.

She took over Alpenklang in 2002, four years after her grandmother founded it with five neighbours. In that time the choir has stayed at twenty-two singers on purpose — enough for four parts, few enough that everyone can hear the person beside them. She teaches the break as a physical event first and a musical one second: where the weight leaves the chest, how much air the leap costs, why the vowel must not close.

“The high note is not louder. If you have to push it, you have already left the yodel and started shouting.”
