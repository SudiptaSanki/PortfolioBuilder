About Maria Bärtschi b. 1984, Grindelwald
leading since 2002

A voice taught first by weather, then by a conservatory

Maria Bärtschi has spent twenty-four years teaching people to sing a melody that has no words, in a room where the heating clicks and the old schoolhouse floor takes the bass like a drum.

I The house

Evenings that ended in a yodel

In the house above Grindelwald where Maria grew up, the day ended with the dishes, then the stove, then a yodel. Her grandmother Rosa had learned the Naturjodel from a hired hand on a neighbouring Alp in the 1950s — a man whose name nobody in the family can now agree on, but whose version of the Zäuerli survives intact in three voices. Rosa sang the middle part. Maria, aged seven, was given the top one because she could reach it without shouting.

That is how the old repertoire travels: not in a book, but from one throat to another, with the errors kept as variations. Maria can still sing the four settings she learned before she could read music. She teaches them the same way — by ear first, notation much later, and only for the singers who ask.

The valley contributed the rest. A yodel learned indoors is a smaller thing than a yodel learned on a path above the tree line, where a phrase thrown at a rock face comes back a half-second late. She still takes new singers up the Schynige Platte path in June, before the season starts, to sing badly at a mountain and hear what it does with the sound.

II The training

Four years in Bern, learning why the old women never breathed

At nineteen she auditioned for the conservatory in Bern with a Jodellied and a Schubert song, and was told — kindly — that she had a folk voice and would have to decide what to do with it. She spent four years there on voice pedagogy and choral conducting, and came out with a technical answer to a question she had carried since childhood: the break is not a trick of the throat. It is a change of register, and the reason the old singers made it sound seamless is that they never let the vowel close as they crossed it.

She also learned to distrust one piece of conservatory advice in particular: that the high note must be supported by more air. In a yodel, more air is exactly what breaks the phrase. The leap is crossed quietly, with the ribs staying where they are, and the note arrives rather than being pushed. She spends most of a beginner's first month on that single correction.

Teaching note, pinned above the piano Chest voice is weight. Head voice is length. The break is where you stop carrying and start floating — and the vowel must stay open through it, or the room hears a door close.

Since 2004 she has also taught at the Oberland Yodel Circle's winter course, a week of morning sessions in Meiringen where twenty singers from six villages learn one Naturjodel thoroughly enough to sing it in four parts without a conductor. The course is deliberately slow. One setting a week is a good week.

III The choir

Twenty-two singers, and no wish for a twenty-third

Alpenklang began in 1998 with nine singers in a kitchen in Interlaken and a standing arrangement that nobody would be auditioned out. Maria took it over in 2002 at eighteen. Twenty-four years later it is twenty-two strong, with a waiting list for the alto line and a permanent shortage of tenors that Maria describes as a law of nature. The choir has stayed small on purpose: in a Naturjodel, each voice has to hear the one beside it and adjust by a few cents. Above twenty-five singers, the tuning goes soft and the room starts to hear a crowd instead of a chord.

Rehearsals are Tuesdays at 19:30 in the old schoolhouse on Kirchgasse, two hours with a break for coffee and argument. The first hour is breath and the break; the second is repertoire. Concerts are few — six or seven a year — and the choir turns down more than it accepts, particularly in summer, when half the altos are on an Alp somewhere and the tenors are needed at home.

IV Chronology

Twenty-four years, in order

  • 1984
    Born in Grindelwald

    Third child of a carpenter and a schoolteacher. The house had a piano that was never quite in tune and a grandmother who preferred not to use it.

  • 1991
    First top part

    Sings the soprano line of Rosa's Zäuerli at a village evening, aged seven, standing on a chair so the room could see her.

  • 1998
    Alpenklang founded

    Rosa Bärtschi and five neighbours, nine singers in a kitchen, and a decision to keep the group small and the repertoire wordless wherever possible.

  • 2002
    Takes over the choir

    Aged eighteen, handed the folder of handwritten first lines. Her first decision is to keep the Tuesday rehearsal and to add a coffee break long enough for argument.

  • 2003
    Conservatory, Bern

    Four years of voice pedagogy and choral conducting. Learns that the break can be taught, and that most of what she knows about it cannot be written down.

  • 2004
    Winter course, Meiringen

    Begins teaching the annual week of Naturjodel for singers from across the Oberland. Still runs it, still one setting per week.

  • 2016
    The Brienz recording

    Eleven settings recorded in a wooden boathouse with two microphones and no edits, because the mistakes were part of the point.

  • 2024
    Twenty-two voices

    The choir reaches its self-imposed ceiling. A waiting list opens for altos; tenors are still invited to walk in on a Tuesday.

V What she teaches

Three things, in this order

Private lessons run on Wednesday afternoons in the same schoolhouse, and they almost always begin with the same three exercises, whatever the singer has come for.

  • The break, without a note. A slide from a speaking ah to a calling oo and back, ten times, listening for the seam. No pitch, no piano, no audience.
  • The vowel that stays open. The syllable ho held across the leap, then la, then ri. Most singers close on ri; that is where the phrase dies.
  • The phrase, in one breath. Eight notes, no gap. If it needs a breath it is not ready, and the answer is a smaller phrase rather than a bigger lung.

Lessons are forty-five minutes, in German or English. Beginners are welcome and are not asked to sing alone in the first session. The choir's open rehearsal on the first Tuesday of the month is the better place to start if you would rather listen first.
