The story, briefly

I came to this trade from the wrong side of the camera. For six seasons I worked as an assistant on a display company's firing crew — wiring, loading, standing in the rain at 23:40 waiting for a decision about wind. The crew reads a show the way musicians read a score: segments, cues, shell classes, timings. Nobody on a firing crew talks about "capturing the magic". They talk about whether segment four fired to time.

In 2016 I brought a camera to a show for the first time and discovered that everything the crew knew was exactly what display photography was missing. The pictures improved the moment I stopped watching the sky and started reading the schedule. Since 2019 this has been the whole practice: New Year and festival displays, shot from the firing schedule outwards, for the cities and companies that fire them.

It is a narrow trade and I keep it narrow on purpose. One pair of hands, a small array of remote cameras, and a rule that has never been bent: no schedule, no booking.

How a booking runs

StepWhat happensWhat you receive
1 · Schedule firstThe firing schedule — or the display company's written segment outline — is requested before any date is confirmed. From it, the frame plan is built on the printed cadence tables.The plan: positions, segments, expected frame counts, with the arithmetic shown
2 · SurveyPositions are walked in daylight. Foreground, sightlines and the exclusion zone are marked. Any position inside the zone is agreed with the display company in writing and worked only under its stated arrangements — escorted in, escorted out.The survey note and the agreed position list
3 · The nightRemotes are placed and set before the zone closes. The manned position shoots the plan segment by segment, and the frame log is kept against the schedule as the show fires — misfires and smoke included, in real time.Coverage of every segment the night allows
4 · Log & deliveryThe frame log goes to the client within 24 hours, unedited — including the frames that failed and the segments the weather shortened. The edited set follows within five working days.Frame log in 24 h · edited set in 5 working days

The weather call is never mine. The display company fires the show or cancels it; my job is to be ready for either answer and to have the terms agreed before the question is asked. Those terms are printed on the index page and applied exactly as written — File 03 is the proof, not the promise.

You cannot ask a firework to do it again

Every other kind of photography gets a second chance. A portrait can be re-lit; a product can be re-shot; a building will still be there tomorrow. A firework burns for four seconds, once, in front of a hundred thousand people, and then it is a story people tell. The whole trade is preparation for unrepeatable seconds — and honesty about the ones that got away.

So the rules are these. Misfires are reported, in the log, as failures — a client whose shells didn't fire deserves to hear it from the frame log, not discover it in the edit. Shortened shows are logged as shortened; I will not pad a seventeen-minute archive with frames from another night. Frames are never composited across shows — a sky built from three different displays is a fiction, and this practice sells records, not fictions. And the crowd's safety distance is not a photo opportunity: if a position isn't available under the display company's arrangements, it isn't available.

The opinion underneath all of it, since you ask: most display photography is luck with a long lens. The schedule is what turns it into a record.
