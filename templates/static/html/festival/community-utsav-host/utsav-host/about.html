About

Fourteen years, sixty-two utsavs, three colonies

I am not an event company. I am the neighbour who keeps the sheet, counts the modak and stands at the gate when the sound has to come down.

It began with a borrowed microphone

In 2011 my grandmother’s committee in Kukatpally had a microphone with a broken lead and no one who wanted to hold it. I held it. The Vinayaka Chavithi that year ran forty minutes late because the bhajan group and the kolatam children had both been told 19:00, and I spent the next week apologising to two aunties who were not speaking to each other.

The next year I wrote the running order on the back of a electricity bill and pinned it to the noticeboard. That sheet is the whole job, more or less. Everything I have learned since is about what to put on it: the quantities, the permissions, the two things that go wrong in every venue and the one thing that goes wrong in this one.

I still host in the same three colonies — Sri Laxmi Nivas, Sai Enclave and Bhagyanagar — and I take four to six utsavs a year beyond them. I do not run weddings. A wedding wants a stage; an utsav wants a street that is still a street when the lamps go off.

How I work

Six things that happen before the pandal

  1. 01

    One meeting, with the treasurer in the room

    Not four WhatsApp threads. Date, budget ceiling, who signs the police form. If the treasurer is not there, the meeting is rescheduled rather than repeated.

  2. 02

    A site visit at the same hour as the event

    The courtyard at 18:30 is a different room from the courtyard at noon: the gate light, the neighbour’s generator, where the queue will actually form. I photograph four corners and the meter box.

  3. 03

    One sheet, printed twice

    One at the gate, one with the sound operator. Every volunteer’s role is a line with a time on it. The cultural block has a hard stop in writing, because that is the only thing that keeps the aarti before ten.

  4. 04

    The quantities before the decorations

    Prasadam, water and chairs are settled first, from the footfall the colony actually had last year plus a fifth. Decorations can shrink on the day. Prasadam cannot.

  5. 05

    One sound operator, measured at the wall

    The meter reads at the boundary, not at the mixer. The number is written on tape on the fader. When the aarti ends the fader comes down, whoever is singing.

  6. 06

    The sweep is booked in advance

    Two sweepers and a skip, confirmed the week before. Flowers go in separate bags to the GHMC compost point. The lane is washed by 23:00 so the milk van at 5:30 has a clean road.

Where the money went

A 250-person Vinayaka Chavithi in a society courtyard, ₹84,000 collected from 96 households at ₹900 each. This is the split I put on the noticeboard on the eleventh day, with the receipts in a shoebox under the treasurer’s table.

  • Pandal and decoration34%
  • Prasadam and kitchen22%
  • Sound and lights18%
  • Cultural and prizes12%
  • Sweep, water, waste8%
  • Contingency6%

The contingency was ₹5,040 and ₹4,200 of it went on a second sound lead and forty extra chairs. That is a normal eleventh day.

What I will not do

  • Run the sound past 22:00. Not for a visiting singer, not for the last bhajan. The permission is the colony’s, and one complaint puts the next year’s permission at risk.
  • Collect money in cash myself. The treasurer collects, the ledger is read out, and I never hold a note. It is the only way to be able to tell a vendor no.
  • Promise a crowd number I have not seen. If the form says 400 and the courtyard has held 260, I plan for 260 and say so in writing.
  • Use plastic-coated prasadam covers. Leaf cups and a paper bag, or the prasadam goes out on a banana leaf. The colony pays for the difference in the sweep line.
  • Host a festival in a lane with one exit and a transformer. I will help find another ground. Two of the three colonies I work in have found one.
