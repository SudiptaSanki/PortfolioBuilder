The stitch · from the wrong side

You see the pattern on the front. I never do.

Phulkari is darned from the back of the cloth. The needle goes down and comes up, and the floss accumulates on the other side in a way you cannot see until the cloth is turned.

Ten centimetres, then another ten

I work sitting on the floor with the cloth over my knees and a cushion under it, the way my grandmother sat. A palm's width at a time, no frame, no hoop. The cloth is turned over every few minutes to check the count, and turned back before the next stitch. The back is where the work happens; the front is the surprise.

The stitch is a simple darning stitch: in front, out behind, a little longer on the return. Its length decides the sheen. Too short and the silk looks like wool; too long and the cloth puckers at the edges and never lies flat again.

The back of a bagh is a field of loose silk threads, almost ugly. The front is the garden. Only the grandmother knows both.

The floss is silk, called pat, bought on spools from a man in Amritsar whose family has sold it for three generations. The khaddar is hand-spun cotton, woven in the village; it has a slight irregularity that machine cloth cannot imitate and the needle needs.

Seven steps, one winter

The sequence has not changed. What changes is the time it takes, and whether there is a wedding at the end of it.

  1. 01

    Choose the khaddar

    Two or three widths joined by hand, selvedge to selvedge. The join runs under the centre motif, where it will never be seen.

  2. 02

    Draw the grid

    A single thread pulled in both directions to mark the centre, then the field divided by eye. No pattern is drawn on the cloth; it is counted.

  3. 03

    Stitch the border

    Two or three bands in mustard and magenta, worked from the corner inward. The border takes a month and sets the size of everything inside it.

  4. 04

    Place the motifs

    Chand-tara at the centre, mor at the base, kikar in the corners. Each motif has a place it has always had; I move them only when the family asks.

  5. 05

    Fill the ground

    The slow part. Between the motifs the cloth is filled with a fine darning in the same colour, so the ground disappears. This is what makes it a bagh and not a phulkari.

  6. 06

    Turn it over

    The first time the front is seen whole. Then a week of corrections: a motif out by one thread, a shade that reads wrong at the edge.

  7. 07

    Wash and fold

    Cold water, no soap, dried in shade. Folded with a muslin between the layers so the silk does not catch. Then it is given away, which is the point.

Nineteen years, in order

  • 2007Learned the darn stitch from my grandmother, on a scrap of khaddar, at eleven years old. She unpicked everything I made for a month.
  • 2011First full bagh, for my cousin's wedding. Nine months, and I still see the mistake in the border.
  • 2015Started taking commissions outside the family, after a shop in Patiala asked for six dupattas.
  • 2018Moved the work into the front room with the north window. Better light, colder in winter.
  • 2021The year of small cloths: cushions and chopes, because no weddings happened.
  • 2023Began teaching two women from the neighbourhood, mornings only, while the light is good.
  • 2026Thirty-four baghs, and a waiting list of four, which is the most I will take.

Care of a bagh

Do not hang it in the sun. Do not dry-clean it; the solvent takes the lustre out of the silk and never gives it back. Air it in shade twice a year, fold it along a different line each time, and keep it away from the monsoon. A bagh looked after will outlive the person it was made for, which is the intention.
