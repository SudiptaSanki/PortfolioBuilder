Lesson 01 · The arithmetic that comes first

The position-size calculator

Every course I teach starts here, before a single pattern. A position size is not a feeling; it is one division. Decide the most a trade is allowed to cost you, decide the price at which the idea is wrong, and divide. Try it with the teaching figures below — the working is printed line by line, because a number you cannot re-derive is a number you do not understand.

Direction of the idea

The working

  1. Risk budget: £10,000 × 1% = £100
  2. Distance to invalidation: |250 − 245| = 5.00 points
  3. Raw size: £100 ÷ 5.00 = 20 units
  4. Rounded down (never up): 20 units
Position size 20 units
Planned maximum loss £100

The planned loss is the budget before costs. Spreads, commissions and slippage add to it, and if price gaps straight through the invalidation level the real loss can be larger. Sizing honestly means leaving room for all three.

Lesson 02 · What a candle actually records

Candle anatomy explorer

A candlestick is a summary of four numbers — open, high, low, close — for one stated period. Nothing more. Pick a shape and read what it records, what it does not, and why the same shape gets different names in different places. Every panel below ends with the same sentence, because it is the sentence the whole practice stands on.

Long body — the one-sided period

Open
100.0
High
112.4
Low
99.2
Close
111.6

What it records: the period travelled 13.2 points and kept almost all of it — the body is 11.6 of those points. One side was in control from near the open to near the close, and the other side's best effort (the short lower wick) was 0.8 points.

What it does not record: why. News, a large order, thin liquidity and genuine conviction all draw the same candle. The shape is silent about cause, and about tomorrow.

A shape is a description of what already happened. It is not a prediction of what happens next.

Doji — the balanced period

Open
100.0
High
104.8
Low
95.1
Close
100.3

What it records: a 9.7-point round trip that ended 0.3 points from where it began. Both sides pushed, neither kept anything. After a long run in one direction, that balance is information about the run — not about a reversal.

What it does not record: a turning point. Dojis appear constantly in directionless markets where nothing turns at all. Textbooks that show only the famous examples are teaching you the exceptions.

A shape is a description of what already happened. It is not a prediction of what happens next.

Hammer — the rejected low

Open
100.0
High
101.2
Low
88.4
Close
100.6

What it records: price fell 11.6 points below the open and the period still closed above it. Sellers drove it down; buyers took it all back before the close. The lower wick is at least twice the body — that ratio is the definition, not a vibe.

Context changes the name: this shape is called a hammer only because it arrived after a fall. The identical shape after a rise is catalogued as a hanging man. The name follows the context; the arithmetic — open, high, low, close — does not change. Learn the arithmetic and the names become trivia.

A shape is a description of what already happened. It is not a prediction of what happens next.

Spinning top — the contested period

Open
100.0
High
105.9
Low
94.6
Close
102.1

What it records: an 11.3-point range with a body of only 2.1. Unlike the doji, the close did move — slightly, in the buyers' favour — but most of the travel was given back on both sides. Effort, roughly equal; result, small.

What it does not record: indecision as a tradable event. A contested period inside a quiet market is just a quiet market. The shape earns attention only when it interrupts a strong run — and even then it says "the run paused", not "the run ended".

A shape is a description of what already happened. It is not a prediction of what happens next.
