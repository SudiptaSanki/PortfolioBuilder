Unit 02 · Case studies

Three commissions, written up properly.

Each one told the way it actually happened: what was asked, the decisions that mattered — patch design, tempo against edit points, loop seams — and what shipped. All three cues can be heard, in browser-synthesis form, in the patch player.

Case 01 · Game score — loop system

Vector Coast — a score built as loops, not tracks

An indie racing game needed music that could stretch and compress with a race: three laps, weather changes, a photo-finish mode. Full-length tracks with the ends trimmed would have clicked, drifted and fought the game. The score was designed as a loop system from the first bar.

The brief

Six race environments, menus and a results screen, for a two-person indie studio shipping their first title. The audio programmer had one hard requirement: every loop had to be sample-accurate and documented, because the adaptive system crossfades layers by race position and there was no time to reverse-engineer composer's files at integration.

Decisions

One tempo grid for the whole game — 118 BPM. At 118, a 16-bar loop runs 32.5 seconds, close to the game's average lap time, so loops resolve near lap boundaries instead of mid-corner. Every cue in the game shares the grid, which lets the menu theme hand over to a race start without a tempo seam.

Intensity as stems, not versions. Each loop was delivered in three layers — drums and bass only (cruising), plus arpeggio (contested), plus pad and lead (final lap) — all printed from the same hardware passes so the layers phase-align exactly. The Vector Coast patch in the player is the contested layer's arrangement.

Loop seams written, not hoped for. Each loop carries a two-bar tail of reverb and delay printed into a separate file, with the loop start and end written down to the sample in the delivery document. The game engine plays the tail across the seam; there is no audible join.

Outcome

Six loop sets, fourteen stingers and the menu/results themes delivered two weeks before the integration deadline. The studio's audio director's verdict is quoted on the home page. The game shipped on its announced date, and the score was later released as a standalone album cut from the same masters.

Case 02 · Trailer cue — re-score to picture

Night Freeway — ninety seconds, four hit points

A motoring drama's trailer had been cut to a library track the producers couldn't clear. The re-score had to land on the same four edit points — logo, first frame, title reveal, button — inside a week, without the audience feeling the join.

The brief

Replace the uncleared track under a locked 90-second edit. The picture was not changing; the music had to fit it exactly, keep the rising shape of the original cut, and clear for worldwide trailer use in perpetuity.

Decisions

Tempo chosen from the timings, not the genre. The four hit points fell at 0:00, 0:12, 0:47 and 1:24. At 105 BPM a bar lasts 2.286 seconds, which puts bar lines within a frame or two of every cut — close enough that the riser into the title could be written as a straight 8-bar climb instead of a tempo map full of exceptions.

0:00Logo sting — sub drop + single arp note, silence after
0:12First frame — bass pattern enters, hats withheld
0:47Title reveal — 8-bar riser, full kit, pad opens to B♭
1:24Button — written 5-second ending, hard stop on the last frame

Low end built for cinema. The patch's square sub-oscillator sits an octave under the saw bass specifically so the cue still reads in a cinema mix at trailer loudness; the browser player uses the same patch, at parlour volume. The ending was composed as a separate 5-second button file, so the editor could re-time the last cut without touching the cue.

Outcome

Demo sketch in 40 hours, full cue delivered on day six, one revision round used (the riser lengthened by two bars after a recut). Delivered as the 90-second master plus 30- and 15-second cutdowns, the button, and full stems — cleared worldwide, in perpetuity, as briefed.

Case 03 · Title theme

Chrome Orchard — a theme that had to survive 45 seconds

A streaming drama's main title is 45 seconds long and plays before every episode. The theme had to state a melody a viewer could hum by episode three, leave room for the title cards' sound design, and shrink to a 5-second sting without losing its identity.

The brief

Main title (45 s), sting (5 s), and a recap bed (30 s) for a slow-burn drama about an orchard-keeping family with a buried secret. The producers' reference was "synth music that sounds like weather, not like a nightclub" — slower and warmer than this studio's trailer work.

Decisions

Tempo and filter set the temperature. 92 BPM, and the patch's cutoff sits at 1.2 kHz — the darkest in this portfolio — because the melody had to feel muffled, like something heard through a wall. In the player you can open the cutoff fader on Chrome Orchard and hear exactly the version the producers rejected as "too bright, too eager".

Two chords, one melody. The harmony never leaves F major and C major; all the movement is in a square-wave lead melody written to be recognised from its first three notes, so the 5-second sting — just those notes over the F chord — still reads as the same programme.

Space left on purpose. The title sequence carries narration-level sound design (rain, a gate, distant traffic). The arrangement keeps the 300–900 Hz band deliberately thin so the two never fight; the recap bed is the pad alone, reprinted without the lead.

Outcome

Delivered to broadcast loudness spec with the cue sheet completed. The theme ran across both seasons of the drama, and the sting was adopted by the broadcaster as the programme's ident for trailers — an extra use that was licensed separately, as the contract allowed.
