Entry № 047 — Opening a record

A side is eighteen minutes and a promise.

I am Saskia Vermeulen, a producer in Rotterdam, and I make records for one format: vinyl. Labels and self-releasing artists hire me to produce the album, sequence it as two sides, sit with the cutting engineer while the lacquer is cut, approve — or reject — the test pressing, and size the pressing run by arithmetic instead of optimism.

Vinyl's limits are not a mood. They are physics, they are printed on this page, and the whole job is planning inside them honestly: the minutes a side can hold, the level those minutes cost, and the number of records a real audience can pay back.

The physics, printed first

What the groove allows

A groove is a single spiral with a fixed amount of room. The longer the music, the closer the turns sit and the quieter the cut — and the last track on a side, riding the smallest circles, distorts first. So a side is not a playlist with a duration; it is a budget with three bands. These are this room's working bands for a 12″ LP at 33⅓ rpm, and the side sheet below is judged against them.

Plate — Side bands, Vermeulen Cutting Room The side sheet reads its verdicts from this plate — nothing pre-computed
BandSide lengthWhat it costs
Band I — Full level up to 18:00 Nothing. This is the side the format promises.
Band II — The trade 18:01 – 22:00 Level, and bass width near the centre. Sometimes worth it. Always stated.
Band III — Will not cut over 22:00 More than the groove has. The plan changes, not the physics.

Other rooms draw these lines a minute either way; the physics underneath them is not negotiable. Minutes are bought with level — the only question is whether the price is printed.

      Pressing economics — the second tool

      Size the run by arithmetic

      A pressing is a fixed bill plus a per-record bill, and it is paid back one sale at a time. The figures below are this room's current plant figures, printed in full; the calculator works from them and nothing else. It answers the only question that matters before a run is booked: how many records must sell before the pressing stops costing money?

      The rate card — production fees

      What the production costs

      Fees are separate from plant costs: the plant is paid at cost, with its paperwork attached, and this room is paid from this card. No percentage of sales, no points — a producer who profits from a bigger run will recommend a bigger run.

      WorkBasisFee
      Album productionDay rate; a typical album books 8–12 days£450 / day
      Sequencing & side planningPer album, flat; two running-order revisions included£350
      Mastering-for-vinyl supervisionPer album; the level decisions are this room's£250
      Test-pressing evaluationPer round; written verdict each time£120
      Pressing liaison & run sizingPer release; plant booking, break-even sheet, delivery check£200

      What moves the price: a second disc doubles lacquers and metalwork, because physics bills per side. A running order changed after the lacquer is cut is not an edit — it is a new lacquer at £380. And the plant's queue is the plant's: ten to fourteen weeks is the current typical wait, and rushing it is not for sale at any fee.

      Margin — deposits Half the production fee books the cutting session; the balance falls due when the test pressing is approved — or rejected. A rejection is work too, and the log charges for it the same.

      The room

      Vermeulen Cutting Room is the practice of Saskia Vermeulen, a fictional producer created for this template. The method, the physics and the arithmetic are stated at real practice level; the clients, records and figures are illustrative.

      Sound

      This site makes no sound, deliberately. The honest demonstration of what vinyl does to music is a record on a turntable; a synthesised imitation of crackle would be the fake warmth this room exists to refuse. The stub's hover sounds were removed, not rebuilt.
