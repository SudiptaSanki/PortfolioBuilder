Entry № 001 — The room itself

The producer keeps the log.

Saskia Vermeulen came to production through the quality room of a Dutch pressing plant, where her job was the least glamorous one in the building: listening to test pressings, all the way through, on a system that told the truth, and writing down what was wrong with them.

Five years of that teaches you the format from the failure side. You learn that most bad records were not badly played or badly mixed — they were badly planned: sequenced like playlists, levelled like files, and sized like wishes. The faults were almost never in the music. They were in the arithmetic nobody had done.

She opened this room in Rotterdam in 2014 to do the arithmetic first. The practice is deliberately narrow — records for vinyl, nothing else — and deliberately documented: every record leaves with its side sheet, its band decisions and its pressing figures filed in the log, where the client can read them as easily as the room can.

Margin — the one opinion Most records don't fail in the studio. They fail in the spreadsheet nobody made. This room makes the spreadsheet first, and lets it argue.

The working method

How a record runs

Four stages, in this order, every time. The order is the method: nothing is cut before it has been sequenced, and nothing is pressed before it has been approved as a physical object.

1

The masters arrive — and get heard properly

Mixes are checked on the room's own system before anything else happens: level, sibilance, bass width, the things a groove will punish. The report is written and sent before a lacquer is booked. (This stage exists in this form because of Case File 02, where skipping it cost £1,015.)

2

The side sheet — sequencing as engineering

The running order is built as two sides, not one list: totals against the printed bands, the loud and the wide seated where the groove is generous, the quiet and the narrow where it is tight. Every trade is priced in level and agreed before the cut, never discovered after it.

3

The cut — attended, with the level calls owned here

This room sits with the cutting engineer for the lacquer. The engineer owns the lathe; this room owns the decisions the lathe is asked to carry out — overall level, bass management, where the side's budget is spent.

4

The test pressing — a verdict, in writing

Five copies arrive; they are listened to on two systems, all the way through, inner tracks last and listened to hardest. The verdict is written either way: approved, or rejected with the fault named and the re-cut priced. There is no third verdict, and there is no pressing without one of the first two.

The honesty plate

What “warm” is actually made of

People say vinyl sounds warmer, as if warmth were a substance pressed into the plastic. It is not. The format's sound is a set of trade-offs — physical, measurable, plannable — and what listeners call warmth is what those trade-offs do to music that was sequenced with them in mind. Music that ignores them does not sound warm. It sounds quiet, narrow and worn at the edges, because that is what it is.

  • Minutes are bought with levelA longer side is a quieter, narrower side. This is not a flaw in any particular record; it is the shape of the spiral, and it is why the bands on the side sheet exist.
  • The centre is the hardest seatThe last track on a side rides the smallest circles at the lowest speed past the stylus. Distortion arrives there first. Sequencing is deciding what can afford to sit there — a decision, not an accident.
  • Bass narrows as the side goes onWide stereo bass near the centre can throw a stylus out of the groove, so it is summed toward mono as the side progresses. The low end you feel at the start of a side is not available at the end of it.
  • Surface noise is the floorEvery record has a noise floor a file does not. Quiet music is mixed and sequenced with that floor in mind — or it is heard through it.
  • Two blunt entriesStreaming numbers are not a pressing quantity — they count listeners, not buyers, and a run is paid for by buyers. And a playlist pressed flat is a playlist with a £2,945 minimum spend: the format rewards albums built as two sides and punishes everything else impartially.

Terms of engagement

Who owns what, stated before anyone pays

The metalworkFathers, mothers and stampers are the client's property, stored at the plant in the client's name. If you change rooms, your stampers go with you; a repress never re-buys them.
The lacquersA lacquer is consumed making the father — it is a single-use object by nature, not by policy. A second cut is a second lacquer, whoever orders it and whatever went wrong.
The feesHalf the production fee books the cutting session; the balance falls due on the test-pressing verdict, approved or rejected. Plant invoices are passed through at cost with the plant's own paperwork attached.
The queuePressing lead times are the plant's — currently ten to fourteen weeks, as of this log entry. Dates are planned backwards from the queue, never promised forwards from hope.
The verdictTest-pressing verdicts are given in writing and belong to the client, including the rejections. Especially the rejections.
Margin — availability Two album slots are open this quarter. Sequencing-only and evaluation work is booked between albums. If the log is full, the room says so on the register rather than taking a deposit against a queue it cannot honour.

The room

Vermeulen Cutting Room is the practice of Saskia Vermeulen, a fictional producer created for this template. The method, the physics and the arithmetic are stated at real practice level; the clients, records and figures are illustrative.

Sound

This site makes no sound, deliberately. The honest demonstration of what vinyl does to music is a record on a turntable; a synthesised imitation of crackle would be the fake warmth this room exists to refuse. The stub's hover sounds were removed, not rebuilt.
