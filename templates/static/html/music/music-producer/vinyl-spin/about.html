The studio

A cutting room the size of a good record shop.

Thirty-four square metres on the second floor of a former printworks, north of the Maas. One lathe, one pair of speakers, and a shelf of lacquers waiting for the courier.

How I got here

I started as a tape op in a studio in Utrecht that no longer exists, running a Studer for anyone who could pay for a reel. The owner had a Technics SP-10 and a habit of playing test pressings at the wrong speed to make a point about arrangement. I learned more about records from those twenty minutes than from three years of plugin tutorials.

In 2016 I bought a VMS-70 out of a closing plant in Bielefeld, drove it back in a rented van with the cutting head in a padded case on the passenger seat, and spent four months learning to keep the temperature in the room stable enough to cut. The lathe is older than I am. It still holds pitch to a micron when the room is right.

Most of my work now is independent releases between 300 and 2,000 copies: house, jazz, Dutch-language pop, the occasional metal record that needs a 45 cut because the guitars are doing something the format does not like. I master for digital as well, because most of these records also stream, and it would be strange to hand over one master and let someone else guess at the other.

What the room sounds like

The chain is deliberately short: a Prism converter, a pair of valve EQ units I use less than people assume, and the lathe. There is no plugin on the cutting path that I cannot bypass, and I will tell you when I bypass it. Monitoring is a pair of ATC SCM25s on stands and a pair of NS-10s for the things the ATCs forgive.

The room is treated, not dead. There is a window that opens onto the courtyard and a fridge that hums at 100 Hz, which is why the rumble filter is a conversation I have at least once a week.

How a session runs

You send the master by WeTransfer or a private link, with a note about what the record is for and which two tracks matter most. I listen and reply with a level report before you book, so no one is surprised by a recut. If you come to the room, we cut, play it back on the same turntable, and adjust. If you would rather not travel, we do it over a call with the lacquer played down the line, which works better than it sounds.

I keep the lacquer off the platter until the last possible moment and I do not play it more than three times. Lacquer is soft. Every play costs a little top end, and the test pressing should be the first thing that hears the cut as it will actually be heard.

What I will not do

  • Master from a streamed or lossy file. If that is all there is, I will say so and cut it, but the level report will carry a note.
  • Cut a side past the point where the inner grooves stop tracking. The client can ask for a louder record; they cannot ask me to make one that skips.
  • Promise a pressing date that belongs to the plant. I give you the cut date and the day the lacquer leaves the building; after that it is in their queue, not mine.
